# 22.8 · A Cell Stops Midway Through Its Growth

*Midway* must first be dealt with, for its sense here is not its sense afterward.

---

A later being, doing a thing, has a beginning, a course, and a completion.

A man builds a house. Foundation, walls, roof, doors and windows. If he stops when the walls are half up, that house is **unfinished**: the walls have an uneven top, the iron stands out of the stone, there is no roof over the rooms.

**It is a half-thing.**

And a half-thing has one recognisable property: **it shows what it was to have become.**

Look at the house stopped halfway, and you know a roof belonged there. That gap has a shape, and that shape is the negative of the shape the house was going to have.

---

Here there are no half-things.

The reason stands at 20.1: here there is no *thinking in progress*, only what has been thought and what has not.

The making of a layer is instantaneous: the tide comes, the interstice opens, it takes; the tide goes, the interstice closes, it locks. Locked, the layer is made and the face stands.

**Between the opening and the locking there is no state of half a layer.**

And if a cell should stop while opened and not yet locked — is that physically possible?

We must work this step, for it is the whole of this section.

---

A cell stops on this condition: its interior reaches equilibrium (22.1), and therefore its interstices no longer open when the tide comes.

And *no longer open* happens in the instant of the tide's arrival.

Therefore:

**A cell's stopping falls necessarily between two layers.**

It finished the nth layer. The tide went out, that layer locked, that face stood.

Then the next tide came.

**And its interstices did not open.**

And so the (n+1)th layer does not exist — not unfinished, but **not there.**

---

The distinction must be written down and left standing.

**Unfinished:** the layer began and was not completed. It exists, and it is deficient.

**Not there:** the layer never began. It is not there. There is no gap, because there is no such position.

The stoppings of every cell here are the second.

**Therefore no cell here stopped halfway through anything.**

Every one of them stopped in the instant when a layer had just locked and the next had not begun.

**Every one of them stopped at a complete place.**

---

Then what does *midway* mean.

Here is the one sense in which it holds here:

**Midway means its growth could have continued.**

When a cell stops, its structure is sound, its heat sufficient, its tide still arriving (22.1).

It has every condition for continuing.

**It simply has nothing left to grow.**

So *midway* is not about its shape — its shape is complete.

***Midway* is about its situation: it stopped at a place where it could have gone on.**

---

One consequence must be set down in what remains of this section, for it is among the things most easily got backwards between here and afterward.

A later being, dying, almost always leaves unfinished things behind.

A man dies, and on his desk is a letter half written. On his shelf is a book stopped at page seventy. In his plans is a place he meant to go next year. His house is being repaired. His child is small.

**His whole life, in the instant of his death, is half-things.**

And the gaps of those half-things have shapes — and those shapes are the negatives of the shapes his future was going to have.

**That is one reason the death of a later being is hard to bear: it leaves behind a great many gaps that have shapes.**

A letter half written is much harder than a letter never begun.

Because the letter half written shows what the whole letter was to have been.

---

And a cell here, stopping, leaves no gap at all.

Every one of its layers is complete. Its last face is as complete as its first.

It has nothing half written. It has nothing stopped at page seventy. It has no place it meant to go next year.

**Its whole life, in the instant of its stopping, is a complete shape.**

---

One sentence must be written with great care here, for it is very easily read as consolation, and it is not consolation.

**That completeness is not because it finished.**

It did not finish. It could have gone on. Its growth-mechanism remained available after it stopped — if that list had suddenly gained one more item (and it will not), a cell not yet stopped would have grown the next layer.

Its completeness is because **its construction makes a half-thing impossible for it.**

A layer either locks or does not exist.

**Its completeness is a completeness of form, not a completion of content.**

---

And the difference between those two must be shown with a later example, for only so does it show at all.

Let there be a letter.

**One:** a man writes the whole of what he had to say, then lays down the pen. That is completion of content.

**Two:** a man writes three words, and his pen stops at the end of the last stroke of the third, and that stroke closes cleanly, and the ink does not trail out. That is completeness of form.

**Every cell here is the second.**

Their last stroke closes clean.

**And what they had to say is not said out.**

---

At the end of this section, what *had to say* means here must be given, for here there is no speech (20.6).

The life of a cell is a sequence of orientations (19.4).

That sequence, when it stops, has a last term that is complete.

**And that sequence has no end.**

We wrote at 19.4: here there is no death by age; a cell's growth approaches stopping without stopping; it may grow for a length not to be imagined, its layers thinning to a thinness not to be imagined, and it is growing still.

Which is to say:

**A cell's life, uninterrupted, is unbounded in length.**

**Therefore any life of finite length has been interrupted.**

And the lives of every cell here are finite.

**Therefore every cell here has been interrupted.**

And the thing that interrupted them is nothing external (22.6).

**They finished their own arithmetic.**

---

And so the last sentence of this section is this:

**Every cell here stopped midway through its life, and its shape in the instant of stopping was complete.**

**And between those two there is no contradiction.**

For *midway* says that it could have gone on.

And *complete* says that it left no half-thing.

**They say two different things.**

And here, those two hold at once.

Afterward, those two almost never hold at once.
