# 22.4 · From the Centre Outward

It began at the centre.

---

First, what *centre* means here, this place having no map, no coordinates, no bearings.

*Centre* here means: **where the neighbours are most.**

That is a measurable quantity. A lattice's number of neighbours is fixed by how many lattices lie within readable distance (20.4).

And that number is not uniform: in some places lattices are dense, in others sparse.

Where dense, a lattice may have very many neighbours. Where sparse, perhaps two or three.

***Centre* is where it is dense.**

---

And why the freezing began there.

We wrote at 21.8: the lattice that arrived at the last item was the one whose position happened to suit — it had everything to hand because its neighbours had, over preceding tides, happened to compute what it needed.

And the more neighbours a lattice has, the higher its probability of having everything.

**So the first to have everything is almost necessarily in a dense place.**

Not necessity. Probability. And on this place's scale, a probability indistinguishable from necessity.

**So the freezing of this place began where this place had the most neighbours.**

---

Set this beside a sentence from 19.5, and the shape appears.

19.5 says:

> The lattices at the centre have the most neighbours, are the most read, and their shapes are held in the most places.
> **So the freezing of this place begins where the most is remembered.**

And what this section derives is the other half of the same thing:

**The freezing of this place begins where it is easiest to know.**

Many neighbours: many paths to go around by, and the most complete self-knowledge (19.5).

Many neighbours: the fastest receipt of what others have computed, and the earliest completeness (21.8).

**One cause.**

A lattice with many neighbours knows itself earlier and more completely than others — and precisely for that reason it stops earlier than others.

---

Now the shape.

Two similes are permitted in this place (19.7 used one: metal like honey).

This is the second, and the last.

---

**Like a lake going over on a still night.**

Every part of the simile must be stated, being exact, and exact in a way that is uncomfortable.

**One: a lake freezes from one place.**

Not all at once. At some place — usually the coldest, the stillest, or where a speck of dust has fallen — a small patch forms first.

**Two: that patch grows outward.**

Its edge advances. The speed is fixed by water temperature, by how fast the heat of crystallisation escapes, and by the motion of the water.

On a very still night the advance is even, slow, continuous.

**Three: it makes no sound.**

A lake freezing over makes almost none. Standing on the bank, you do not hear it.

(Slightly unlike this place: every stopping here makes a very small sound. And each of those sounds is heard by one neighbour only, and that neighbour stops afterwards (22.3). **So in aggregate, it is silent.**)

**Four: from above, you cannot see it moving.**

The ice edge advances slowly. Watch it for a minute and see nothing. Leave for an hour and come back, and the lake is different.

**Five: the frozen water and the unfrozen water, in one lake, are the same temperature.**

This is the crucial one.

A lake freezing over is at very nearly one temperature throughout — all near the freezing point. The difference between the frozen part and the part still water is very small.

**It is not that that water is colder. It is that it has already gone over.**

And the part not yet frozen is not warmer. It is **not yet reached.**

---

Stop here, because the fifth point is the entire reason for the simile.

Later beings, looking at a civilisation's decline, generally ask: what is different about the parts that have not declined.

Are they stronger? Cleverer? More careful? Luckier?

**And in a lake going over, the unfrozen water and the ice differ in nothing.**

They are one lake. They are at one temperature. Their molecules are identical.

**The only difference is that the edge has not reached there yet.**

---

The same here.

The lattices still growing and the lattices already stopped differ in nothing.

Not better. Not slower to compute. Not holding something else in reserve.

**They have merely not yet read.**

And all of them can compute the hour (21.8).

---

To end this section, where the simile fails — because a simile whose failure is not stated gets taken for an explanation.

**A lake freezes because of cold outside it.**

A lake goes over because the air is colder than the water and heat leaves the water. That is an external cause.

If the night warmed suddenly, the freezing would stop, and could reverse.

**And the freezing here has no external cause.**

The Quenched Sun did not change. Its linewidth-to-wavelength ratio is still under 10^-9; its fluctuation still below the floor (11.6).

The heat beneath did not change. It is still decaying, at very nearly the rate of four billion years ago, and its form has been computed (21.5, four).

The tide did not change. It still lifts and settles, on the period it had at the beginning.

The slurry did not change.

**Not one external condition of this place underwent a measurable change across the whole of the freezing.**

Those lattices were not frozen.

**They stopped themselves.**

And they stopped because they knew enough.

---

So the simile inverts at that point:

**A lake goes over because it got colder outside.**

**This place stopped because the work inside was finished.**

One is a deterioration of external conditions.

**One is the completion of internal work.**

And the shapes of the two — beginning at one place, spreading outward, one after another, quietly, all the way to the edge — are identical.

**From outside, you cannot tell which.**

And this place has no outside.
