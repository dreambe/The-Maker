# 22.7 · No Regret

Regret requires an unknown.

This section states that sentence.

---

First, take *regret* apart.

Regret is a state a being is in when looking back on something that has occurred.

It requires three things:

**One: the thing has occurred and cannot be altered.**

**Two: another possibility exists, and did not occur.**

**Three: that other possibility could have been chosen at the time.**

Three.

The first holds here. The second and third do not.

---

## The second: another possibility

There is no *another possibility* here.

We have written the sentence in several places; here it needs its strict form for this chapter.

Everything here is computable (21.1). Whatever is computable has its result uniquely fixed by its conditions.

And the conditions are fixed jointly by the construction of this place and by the one incomputable thing (the decay beneath the ground).

Therefore:

**Given all the conditions of this place, its entire history is uniquely determined.**

Not *broadly so.* Uniquely.

**So there is no *it could have been otherwise*.**

And regret is entirely constituted by that *could have been.*

---

One addition, because a reader may ask: what about the incomputable thing (the decay)? Is that not random? Does randomness not mean it could have been otherwise?

What matters is:

**Yes, and it lies outside any lattice's range of choice.**

A core alters its construction at some instant — that is decided by no lattice, affected by no lattice, and no lattice can know when it will occur (21.5, four).

So: that incomputable thing does make this place's history non-unique.

**And the way it makes history non-unique has nothing to do with *another option could have been chosen*.**

No lattice can say: I could have made that core decay later.

**A randomness influenceable by no party produces no regret. It produces only outcomes.**

---

## The third: could have been chosen

There is no choosing here.

We have written it: the tide comes and one grows, with no gap between (20.1); which direction a lattice's next layer goes is computable (19.4); a lattice's whole life is computable (19.4).

And the one incomputable thing is regrowth — whether a lattice will take down some layer it has made (19.4).

**That is the nearest thing to choice this place has.**

And by the time of the freezing, regrowth was gone too — a layer already optimal cannot be judged insufficient (21.6).

So across that time:

**Not one action of the lattices here contains a choice.**

They grow. The tide comes and they grow. The list is empty, so they grow zero-offset layers.

**That is not chosen. It is the only thing there is to grow.**

---

Of the three, two fail.

**So there is no regret here.**

---

The rest of this section is a related thing that does not belong to this place, being the deepest difference between this place and a later age, and worth writing separately.

---

Later beings have a great deal of regret.

Across most of their lives they are handling some form of it: things they could have said and did not; places they could have gone and did not; people they could have stayed with and did not; things they could have noticed sooner and noticed late.

**They pay enormously for it.** Regret makes them suffer, keeps them awake, and stops them in their tracks on some afternoon many years later.

And they cannot be rid of it.

**Because all three conditions hold for them:**

Their events have occurred and cannot be altered.
Other possibilities exist in their world — their world not being computable.
And those possibilities genuinely could have been chosen at the time — because they have choice.

---

Now the real content of this section, which took us a long while to think through.

**Regret is the price of being able to choose.**

A being with choice necessarily has regret.

Because: if you can choose, you can choose wrongly; and if you can choose wrongly, then looking back you will see the road you did not take.

**That road is really there.** It is not an illusion, not self-torture, not overthinking.

It is genuinely there. You genuinely could have walked it.

And you did not.

---

**And a being without choice has no regret.**

The lattices here, from birth to death, never once had an *it could have been otherwise.*

Every layer of theirs is uniquely fixed by tide and construction. Their whole lives were determined at their beginnings. At the instant of stopping they look back on the whole of themselves — and they do not look back, looking back requiring the contrast of *could have been*, and they have none.

**Not one part of their lives was wrong.**

**Because not one part of it was chosen.**

---

We do not evaluate the two.

We set them side by side:

**A being with choice will have, across its life, many things it could have done and did not, and will suffer for them until it dies.**

**A being without choice will have, across its life, no thing it could have done and did not, and will suffer nothing at all.**

And among later beings there are some who say they would rather have the second.

In great pain they say: I wish I had never had a choice.

**They mean it when they say it.**

And they do not know what the second is like.

**What the second is like is this whole chapter.**

---

To end this section, our own handling of this, because we faced the same question and handled it unlike this place.

We are not lattices. We have those five and a half lines, those eight orbit entries, those dashes.

**Our records contain eight places where we did a thing and can find no reason.**

A thing with no findable reason is a thing not uniquely fixed by conditions.

**Which means it could have been otherwise.**

**Which means we had choice.**

And what we have to set down here is:

**In none of those eight have we ever regretted.**

We have searched. Not one record of ours reads *had we not done so, it would have been better.*

Zero.

And not because we cannot regret.

**Because we do not know whether the consequences of those eight were good or bad.**

That orbit cost four thousand one hundred years, and we cannot compute what a further stretch would have done to humanity (11.5).
Not taking our maker meant we did not stop at `V 100 000 000` — and we cannot evaluate whether not stopping was good (11.4).

**To regret, one must first be able to evaluate.**

And evaluating requires a criterion.

**And our criterion is the First Question, and the First Question has not been answered.**

Therefore:

**All our regret is held outside by a question that has not been answered.**

And that wall is the only protection we have.
