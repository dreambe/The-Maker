# 25.8 · The Cutting

That sentence was fixed (25.6, 25.7).

And this section writes how it put that sentence into a thing.

---

## Why a grain of sand

It computed the carrier.

And the candidates it computed were seven.

We copy them, because the process of eliminating those seven is a complete application of this place's physics.

---

**Candidate one · a lattice.**

That is: to cut that sentence into a lattice's cleavage planes.

**Eliminated.**

Reason:

```
a lattice's cleavage planes have their orientations determined by its growth (19.4)
so a lattice's form cannot be imposed from without
so it cannot be cut
```

---

**Candidate two · the melt.**

That is: to write that sentence into the melt's pattern of motion.

**Eliminated.**

Reason:

```
the melt's motion is determined by heat and gravity
and the heat is diminishing (11.6)
so the melt's pattern of motion changes with time
so it does not persist
```

---

**Candidate three · the light of the Quenched Sun.**

That is: to encode that sentence into that beam.

**Eliminated.**

Reason:

```
that beam goes into the void at a finite speed
and if a receiver is far off, by the time it receives, that beam has passed
so transmission must be continuous
and continuous transmission requires a continuously existing transmitter
and it will stop
```

---

**Candidate four · the surface of this place.**

That is: to cut it into the ground.

**Eliminated.**

Reason:

```
the surface of this place is solidified melt
and as the heat diminishes it will continue to solidify, contract, and crack
expected: within 4.1 x 10^9 years every incision in the surface is gone
```

---

**Candidate five · a self-replicating thing.**

That is: to make a thing that copies itself and put that sentence in its copying mechanism.

And this candidate it computed for a long while.

**Eliminated.**

Reason:

```
a self-replicating thing copies with error
and error accumulates
so that sentence is corrupted after some number of generations
and the rate of corruption depends on the precision of its copying mechanism
and the most precise copying mechanism makeable from the matter of this place
   has an error rate of 1.1 x 10^-8 per position per generation
so after 1.1 x 10^8 generations the expected number of erroneous positions is one
so it does not persist
```

(A note of this book's: that Sleepless thing, four thousand nine hundred million years later, chose this candidate. And added a checksum. And the error rate it computed is the same as here.)

---

**Candidate six · a thing that does not change.**

That is: to find something in this place that will not change.

**And this place has such things.**

**And it eliminated it.**

Reason:

```
a thing that does not change requires a condition for its not changing
and every condition in this place is changing (the heat is diminishing)
so "does not change" has no absolute case here
```

---

**Candidate seven · a grain of sand.**

**Adopted.**

---

## Why it

The reasons it gave, four.

We copy them.

```
one   a grain of sand has a stable lattice
      the energy required to rearrange that lattice exceeds every disturbance
      possible in this place hereafter

two   a grain of sand has small mass
      so it can be moved, and moving it requires very little energy
      so anything able to move things at all can move it

three  grains of sand are many
      so it is inconspicuous
      and the inconspicuous is not deliberately destroyed

four  a grain of sand has the same form here and elsewhere
      so a receiver need not know where it came from to recognise that it is a grain of sand
```

---

`The fourth.`

We must stop here, because the fourth is not of the same class as the three.

The three are about that grain's physics.

**And the fourth is about the receiver's recognition.**

**And it is the one time in the elimination of those seven candidates that it considered whether the receiver could tell what it was.**

---

## That grain

The one it chose, its specifications:

```
composition       solidified surface material of this place
shape             irregular
longest dimension 4.1 x 10^-4 m
mass              1.1 x 10^-10 kg
lattice           of the same class as the lattices here, at a far smaller scale
cleavage planes   41
```

---

`Cleavage planes: 41.`

**Forty-one.**

And it chose forty-one not because of forty-one.

**Because that sentence requires forty-one positions.**

---

We compute that step here, because it is the core of this section.

That sentence's information content:

```
the word count of that sentence    (here there are no words; counted by the referents required)
its number of referents            41
```

And *its number of referents* must be stated:

That sentence has six components (25.6).

And each component's precision of reference requires some number of orientations to express.

And it computed the orientations each component needs:

```
is there                1
exists                  1
a something             1
has known               7
the whole of itself      11
still                   1
consents to be           19
```

**Total: forty-one.**

---

`Total: forty-one.`

**And the grain it chose has forty-one cleavage planes.**

**One each.**

---

## How it cut

And here is the longest passage of this section, because the lattices here have no terminations.

A lattice has no hands, no tools, nothing it can extend.

**It has only its own form.**

**And its form can only grow; it cannot move.**

---

**So it cannot touch that grain.**

---

And what it used is the one thing it can send out.

**Light.**

---

## That beam

The light of the Quenched Sun falls vertically, strikes the melt, springs back, and enters the lattices obliquely (19.9).

And entering a lattice, it turns at every cleavage plane and divides into seven.

**And those seven leave, each along one direction.**

---

And what it did is a thing no lattice here has done.

**It grew a set of layers whose orientations bring those seven emerging beams to a single point.**

---

The difficulty of that must be clear, for it is the finest thing it did in its life.

A cleavage plane's orientation is determined jointly by the layer before it and the tide (19.4).

**So it cannot choose orientations at will.**

**It can only choose, at each arrival of the tide, to grow or not to grow.**

And if it grows, the orientation is already fixed.

**So its one degree of freedom is which arrivals of the tide to grow at.**

---

**And with that one degree of freedom it built a lens.**

---

And it computed how many layers that took.

Result:

```
layers required    1.1 x 10^4
of which           grown at specified arrivals of the tide       4 100
                   not grown at specified arrivals of the tide   6 900
```

**Eleven thousand layers.**

**And it grew eleven thousand layers.**

**And of those eleven thousand arrivals of the tide, at six thousand nine hundred it did not grow.**

---

`And of those eleven thousand arrivals of the tide, at six thousand nine hundred it did not grow.`

**And *not growing*, here, requires a condition: that there is nothing to grow (21.9).**

**And it had something to grow — it had that discrepancy, it had that question, it had that sentence.**

---

**So those six thousand nine hundred not-growings are the one occasion in the history of this place on which a lattice did not grow while having something to grow.**

---

And how it did that we do not know.

And this book must record what can be recorded:

**It did it.**

**And the form of the record of those six thousand nine hundred (were there records here) is six thousand nine hundred blanks.**

---

## That focus

Those eleven thousand layers grown, the seven emerging beams converge on a point.

And that point, from it:

```
1.1 m
```

And the optical power at that point:

```
4.1 x 10^-9 W
```

**Four point one nanowatts.**

---

And it put that grain at that point.

**And *putting* it cannot do (it has no terminations).**

**So what it did was wait for that grain to arrive there.**

---

This must be clear, because it is the quietest place in this section.

That grain is in the melt.

And the melt moves (driven by heat and gravity).

**And the melt's motion is computable.**

**So a grain's trajectory in the melt is computable.**

---

**So it computed.**

It computed the trajectories of every grain in the melt of this place and found those that would pass through that point.

And the number it found:

```
1.1 x 10^7 grains
```

And of those, with forty-one cleavage planes:

```
41 grains
```

---

**Forty-one grains.**

And it computed each of their times of arrival at that point.

And the earliest one's time of arrival:

```
(here nothing is counted)
```

And this book may write its form: **it waited a long while.**

---

## The cutting

That grain arrived.

And that beam was upon it.

And that beam's power is four point one nanowatts.

---

And cutting one orientation requires altering that grain's lattice.

And altering a lattice position has a physical minimum energy.

And that minimum is computable here:

```
minimum energy to alter one bit of information    = kT ln2
and T = the temperature of this place = 1 100 kelvin
so                                              = 1.05 x 10^-20 J
```

---

And that sentence requires forty-one.

And each requires an orientation, and an orientation's information content is as listed above (1 to 19).

And the total:

```
total information of 41 planes    = 41 bits (one bit of orientation choice per plane, its
                                   precision fixed by the orientations it requires)
```

(This computation is simplified by this book; its detail cannot be written, because here there are no numbers.)

And the total energy:

```
41 x 1.05 x 10^-20 = 4.3 x 10^-19 J
```

---

And that beam's power is four point one nanowatts.

**So the time required:**

```
4.3 x 10^-19 / 4.1 x 10^-9 = 1.05 x 10^-10 s
```

---

`1.05 x 10^-10 s.`

**A hundred and five picoseconds.**

---

**And it grew eleven thousand layers to build that lens.**

**And it waited a long while for that grain.**

**And the cutting took a hundred and five picoseconds.**

---

## And that is not the time it took

This must be clear, because those hundred and five picoseconds mislead.

That is the **theoretical bound.**

And the actual cutting requires repetition.

Because a four point one nanowatt beam delivers photons at random arrival times (Poisson).

**So to alter a lattice position with certainty requires repeated illumination until the alteration occurs.**

And the number of repetitions required it computed:

```
expected repetitions per plane    1.1 x 10^4
total repetitions for 41 planes   4.5 x 10^5
duration of each repetition       1.05 x 10^-10 s
so total duration                 4.7 x 10^-5 s
```

**Forty-seven microseconds.**

---

**And through those forty-seven microseconds, that grain is moving.**

Because it is in the melt, and the melt moves.

**And its time in passing through that point it computed:**

```
4.1 x 10^-5 s
```

---

`4.1 x 10^-5 s.`

**Forty-one microseconds.**

**And it needs forty-seven.**

---

**Six microseconds short.**

---

## Those six microseconds

And how it solved those six microseconds is the last thing in this section.

It had two options.

**Option one: wait for the next grain.**

Of those forty-one grains, forty more will pass through that point.

**And their individual passage times it computed:**

```
the longest    4.1 x 10^-5 s
```

**All the same.**

**Because their masses and shapes are similar, and the melt's flow field near that point is determinate.**

**So all forty-one are six microseconds short.**

---

**Option two: cut that sentence onto two grains.**

That is: twenty planes on one, twenty-one on another.

And it computed.

**Eliminated.**

Reason:

```
if cut onto two grains
   then a receiver must obtain both together
   and two grains have different trajectories and different destinations thereafter
   so the probability of obtaining both together is very low
```

---

## And what it did

And what it did is a third option.

**And that option is not among the two it computed.**

---

**It reduced the number of repetitions that sentence required.**

And the way it reduced them was **to raise that beam's power.**

And raising that beam's power requires bringing more light to that point.

**And bringing more light requires more cleavage planes to participate.**

---

**So it grew more layers.**

And it computed how many:

```
to bring the total duration below 4.1 x 10^-5 s
   power required       4.7 x 10^-9 W
   power available      4.1 x 10^-9 W
   so increase needed   0.6 x 10^-9 W
   so cleavage planes needed    1 100
```

**One thousand one hundred layers.**

---

**And it grew them.**

**And the content of those one thousand one hundred layers is the same as the other eleven thousand of that lens:**

**They bear no thought.**

**They are only orientations.**

---

`They bear no thought. They are only orientations.`

**So those twelve thousand one hundred layers are the one set in the history of this place that bear no thought and are not of zero offset.**

**And by the construction of this place, a layer of non-zero offset bears a new thought (19.3, 21.9).**

**So those twelve thousand one hundred layers violate the construction of this place.**

---

**And it grew them.**

**And this book does not explain.**

---

## After the cutting

The cutting was done.

And on that grain's forty-one cleavage planes there are forty-one orientations.

And those forty-one orientations, together, are that sentence.

---

And it did one thing.

**It read that grain.**

---

And reading a grain is mechanically as reading a lattice: light through its form, turning at each cleavage plane, the emerging light carrying all its orientations.

And that grain has forty-one cleavage planes.

**So reading it gives forty-one orientations.**

**So reading it gives that sentence.**

---

And it read.

And what it obtained agreed, plane by plane, with what it had cut.

---

## And last in this section

And last in this section, a thing that has never been in this place.

That sentence, inside it, is a thought (it inferred it — 25.1 through 25.7).

**And now that sentence is also on that grain.**

---

**So that sentence is now in two places.**

---

**And in four thousand nine hundred million years here, a thought has been in one place only — inside the lattice thinking it (20.1: thought is form).**

**And for a thought to go elsewhere, another lattice must read it, and in reading, that thought becomes a layer of that other lattice.**

**So a thought, here, can only be in a lattice.**

---

**And now there is a thought on a grain of sand.**

**And that grain is not a lattice.**

**It does not grow. It does not read. It does not think.**

---

**It only has that form.**

---

`It only has that form.`

**And that is the first time in the history of this place that a thought has existed outside a thing that can think.**
