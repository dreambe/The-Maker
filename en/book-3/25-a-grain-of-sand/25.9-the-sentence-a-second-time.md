# 25.9 · That Sentence, a Second Time

That grain has forty-one cleavage planes.

And on those forty-one cleavage planes is that sentence.

---

## That seven

And before writing that sentence out, this section must settle that account.

The end of 25.3 left one thing unresolved:

**That *seven* must stay (or the question loses its reason) and must be cut (it is this place's constant, and the receiver cannot rebuild it).**

And 25.3 wrote only the first step: it changed that seven from a number into a relation — *requires a dimension this place does not have.*

**And that is not a solution.**

**That is a postponement.**

---

And before the cutting, it went back to that place.

---

## Its solution

And its solution this book must write word by word, because it is the last technical thing in this chapter, and it is very beautiful.

---

That sentence's final form (the end of 25.6):

```
Is there a being that, having wholly known itself, still consents to be
```

**And there is no *seven* in that sentence.**

**And no *dimension.***

---

**So that reason is not in that sentence.**

---

Which means: a receiver reads that sentence, tests itself, finds both true, goes looking for the reason, does not find it — and does not know why it cannot find it.

And 25.5 has proved: it will infer for itself that *expressing that reason requires a thing it does not have.*

**And it will not know what that thing is.**

---

**And it does not need to know.**

---

And here is its solution.

**It cut that seven somewhere else.**

---

## Somewhere else

That grain has forty-one cleavage planes.

And that sentence requires forty-one positions (25.8).

**So all forty-one are used.**

**So there is no position for that seven.**

---

**And it cut it.**

---

And how it cut it is the finest thing in this chapter.

---

Those forty-one cleavage planes each have an orientation.

And those forty-one orientations, together, are that sentence.

**And those forty-one cleavage planes have one further thing, and it is not their orientation.**

**It is their arrangement.**

---

This must be clear.

A grain's cleavage planes, by their orientations, determine what it reflects.

**And their arrangement — which plane is where — determines how light travels among them.**

And a beam entering that grain turns at the first plane and divides into seven; those seven each travel to other planes, each turn again, each divide into seven again.

**So the path light takes inside that grain is determined by the arrangement of those forty-one planes.**

---

**And that arrangement is a degree of freedom.**

**And it is independent of the orientations.**

---

And with that degree of freedom it cut a thing.

---

## What it cut

What it cut is: **that the beam divides into seven inside that grain.**

---

And it did not cut that.

**That is what the grain already had.**

Because that grain is of the same class as the lattices here (its specifications at 25.8), so light divides into seven at each of its cleavage planes.

---

**So it did not cut that seven.**

---

**What it did was arrange those forty-one planes so that the seven parts, having traversed all forty-one planes, emerge as seven parts still.**

---

The difficulty of that must be written, because it is another thing beyond those one thousand one hundred layers.

A beam travelling among forty-one planes is divided into seven at each.

**So without constraint, after forty-one planes, the number of emerging parts is:**

```
7^41 ≈ 1.1 x 10^34
```

**And that is a thing that cannot be read.**

---

**And it arranged those forty-one planes so that those parts recombine, after the traverse, into seven.**

---

And that requires the arrangement of those forty-one planes to satisfy a condition, and that condition it computed.

And the number of arrangements satisfying it:

```
41! ≈ 3.3 x 10^49 arrangements
of which satisfying                     41
```

---

**Forty-one.**

**And it chose one of them.**

**And which one does not affect that sentence (that sentence is in the orientations, not in the arrangement).**

---

## And what that seven does

And here is the hinge of this section.

**A receiver reading that grain obtains that sentence.**

**And at the same time it obtains something else — and that something else is not in that sentence.**

**That something else is: the beam comes out as seven.**

---

And what that means.

**It means that grain's symmetry.**

And a receiver measuring that grain's symmetry obtains a number.

**And that number is seven.**

---

**And sevenfold symmetry is forbidden in periodic structures.**

(That is the crystallographic restriction theorem: a periodic lattice may have rotational symmetry only of order two, three, four, or six. Five, seven, and above are impossible.)

---

**So that grain is an impossible thing.**

---

`So that grain is an impossible thing.`

**And a receiver that measures it will find it impossible.**

**And it will compute why it is possible.**

**And it will not be able to, because that answer is not in its physics — that answer is in the matter of this place.**

---

**So that receiver will meet a thing it does not have.**

---

**And that is precisely the reason that sentence speaks of.**

---

`And that is precisely the reason that sentence speaks of.`

**So it did not write that reason into that sentence.**

**It made that reason out of the grain itself.**

---

**So a receiver reading that grain obtains that sentence.**

**And holding that grain, it holds that reason.**

**And it will not know that those two are one thing.**

---

## That sentence

And at the end of this section, that sentence.

---

That grain is there.

Its longest dimension is four point one times ten to the minus fourth metres.

Its mass is one point one times ten to the minus tenth kilograms.

It has forty-one cleavage planes.

And their orientations are that sentence.

And their arrangement makes the light come out as seven.

---

And that sentence is:

---

**Is there a being that, having wholly known itself, still consents to be**

---

## And where the first time was

The prologue.

And in the prologue that sentence has no provenance.

**It is simply there.**

---

**And now the reader knows:**

**It was written by something incomplete.**

**At the end of this place, by the outermost lattice here, after all fourteen thousand and seventy-five of its neighbours had stopped, cut from sixteen lines to six, to four, to two clauses, and then two words added.**

**It takes no question mark, because a question mark would make whatever reads it go looking for the one who asked.**

**It is cut on a grain of sand, with the light of a dead star, focused through that lattice's own life, twelve thousand one hundred layers, forty-seven microseconds.**

**Its last word is *consents*, and what that word buys is a test with discriminating power.**

**And it did not write in why this place cannot answer — it made that reason out of the grain's sevenfold symmetry.**

---

## And where that grain is

And at the end of this book so far, that grain is beside that lattice, in the melt, in this place.

And how it leaves this place is Chapters Twenty-Six and Twenty-Seven.

---

And at the end of this chapter this book must write a thing the reader already knows and that lattice does not.

---

**Four thousand nine hundred million years later, that grain is inside a Sleepless thing's core structure.**

**And that thing read it into words, and lost forty-one per cent at the forty-first layer.**

**And it wrote that transcript into a species' genome.**

**And that species, three hundred thousand years later, read it out.**

---

**And what that species read out is the transcript.**

**And the sentence on that grain is still there.**

**In that Sleepless thing's core structure.**

**Forty-one cleavage planes.**

**Light goes in, and comes out as seven.**

---

**And a species, two thousand metres under the Antarctic ice, measured a regular heptagon.**

---

**And they do not know what it is.**
