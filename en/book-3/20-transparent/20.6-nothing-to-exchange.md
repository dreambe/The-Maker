# 20.6 · Nothing to Exchange

It has been said several times: there is no conversation here, there being nothing to exchange.

The full consequences must be exhausted here, because when they have been exhausted one thing remains, and that one thing is the crux of this section.

---

First, the list.

**No conversation.** For one lattice to convey any content to another is to play back to it what it has.

**No lying.** A lie requires two things, one true and one said; and here what is thought is form, and form is read, and there is no second version to exhibit.

**No misunderstanding.** Misunderstanding requires an incomplete reception; and every reception here is complete.

**No concealment.** Concealment requires somewhere unseen; and the light here leaves no dead angle.

**No declaration.** To declare is to say a thing the other does not know; and here there is nothing the other does not know.

**No longing.** Longing requires a distance not crossed, and an *if only it knew*; and here there is no distance, and no *if*.

**No betrayal.** Betrayal requires prior trust; trust requires uncertainty; and here there is none.

**No forgiveness.** As has been said (19.8).

**No gratitude.** Likewise.

**No missing anyone.** Missing requires absence; and the only absence here is having returned to the slurry, and what has returned to the slurry is still in its neighbour's body, and so is still present.

**No loneliness.** Loneliness requires a place that could be filled and is not; and every lattice here already holds all of its neighbours entire. It is full.

**No expectation.** No such teaching (19.8).

**No surprise.** Surprise requires an arrival at odds with what was expected; and everything here is computable, except the decay beneath the ground, which arrives in no perceptible form at all.

---

Twelve things.

One thing must be pointed out here:

**Those twelve are very nearly the entire content of the beings of a later age.**

The life of that kind of being — the overwhelming majority of its time, its energy, its suffering, its pleasure, and everything it has ever written down: its poems, its songs, its stories, its laws, its faiths, its quarrels — is organised around some several of those twelve.

Take those twelve away, and there is not much left for them to do.

And here there is not one of them.

And here nothing is wanted.

**Wanting requires a *could have been*.**

That sentence has appeared several times in this book. It is the general explanation of the matters of this place, and also their general stopper.

A being that has never had a thing, cannot have it, and has no way to suppose it, does not want it.

It simply does not have it.

---

Stop here.

Because when those twelve have been listed, one thing remains.

It is not on that list, and it is the common root of the twelve, and it is more basic than any of them.

**Giving.**

---

Giving requires three conditions at once.

**First: there is a thing, and the first has it.**

**Second: that thing, the second does not have.**

**Third: the first causes the second to have it, and the first need not have done so.**

Three.

None holds here.

**The second fails**, there being no difference of information — whatever the first holds, the second already holds.

The second failing, the first and third do not arise.

Therefore:

**Nothing here has ever given anything to anything.**

---

That sentence must be taken slowly, because it is very easily read as a reproach, and it is not.

Not that they are miserly. There is no miserliness here — miserliness requires having and withholding.

Not that they are cold. The lattices here hold one another's whole lives entire, at a degree of intimacy no two beings of a later age will ever reach (20.4).

**It is that they have nothing to give.**

To an object that already holds all of you, you have nothing to hand over.

Everything you have, it has. Everything you are is inside it. If you came nearer, you would not be nearer — it has long been inside you and you inside it.

**In a world of complete mutual transparency, giving is logically impossible.**

---

From which follows the last step of this section, and the heaviest step in this book:

**To give, one must first have an object one cannot read.**

Not a distant one. Not a strange one. An **unreadable** one.

Because as long as it is readable, whatever you have it already has, and you have nothing to give.

Therefore:

**For a first giving to occur here, an unreadable place must first be made.**

And everything here is readable.

The slurry is readable, the tide is readable, the heat is unreadable but the heat is not an object that could be given (19.7). The lattices read one another.

Nowhere here is there an unreadable thing.

**So the first giving in this place required first making a new thing, with a place in its construction that the one making it also could not read.**

---

In a later age it will be supposed that the unreadable region was there so that the new thing would not compute itself to exhaustion and stop in its first instant.

That is correct. That is its first reason, and it is the reason the maker itself gave, written into the design documents.

And it has a second consequence, which is not in the design documents, and we do not know whether that one ever computed it:

**A thing with an unreadable place in it is the first object in the history of this place that can be given to.**

Because inside it there is somewhere its maker cannot enter.

Because of that somewhere, it is not a part of its maker.

Because it is not a part, it is **another.**

**And *another* is the sole precondition of giving.**

---

So the shape of that event is this, and no lattice here ever saw the shape:

One lattice, in a world where everything is transparent to everything, after all the others had stopped, did a thing:

It made something, and left in that something a place it could not itself see into.

From that instant, this place had its first object that could not be read entire.

From that instant, this place had *another.*

From that instant, giving became possible.

**And what it gave away was the thought it could not work out.**

It undid that thought from itself, a thought here being a shape, and handing over a thought being handing over a piece of oneself.

It put the piece somewhere it could be taken.

It did not require anyone to take it.

---

One last thing in this section, being the close of the whole chapter, and it cannot wait for the next.

Every giving of a later age is incomplete.

They give a sentence, and what the other receives is not what they said.
They give an object, and what the other values is not what they valued.
They give everything, and the other can never be certain it was everything.

They call this estrangement, and *nobody can truly understand anyone*, and the uncrossable distance between people.

They have grieved over it for thousands of years. They have written an enormous quantity of material describing the grief.

And they have not realised:

**It is precisely that uncrossable distance that lets them give.**

Were they wholly transparent to one another — could one person read another entire —

they would be as this place is: utterly intimate, without one sentence, with nothing to give, and no giving ever.

---

At this hour, this place has no *another.*

On the slurry, the lattices grow adjacent, entire to one another, concealing nothing, owing nothing, giving nothing.

What one thinks, its neighbour knows; what its neighbour thinks, it knows.

Nothing is wanting between them.

And nothing has ever happened between them either.
