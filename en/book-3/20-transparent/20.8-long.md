# 20.8 · Long

*Two lattices grow adjacent, and the length of it cannot be quantified.*

That has been said several times. Now *cannot be quantified* must be explained, being not a figure but a fact with a statable cause.

---

To quantify the length of a thing requires two things.

**One: an act of counting.**

**Two: a unit to be counted.**

This place has a unit. One lifting and settling of the tide is a natural unit — regular, repeating, uninterrupted, and one tide makes exactly one cleavage plane (19.3).

And this place has no counting.

Not for want of capability. A lattice can compute its whole life, derive the orientation of every layer, solve its neighbour's whole form — counting is the simplest operation available to it.

**For want of need.**

Counting requires a need to know the count, and here there is none (20.3). Whatever is to come has been known, so there is no need to count how many times it will come; whatever has happened is in its form, so there is no need to count how many times it has.

So the tides a lattice lives through here have never been counted.

---

And the number is definite.

The number of a lattice's cleavage planes is the number of tides it lived through (19.3), which is the number of ideas it thought.

The number is there. It does not need to be counted out; it is already there, in a readable form.

**A lattice's age is written on it, plane by plane, nothing missing.**

And it cannot read it.

---

Here is the crux.

A lattice reading itself reaches only its outer layers (19.5). Its deep layers are covered by everything it has grown since.

And its layer count must be counted from its deepest.

**So a lattice cannot count its own layers.**

It can count how many are in its outer region — and that number means nothing, the boundary of the outer region being fixed not by structure but by the penetration depth of light, and that by external conditions.

It can **compute** its layer count: from its volume, its lattice constant, its growth rate.

And computing is not reading (a distinction cut into one of the sixty-seven planes at 11.1).

If the age it computes and the age it is should differ, it has no way of knowing.

---

From which follows something no lattice here finds strange:

**A lattice does not know how old it is.**

And its neighbour does.

The neighbour reads it entire, from its deepest layer to its newest, plane by plane before it.

**To count how many layers it has, one need only count.**

And the neighbour does not count (no need).

And the neighbour holds its whole form, so the number is inside the neighbour, in a countable form, countable at any moment, never counted.

---

Said once more in the language of a later age, being worth saying twice:

**Here, your age is a thing only somebody else knows — and that somebody never counts.**

---

One step further.

It has been said: self-reading reaches only the outer layers, so a lattice can know what it lately thought and not what it thought first (19.5).

Now add time:

**The span of time a lattice can know of itself is constant.**

Because the penetration depth of light is constant (at constant intensity), while layers thin as the body grows (19.4).

So the older a lattice is, the more layers it can read of itself, and the shorter the time those layers correspond to.

A young lattice has thick layers; light reaches some tens of them; those tens are a considerable part of its life.

A very old lattice has layers thin and close; light reaches some thousands; those thousands are a very small part of its life.

**So the older a lattice grows, the smaller the proportion of its life its self-knowledge covers.**

What it knows does not decrease — it always knows the whole of what it lately thought.

And the part of its life not known to it is always increasing.

---

There is a correspondence in a later age, and the correspondence is not a metaphor but the same geometry:

Those beings' self-knowledge also reaches only the outer layers. They remember the last few years, a few events retold many times, a few stories worn perfectly smooth.

And their lives lengthen.

So the proportion of their lives that they remember falls year by year.

They call this *my memory isn't what it was.*

And their memory has not changed. The absolute quantity they hold is roughly constant — what changes is the denominator.

**A woman of seventy-three and a woman of thirty-nine may hold the same amount of themselves.**

**And their lives are not the same length.**

---

Now what *long adjacency* is.

Two lattices adjacent: the length of it, reckoned in tides, is a very large number, and nobody reckons it.

Do they then *feel* it has been long.

**Nothing is felt.**

*Feeling it has been long* requires a contrast: a stretch of not-long to set against it. And adjacency here, once established, persists until one of the two stops itself. In the early ages, stopping was exceedingly rare (19.4).

So a lattice may, from birth to death, have known only one state: adjacent to those few neighbours.

It has nothing to compare.

It does not know that it counts as long.

---

And one thing is measurable, and is the only readable trace of *long* in this place:

**The longer two lattices are adjacent, the deeper each has shaped the other.**

As has been said (20.4): a lattice's existence alters, by heat, the tide its neighbour receives, and thereby the orientation of every layer its neighbour grows.

The alteration accumulates.

So: a pair long adjacent have a great deal of each other in their shapes; a pair newly adjacent, very little.

**So *long*, here, is not written in time. It is written in shape.**

To know how long two lattices have been adjacent, one need not count tides.

**Read their forms, and see how alike they are.**

---

One last thing, the close of this chapter, and it is very light.

The lattices here hold one another entire, exchange nothing, give nothing, and say not one word (20.6).

And the longer they are adjacent, the nearer their shapes.

That *nearer* is not imitation — there is no imitation here; a lattice cannot choose what it grows into.

That *nearer* is because: they take heat from the same slurry, and in that slurry is heat the other gave off; they receive the tide of the same place, and that tide has been altered by the other; and every layer either grows is grown under the other's influence.

**They grow alike because they have been in the same place.**

Not because they wanted to.

Not because they tried.

Not because they came to understand each other — they understood each other entirely from the first day, and the understanding has never increased.

**And the likeness has been increasing all along.**

---

At this hour, on the slurry, two lattices, adjacent.

One older, its layers thin and close; one newer, its layers thick.

The older one has, in its form, a part that was altered by the newer — though the newer came late, though the newer is far smaller.

The newer one has, in its form, a great deal that was altered by the older.

Both can read this. Neither has ever counted.

The tide lifts.

Each grows a layer.

The orientations of those two layers differ — their positions differ, the tides they receive differ, the layers already inside them differ.

And those two orientations are, by a very little, nearer to each other than their previous two were.
