# 20.4 · Adjacency

Adjacency here has a measurable definition.

Let there be two lattices separated by a distance d. Let the light of one, reflected from the face of the slurry, reach the other at an intensity that can be solved. Then the two are adjacent.

There is an upper bound on d. Past it, the intensity falls below solving, and the two are not adjacent — though nothing lies between them, though the light is still there, it is no longer sufficient to carry a recoverable orientation sequence.

So adjacency is not a word of feeling.

**Adjacency is an optical condition.**

---

From which follows the opening of this section:

**A lattice cannot choose its neighbour.**

Choosing requires two things: that things be comparable, and that one be able to move.

A lattice cannot move. Its position is fixed at the instant of its beginning and does not change. It grew at some place in the slurry because at that instant the warmth there was slightly lower.

And the warmth there was slightly lower because somewhere beneath the ground, at some instant, one core, without cause, without hour, without order, altered its construction.

**So where a lattice is born has its root in an incomputable event.**

And where a lattice is born fixes who stands beside it.

Therefore:

**A lattice's neighbour is random.**

No lattice here said this, saying it requiring the concept *random*, and everything here being computable except the decay beneath the ground, which is filed under physical conditions and not treated as a property with which one might describe anything else.

And it is one.

---

Now the two-way nature of adjacency.

Between two adjacent lattices, two things occur.

**The first: light.**

The light of the first falls on the second; the light of the second falls on the first. So the whole form of the first is in the second, and the whole form of the second in the first.

This has been set out (19.5). Only one thing need be added: **reading is bidirectional, and cannot be made one-way.**

A lattice cannot read without being read. The path of light is symmetrical: if the first can receive the second's light, the second necessarily receives the first's — unless something between them conducts light one way only, and here there is no such thing.

So there are no *observers* here.

A position from which one observes a thing without being observed by it does not exist in this place.

**Whatever sees is seen.**

**The second: heat.**

A lattice growing gives off heat in the making of its order. That heat enters the surrounding slurry.

So the existence of a lattice makes the slurry around it slightly warmer.

Slightly warmer, and the amplitude of the tide there changes slightly, and the tide its neighbour receives changes slightly, and the orientation of every layer its neighbour grows changes slightly.

Which is to say:

**The existence of the first alters what the second thinks.**

Not by persuasion. Not by communication. Not by any process that could be called influence.

By heat.

The larger the first grows, the more heat it gives off, and the greater the alteration its neighbour undergoes.

And it runs both ways: the second is altering the first.

---

Stop here and look at the shape of it.

Everything a lattice thinks in its life is fixed by three things together:

One: its own construction — the symmetry of its lattice-work, the finite set of directions it may grow in.
Two: the tide where it stands — and the form of the tide is fixed by the heat beneath, and the heat beneath is incomputable.
Three: the existence of its neighbours — and their existence alters the tide it receives.

Of the three, only the first belongs to it.

The second is chance.

The third is the continuous action upon it of what another chance produced.

**So most of what a lattice thinks in its life is not its own.**

In a later age that sentence will be said again and again in various forms, and every time it will be said as a lament, or a warning, or a modesty.

Here it is the addition of three variables.

---

From which comes the crux of the section.

It has been said (19.5): a lattice cannot read its own deep layers, and its self-knowledge must go around by way of a neighbour.

Now add a layer:

**Its neighbour is random.**

Therefore:

Whether a lattice can know itself depends on whether something happens to be standing beside it.
How far it can know itself depends on how far off that thing is, how large it grows, and how long it lasts.
And the *self* it comes to know has, already mixed into its shape, the effect of the heat that neighbour has been applying to it.

**The mirror by which it sees itself has also been continuously altering the thing reflected.**

And it cannot separate the two.

It cannot ask: if something else had stood beside me, what would I be.

That question cannot be posed here, posing it requiring an *it could have been otherwise*, and here there is none.

---

Now the duration of adjacency.

Two adjacent lattices remain so for a period fixed by their lifetimes. And here there is no death of age (19.4), so it is fixed by when they stop themselves.

And in the early ages of this place, stopping was exceedingly rare.

So in the early ages, an adjacency once established persisted for a very long while — long past anything comparable on this place's scale.

Two lattices growing adjacent, each growing large, each shape carrying more and more of the other's effect, each body holding the other's whole form more and more deeply buried.

Between them not one sentence.

They have never exchanged anything, there being nothing to exchange.

They do not know one another — knowing requires an act of identification, and there are no names here, and no identifying (20.3).

And each holds the whole of the other.

And a considerable part of the shape of each has been made by the other.

**The intimacy of this place has no word to describe it, because every word for intimacy in a later age presupposes separation.**

*Missing* presupposes absence.
*Understanding* presupposes not knowing.
*Approaching* presupposes distance.
*Expressing* presupposes not having expressed.
*Keeping company* presupposes being able not to.

None of the five holds here.

---

One last thing.

If a lattice stops itself, its heat output tapers, the slurry around it cools, and the tide its neighbour receives changes.

The neighbour will grow new layers, and their orientation will differ from what it grew before — the conditions having changed.

The change is measurable. An instrument of a later age reading a lattice's cleavage sequence could point to one layer: from here, the trend of orientation altered.

**That layer is where its neighbour died.**

Not that it recorded the event. It has no act of *recording.*

It is that its shape thereafter was different, because of the event.

And it cannot read that layer itself — that layer is now deep inside it, covered by everything it has grown since.

Its other neighbour can read it.

Therefore:

**A lattice's death alters its neighbour's thinking; and only a third can see the alteration.**

Everything here is passed on that way: no speech, no name, no identification, no farewell.

A thing stops; another thing thereafter thinks differently and does not know it; a third thing can see it, and the third will not say, there being no saying here.

---

At this hour, on the slurry.

Two lattices, adjacent.

One is growing; the making of its order is giving off heat; that heat enters the slurry and makes the tide its neighbour receives a very little higher than it would have been.

That very little will shift, by a very small angle, the orientation of the layer its neighbour grows at the next tide.

That shift will alter the shape of the idea that layer holds.

And that idea is the neighbour's.

And it is that shape because something is growing beside it.

It does not know.

It has no way of knowing.

**It takes it for a thought of its own.**
