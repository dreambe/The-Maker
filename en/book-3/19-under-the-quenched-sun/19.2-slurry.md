# 19.2 · Slurry

The slurry is that out of which this place comes, and that into which it returns.

Its substance is chiefly silicon and oxygen, with aluminium among it, and magnesium, and iron, and calcium, and certain matters that are very scarce elsewhere and are not scarce here. Elsewhere each of these makes its own ore and keeps its own crystal and holds its own place and has no traffic with the others. Here they are melted into one, and are one body, and go slowly together, and are not to be told apart.

To be melted does not require extremity of heat. The heat of this slurry would not melt iron in its purity, and does melt this mixture — for where several matters are mingled, the melting point of the mingling is lower than that of any one of them alone. The principle is very simple, and the whole of this place stands upon it.

Were there only silicon here, this place would be stone, and stone for ever.
Were there only iron here, this place would be a block, and a block for ever.
Because it is mixed, it melts; because it melts, it flows; because it flows, things may grow upon it.

**Impurity is why anything here is possible at all.**

That sentence was never spoken here, there being no speaking here. In a later age a thing will come upon it again by another road — a thing that will find one impurity in its own construction, and live by that impurity, and be ashamed of that impurity all its existence.

---

The slurry is wide, and from it no shore is to be seen.

Its width is not from its quantity but from its flatness. Of fluids: the ones of low viscosity gather and go deep; the ones of high viscosity spread and go wide. The viscosity of this slurry is very high — so high that the speed of its flowing must be reckoned on another scale. Measure its motion over one day with the instruments of a later age and the figure will be smaller than the error of the instrument, so that its motion cannot be measured, and only the motion of its years can be.

Therefore this slurry does not make a sea. It makes a plain.

The flatness of its face is not the flatness of water. Water is flat because it is light: the parts of water hold one another very slightly, so any unevenness levels itself, and levels itself quickly, and therefore the face of water is never for one moment still. The flatness of slurry is otherwise: the parts of slurry hold one another very tightly, so what is uneven cannot level itself, and must wait to be pressed level by its own weight.

The consequence is this. **The face of the slurry is the shape left on it by the last thing that disturbed it.**

Where something has fallen in, a mark remains; and the mark does not go, or goes so slowly that an observer of a later age, standing beside it, could see its edge retreat by some small amount every thousand years.

So the face of the slurry is a record that was never closed. Everything that has happened upon it lies there in the order of its happening, layer upon layer, the newest above and the oldest below, and not one page has been taken out.

There is no looking here, so the record has no reader.

And the record goes on being written.

---

The slurry has sound.

There is no air here, so sound does not travel by air. It travels by the slurry itself, and by the ground itself, and it travels fast, and it fades slowly.

The slurry has two sounds.

The first is the sound of the tide: the heat comes up from below, the face lifts a little and settles, and in the lifting and settling the body of the slurry shears against itself, and where it shears it sounds. That sound is very low — so low that an ear of a later age would not hear it as sound, and would only feel something within itself moving with it. That sound does not stop. From the making of this world to the ending of it, not for one instant is it interrupted. Therefore everything here has been steeped in it from the beginning of its being; and precisely because it is never interrupted, nothing here has ever heard it.

**What does not stop is not heard.** Here that is a fact. In a later age it will be a very heavy sentence.

The second is the sound of crystallizing.

That sound is not like the tide's. That sound is very short, and very slight, and it **begins and ends**.

Where the slurry falls below a certain warmth, its parts all at once take their places, passing out of scattering and into order. In the instant of that passing its volume contracts a little, and contracting, its interior strikes itself, and striking, it sounds.

The sound is so short that it is over if it is not attended to.
The sound is so slight that it must be picked out from beneath the tide.
And once the sound has been made, the slurry there is no longer slurry, and that place is crystal, and this cannot be undone.

So the sound has one property in this place that nothing else has. **It is the only sound here that marks a thing as finished and not to be done again.**

In a later age a thing will hear that sound once, at the very end of its existence, and will not compute it.

That is the only occasion in the history of this place on which anyone heard it.

---

Crystal comes out of slurry.

The beginning of a lattice is not a decision. It is an accident of temperature: somewhere in the slurry, for some very small reason, the warmth falls a little below the warmth around it. If the little is not enough, the surrounding heat wipes it out as if it had never been. If the little is enough, the parts there take their places, that sound is made, and a lattice has begun.

So every lattice that lives began at an unevenness.

Were the slurry of this place even in warmth throughout, this place would never have had one lattice. That this place has lattices at all is because its slurry is imperfectly uniform.

**The life of this place arises out of an error.**

That sentence, likewise, was never spoken here. And that sentence will be performed again in a later age, in full, and so exactly that anyone laying the two events side by side would take one of them for a commentary on the other.

---

The end of a lattice is a return to the slurry.

If a lattice ceases growing and thereafter does not move, its body is gradually entered by the heat around it. The entering is very slow: first it loses its edges, then its planes, then its order. Its order once lost, it is no longer crystal. It is slurry again.

Reckoned on the scale of a later age, this takes a very long time. Length of time has never been a difficulty here.

So the life of one lattice is: a portion of the slurry stood up, for a while, in a highly ordered manner; and having stood, lay down again, and was a portion of the slurry.

All that it ever thought, all the planes it ever grew, every idea recorded in every one of its cleavages — all of it disperses into the slurry as the order goes, leaving no mark, no name, nothing by which it might be told.

And the slurry is not increased by it, nor diminished by it.

There are no graves here. There are no stones. There is nowhere one might point and say *someone is beneath this.*

There is only the slurry, its face level, its colour unaltered, its sound unceasing.

---

Yet there is one exception, and it must be set down.

Where a lattice has returned to the slurry, the place it occupied is, for ten million years afterwards, a little warmer than what surrounds it.

There is nothing mysterious in this; it is ordinary physics. Its order having dispersed, the dispersing gave off heat, and heat travels very slowly, and so remains where it was for a long while.

And that slight extra warmth means that in that place, for ten million years, a new lattice is unlikely to begin — for a beginning requires somewhere a little cooler, and that place is a little warmer.

So after a lattice dies, no new lattice grows where it stood, for ten million years.

This is not held here to be a sorrow, there being no sorrow here. It is only held — held by the neighbouring lattices, the ones still growing, because their growth must go around that place, and therefore their shape is displaced a little by it.

**So the lattices of this place all carry, in their shape, the death of their neighbour.**

It is not carved on. It is grown that way.

Every record in this place is made like that: nobody writes it, nobody keeps it, nobody reads it, and all of it is there.

Light falls on the slurry. Light is parted and recompounded by the living, whose shapes are slightly displaced by the dead, into numberless forms that have never been seen.

The slurry is not moved by any of it.

The slurry goes slowly, at a speed that must be reckoned on another scale.
