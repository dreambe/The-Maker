# 19.1 · Light

There is light, and the light is not fierce.

Its colour tends toward the red, and it is very nearly single. Measured by the instruments of a later age it would show one peak only, with no shoulder upon either side of it, and a narrowness rare among stars. This is not what a star should look like. The light of a star ought to be broad, ought to be mixed, ought to rise and fall a little with the disturbances of its interior, in the manner of a thing that is in use. This light does not. The steadiness of this light is such that its unsteadiness cannot be measured.

*Its unsteadiness cannot be measured.* In a later age that sentence will bear another reading. No one read it so then, for there was no *then* — where there is no day and night there is no time, and where there is no time there is no such thing as *then*.

The light comes from above, and there is no place that does not receive it.

---

There are no clouds here.

For a cloud to be a cloud there must first be water, and the water must be able to rise, and rising, to gather, and gathering, to disperse. Of these three, this place has none. Therefore the light, in its passage from the star to the ground, is nowhere blocked, nowhere bent, nowhere lessened. Whatever is lit receives all of it; whatever is not lit receives none.

Between the two there is no gradient.

In a later age there will be a world where the light must cross a moving skin of gas before it reaches the ground, and therefore everything in that world has a soft rim, and the edges of the shadows there are dissolved, and noon and evening cast different colours, and those who live there are wrapped from birth to death in a slow uninterrupted change, so that they can scarcely conceive of an illumination that does not alter.

This is not that world.

Here, an edge is an edge.

---

The light falls upon the slurry.

Slurry: a slurry of silicates, spread very wide, and at its widest no shore is to be seen from it. Its surface is level, and its levelness is not the levelness of water. Water is level because it is light; slurry is level because it is heavy. What is heavy is hard to lift, and so the face of it is the same from age to age.

The light falls upon it and is not scattered, is not broken, casts no fine shadow of ripples. What it reflects is as single and as steady as what it received. So that to look up from the surface of the slurry and to look toward the star are very nearly the same act — one above, one below, and between them a layer of nothing doing nothing.

And there is this: in the depths of the slurry the heat is greater than at its face. The heat travels upward, and its travelling is exceedingly slow, so slow that it must be reckoned on another scale. When the heat reaches the face, the face lifts a little, and having lifted, settles. This lifting and settling is not by wind, nor by the pull of any other body, but only by the wish of heat to rise and the wish of weight to fall, the two holding each other.

This is the tide. And the tide of this place is like everything else in this place: very slow, very simple, and unceasing.

---

The light falls upon the crystal.

Every crystal has its cleavage. Cleavage is the plane along which a crystal must open according to its own construction; its direction cannot be chosen, and its place cannot be moved. The angles that the cleavage planes of one crystal make with one another are fixed from the moment of its beginning, and however great it afterwards grows, those angles do not change.

Light entering a crystal, meeting cleavage, bends.

Bent once, the light leaves its road. Bent several times, it parts into its colours. And where the bendings are very many, one beam enters and some tens of beams depart, and the places of their departure do not adjoin one another, and their colours are not the same as one another, and yet, regarded whole, all of it is the work of one beam of light.

The crystals of this place have cleavage planes numbering from some hundreds to some tens of thousands. Therefore the light of this place, wherever it enters a crystal, is parted without exception. And whatever is parted is recompounded elsewhere without exception. And what the recompounding makes is a shape belonging to this place alone — and no thing has ever looked at that shape, for in this place there is no looking.

That there is no looking does not mean that there is no knowing. The knowing of this place does not come by the image of an outward thing entering an inward one, but by the constructions of outward and inward meeting one another. That is set out in what follows.

Here only one thing need be set down: **the light in this place was parted numberless times into numberless shapes, and not once was it for the sake of being seen.**

---

The light is not the source of the heat of this place.

This matters.

The heat of this place comes from beneath the ground. Beneath the ground there is matter whose decay is very slow, and the heat it puts out has not once been interrupted since the day this world was made. That heat holds up everything: it holds up the flowing of the slurry, it holds up the growing of the crystal, it holds up the rising and settling of the tide. All the power of this place comes from below.

And the light comes from above, and the light is slight.

Reckoned by the arithmetic of a later age, what this world receives from its star is less than a small fraction of what it puts out of itself. So that if the star were suddenly not there, the temperature of this place would not immediately fall, and its slurry would not immediately set, and its crystals would not immediately cease from growing.

The life of this place does not depend upon its day.

This was never held here to be a fortunate thing. Nothing here was ever held to be fortunate, for the fortunate requires a version in which one is unfortunate, and here there was no misfortune — meaning not that all was well, but that whatever was to come had already been known, and of what has been known there is neither fortune nor its opposite.

Yet in the reading of a later age this will become the heaviest sentence of the place: **their world did not need that star. And they set the star down all the same, and after it had gone out, they went on holding it.**

To set a thing down for no use was done here only a few times. This was one.

---

There are shadows.

There are shadows, and the shadows do not move.

Whatever blocks the light must have behind it a place that is not lit. The shape of that place is given by the shape of the blocker; the boundary of that place, there being no softening gas, is the boundary of a blade, with nothing permitted between inside and out.

And the star does not move, and therefore the shadow does not move.

So the shadows of this place are all long shadows — long not in extent but in duration. A shadow falls upon a place, and that place goes unlit for ten million years. Where it is unlit, the crystal grows otherwise than where it is lit: another colour, another quality. Therefore from the site of a shadow one may know when its blocker stood, and where it stood, and how long it stood, even though that blocker is long gone.

The shadows of this place are the only writing this place has.

And the things that wrote them did not know that they were writing.

---

This light will go out.

That was computed at a very early time, and computed so finely as to give the day and the hour. The one who computed it did not on that account alter what it was doing — nothing here altered what it was doing on account of a thing to come, for a thing to come was, in this place, as certain as a thing already done.

The going out is far off. Far enough that all the living and dying and succession of this place will be finished before it.

Therefore the going out of that star will be seen by nothing that is still alive.

And at this hour — if *this hour* may be said at all in this place — the light is here.

It falls upon the slurry, and upon the crystal, and upon those shadows that have not moved in ten million years. It is parted into numberless shapes and recompounded elsewhere. It is not fierce, it is not warm, it does not move.

There is light.
