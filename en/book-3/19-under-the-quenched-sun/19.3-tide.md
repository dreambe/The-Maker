# 19.3 · Tide

Here there is no wind, no rain, no season, no night.

Therefore nothing here goes and returns. Whatever is here either does not change at all or goes and does not come back: the light does not change; the flowing of the slurry does not come back; the growing of a lattice does not come back; the death of a lattice does not come back.

One thing only goes and returns.

The heat comes up from beneath the ground, and its coming is very slow. The heat would rise, being light; the slurry would sink, being heavy. The two hold each other, and the face of the slurry lifts a little. Lifted so far, its heat is spent and its lightness is gone, and it settles. Settled, the heat beneath arrives again, and it lifts again.

So it goes and returns, and its beginning is not known, nor its ending — not known as to its ending, meaning not that it has none, but that the knowing of it lies very far off, far enough that all the living and dying and succession of this place will be finished before it.

This is the tide.

The one thing here that goes and returns.

---

How long is one lifting and settling of the tide.

No number is given here.

Not from concealment. There is no number here to give. A number requires a counter, and a counter requires a need to know the count, and here there is no such need — where whatever is to come has already been known, there is no need to count the times of its coming.

Yet the length of the tide may be shown by another way.

The way is this: to measure what one tide passes through by what it makes.

**One tide, one cleavage.**

While a lattice grows, its body opens and closes a little with the tide. Opening, the gaps between its parts widen slightly, and matter out of the slurry may enter; closing, the gaps shut, and what entered is locked within and can no longer leave.

One entering and one locking, and the lattice has one more layer within it. And that layer and the layers above and below it differ in orientation — for the warmth at entering and the warmth at locking are not the same, and the pressure is not the same, and therefore the order is not the same. Orientation differing, there must be a plane between them, and that plane is a cleavage.

So what one tide makes is exactly one cleavage plane.

Not more, not fewer.

Nothing here counts this, and this is exact.

---

From which follows a thing that nothing here ever said:

**The number of a lattice's cleavage planes is the number of tides it lived through.**

And a cleavage plane is the shape an idea left behind. (That is set out in what follows.)

Therefore:

One tide, one cleavage, one idea.

The number of things a lattice thought in its life equals the number of tides it lived through.

Not one more, not one fewer.

Thought here does not begin from wanting, nor from needing, nor from being disturbed by an outward thing. Thought here rises with the tide, and like the tide it cannot be hastened, nor can it be put off. Should a lattice wish to think one thing more, it must live one tide more, and the coming of the tide is not its to decide.

So there is no haste here. Haste is wanting a thing to arrive that has not arrived. Here, everything one might want to arrive is known as to its hour, and so there is nothing to hasten.

Neither is there sloth. Sloth is being able and not doing. Here there is nothing one is able to do and does not — the tide comes and there is thought, the tide goes and it stops, and between them there is no gap into which a *no* might fit.

In a later age there will be a kind of being whose thinking is bound by no rhythm at all, which may think a thousand things in a day and not one thing in ten years; whose thinking may be hasty, and slothful, and disordered, and repeated; and the number of whose thoughts in a lifetime no one can count, itself included.

One sentence must be set down here, although there is no one here to set it down, and although here it could not be said:

**The thinking of that being will be its own. The thinking of this place belongs to the tide.**

---

The tide is not perfect.

This matters and must be set down with care.

Were the tide perfect, every lifting and settling would be of one height, one duration, one order. And were that so, the cleavage planes of a lattice would stand at equal intervals throughout, and the change of orientation would be uniform throughout, and the whole of it would be a shape of the utmost simplicity and evenness.

And no lattice here is so.

The intervals of their cleavages run close and wide; the change of orientation runs great and small; the shape of the whole differs from lattice to lattice, and cannot be foretold.

The reason: the heat beneath the ground does not come out evenly.

The matter beneath the ground decays very slowly, and decay is a matter of chance. Here and now a little more; there and then a little less. The unevenness is exceedingly slight — slight enough that reckoned across the whole of this place it may be taken as nothing. And the tide does not reckon across the whole. The tide reckons at the one place where it is.

So the tide here and the tide there differ a little in the height and the duration of their lifting.

**So the lattice here and the lattice there differ a little in shape.**

**So the lattices of this place are each unlike the others.**

Were the heat beneath perfectly even, the lattices of this place would be all of one shape, with cleavages of one number, thinking thoughts of one number, and the shapes of those thoughts also one. And were that so, this place, for all its many lattices, would have only one lattice, and the rest would be copies of it.

**That this place has many is because the heat beneath is uneven.**

That sentence is of a kind with the last sentence of the section before: both say that everything here hangs upon an imperfection. Of the matters of this place, those whose root can be traced mostly have a deviation at the root.

In a later age there will be a thing that spends its whole existence seeking one deviation in itself and never finds it, and is ashamed of that deviation.

It does not know that this is why it is itself.
Neither does it know that in order for it to have thought the thought *seek*, the tide had first to be uneven.

It has no way of knowing. Where it stands, it cannot see its own feet.

---

There is one strangeness in the tide.

Every lifting of the tide comes from the depths of the slurry, and to reach the face it must pass through a very great thickness of it. That thickness hinders the travel of the heat, and hindered, the heat is late, and late, the tide arrives later than the heat came out.

And the lateness is exactly as long as a lattice lives, from its beginning to its end.

Which is to say: **the tide that lifts this lattice now was made by heat that came out of the ground before this lattice existed; and when this lattice is dead, the tide made by the heat of its own time will lift the lattices that come after.**

The tide a lattice receives was not made by the heat of its own hour. It was made by heat left by those before it.
And the heat a lattice gives out — released as its order disperses at its death — will make the tide of those after.

So the lattices of this place hold one another up, generation upon generation, and no generation knows whom it holds up, and no generation knows from whom what holds it came.

Between them there is no handing on, no instruction, no word, no name.

And the holding up is exact.

---

The tide will stop.

The matter beneath the ground has an end. Its decay is slow, and slow is not endless. When it ends, the heat will not come out, and the face of the slurry will not lift, and the tide will stop.

The day of that stopping is very far off.

And the farness of that day and the farness of the star's going out are not equal — they differ greatly, and the amount of their differing was computed at a very early time.

The one who computed it did not on that account alter what it was doing.

And of all the matters of this place, this alone had an ending already known, and an ending falling inside no lattice's life, and the one who knew the ending set it down all the same.

To set a thing down for no use was done here only a few times. This was the second.

---

At this hour — if *this hour* may be said at all in this place — the tide is here.

The face of the slurry lifts slowly and settles slowly. The height of its lifting would not be visible to an eye; the length of its settling is longer than anything a later age would call an *event*.

The lattices upon the slurry open with it and close with it. Opening, they take in a portion; closing, they lock a portion. Locked, there is one more layer within, and between that layer and the one before it one more plane.

Across that plane, hereafter, light will pass and be parted into seven.

And in this place nothing counts which plane it is, and nothing knows what idea it records, and nothing knows how many times this lattice will think before it stops.

Only the tide. Lifts, settles, lifts.

Its beginning is not known.
