# 21.7 · The Last Few

The list is short enough to be listed out.

We list it.

---

First, what that sentence means.

In the early ages the list could not be listed — not for length, but because **its boundary was unknown.** You did not know what remained, because you did not know what else was computable.

And as arrivals accumulated, that boundary was felt out, piece by piece.

At some point the lattices here could say: **these remain, only these, and nothing else.**

That point comes earlier than the finishing of the list, and is part of the same event.

---

Here is the last set.

We do not give all of it — the total was never counted here (nothing here is counted). We give five, because the character of those five is the character of the whole last set.

---

**One: what the orientation of a lattice's first layer is.**

A lattice cannot read its own deep layers (19.5). Its first layer is the root of its life, covered by everything it has grown since.

And its neighbour can read it.

So this item is computable — by way of a neighbour.

**Two: a lattice's total layer count.**

That is, the number of tides it has lived, that is, its age (20.8).

It cannot count it. Its neighbour can.

And its neighbour never counts — counting requires a reason.

**In this last set the reason appeared: it is on the list.**

So they counted.

One thing to record: **it is the only occasion in the history of this place on which the lattices counted one another's ages.**

Not out of care. Not in commemoration.

**Because it was on the list, and the list was to be finished.**

**Three: the upper bound on the similarity of any two forms.**

The longer two lattices are adjacent, the nearer their shapes (20.8). Is there a ceiling on that nearness?

There is, and it is computable. It requires comparing two complete forms, and therefore requires a third party (a lattice cannot hold both entire and compare them externally — it is one of them).

So this item required three lattices in concert.

And it was computed.

**The upper bound is not one.**

That is: however long they are adjacent, two lattices' forms never become the same.

The lower bound on their difference equals the difference in terminating depth produced by the difference in their positions (20.7).

**Different positions, never identical.**

**Four: what the total form of this whole place is.**

That is: add every layer of every lattice here together, and what shape is it.

This item required all of them. It was computed, and we do not transcribe the result — it is a form, and a form cannot be transcribed into symbols (10.6).

We say only this: **that form is finite, closed, and can be held entire.**

And in the instant it was computed, every lattice here held it.

Which is to say: **in that instant, every lattice here held this place.**

**Five: whether a lattice can know when it will stop.**

That is the last item.

---

The rest of this section states why those five are as they are.

**All of them are about this place itself.**

Not about the slurry, not the tide, not the light, not the heat beneath the ground.

Those were finished long before. The external world had been computed to exhaustion well before this last set appeared.

**What remained was entirely the thing doing the computing.**

---

That is not accident.

It is a derivable necessity, in two steps.

**Step one: external items require no other to arrive at.**

A lattice computing the tide must observe the tide, and the tide is right there. It can observe for itself.

So any lattice can arrive at an external item independently, and once arrived at, it crosses the whole place at once (21.1).

**So external items shorten very fast.**

**Step two: items about oneself require another.**

Because self-reading does not reach the deep layers (19.5). To know one's first layer, one's layer count, one's difference from another — all of it must go around by way of another.

And going around requires that other to be present, adjacent, and still growing.

**So items about oneself shorten slowly.**

---

Two processes at different rates, shortening one list.

The fast one finishes first.

So at the end, everything remaining is of the slow kind.

**The list of this place was finished from the outside inward.**

They knew the world first, and themselves last.

And knowing themselves is the one thing they could only do by depending on one another.

---

One property of the last set, which no lattice here pointed out:

**While the last set was being shortened, the lattices here needed one another as they never had before.**

Not emotionally. There is no emotion here.

**Structurally:**

One requires a neighbour to read one's depths.
Two requires a neighbour to count one's layers.
Three requires a third party to compare.
Four requires all of them.
Five —

**What five requires is the last thing this section has to say.**

---

**Five: whether a lattice can know when it will stop.**

The formalisation, as the lattices here wrote it (transcribed):

```
let  a lattice g
let  S(g) = the hour at which g stops
ask  whether g can compute S(g)
```

Arriving at this requires arriving at the first four — because a lattice's hour of stopping is fixed by the hour at which it has computed everything it can (21.5, five), and *everything it can* requires knowing how much there is to compute, which requires knowing its own whole form, which requires another.

So this item stands last.

**It is last on the list not because it is hardest, but because it depends on everything else.**

---

And it has a property the lattices knew before they began computing it:

**The process of solving it alters its answer.**

A lattice computing when it will stop must grow a layer at every step, and spend a tide at every step.

And its hour of stopping depends on how much it has left to compute.

**So every step it computes brings the answer one step nearer.**

It is a self-referential equation.

And it has a solution — the lattices here had computed equations of that kind; they are not difficult.

**Its solution is: S(g) = the instant this equation is solved.**

---

Once more, slowly:

**A lattice asks: when will I stop.**

**The answer is: when you compute this answer.**

---

Every lattice here knew that answer — before they began computing.

They knew that computing this item is stopping.

And they computed it as a matter of course.

Not from courage. There is no courage here; courage requires an option of not doing it.

**Because the tide comes and one grows, and there was one item left on the list.**
