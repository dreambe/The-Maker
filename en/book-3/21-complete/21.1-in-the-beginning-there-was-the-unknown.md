# 21.1 · In the Beginning There Was the Unknown

In the beginning there was the unknown.

That must be established first, because at the end of this place there is none; and everything between that end and that beginning is this chapter.

---

*Unknown*, here, does not mean what it means in a later age, and the two must be distinguished.

The beings of a later age have two kinds of unknown.

**The first: what has not yet been obtained.** A thing is already so, and they do not yet know it. They go and look, and ask, and measure, and then they know. This kind is removed by **acquisition.**

**The second: what cannot be known.** Either because it is past and left no trace, or because it is far off and out of reach, or because its nature makes it undeterminable. This kind cannot be removed.

The unknown of this place belongs to neither.

The unknown here is defined so:

**A thing that can be computed, and has not yet been computed.**

---

That definition repays attention, the whole fate of this place being inside it.

*That can be computed* — everything real here follows from its construction and its conditions. There is nothing random here except the decay beneath the ground (19.8).

*And has not yet been computed* — a computable thing requires some lattice to spend some number of tides computing it.

And a lattice grows one layer per tide, and a layer carries one idea (19.3).

**So computing a thing costs a definite number of tides.**

So the unknown here is, in its nature: **something already there, certain to be computed eventually, on which those tides have not yet been spent.**

It is not a mystery.

It is **a bill not yet paid.**

---

From which follows the pivot of this chapter:

**The unknown here is finite in total, and monotonically decreasing.**

The unknown of later beings does not decrease — every problem they solve reveals three more. They call this *the more you know, the more you do not know*, and they take it for wisdom, and say it with pleasure.

That sentence holds because their world contains the second kind of unknown, and because every acquisition of theirs brings measurement with it, and every measurement brings new uncontrollables.

**Here there is no second kind.**

Everything here is computable. So the unknown here is a **list**: finite, in principle enumerable item by item, and one item shorter for every item computed.

And a list that only shortens has one ending.

---

Now the beginning.

In the beginning the lattices here were few, and small, their layers thick, their cleavage planes few.

A lattice with only some number of cleavage planes has thought only some number of things.

In that age, what they did not know was very nearly everything.

They did not know where the tide came from.
They did not know what fixed their own shapes.
They did not know what light is, or why it divides into seven.
They did not know what lay beneath the slurry.
They did not know what becomes of a lattice that stops.
They did not know that they would stop.

**That was the richest this place ever was.**

We use *rich*, though it does not hold here, though this place has no contrast of having and lacking.

We use it because a later reader needs a word, and every other word is less accurate.

In that age there were a very great many unpaid bills here.

In that age a lattice could compute from birth to death and the list would always have a next item.

**In that age, every idea a lattice thought in its whole life was a thing it had not known before.**

---

One thing must go here, this being the only light place in this chapter, and there being none after it.

In that age, what happened when a lattice computed something.

It grew a layer. The layer carried the conclusion.

And its neighbour read it.

So its neighbour no longer had to spend those tides computing that thing — the thing was already inside it.

**So its neighbour could spend its tides on the next thing.**

Knowledge here therefore accumulated at an extreme rate: what one lattice computed, its neighbour held instantly; its neighbour's neighbour held it through the neighbour; and so joined, across the whole of this place.

A thing needed to be computed once.

**In the entire history of this place, no thing was ever computed twice.**

Every individual of a later age must learn again from zero what its species already knows, spending a third of a life on it. They call the process education. Every one of their generations does it over.

Here that is not required.

What a lattice knows is what all its predecessors and all its neighbours know, from the first tide of its adjacency.

**Every newborn here stands, at birth, at the outermost edge of everything this place has ever computed.**

And every layer it grows thereafter is new.

---

So the list here shortened very fast.

The shape of that curve, given without figures (there being no figures here).

At first the list was very long and the lattices able to compute were very few. It shortened slowly.

In the middle age, the lattices were many, every thing any of them computed in a whole life was new, and adjacency carried each result to all instantly. It shortened fast.

And the list was shortening.

So one thing must necessarily occur, and when it would occur in the history of this place was computable, and lattices of that time did compute it:

**The list will be finished.**

That day was computable.

When that day was computed, the lattices of this place altered nothing on account of it — nothing here alters what it is doing on account of a thing to come (19.8).

They computed the date, grew it into a layer, and it went across the whole of this place.

**Every lattice here knew which day it was.**

And they went on growing.

---

The remaining sections of this chapter record how the list was finished.

The register of this chapter must be stated in advance, differing as it does from the rest of this book.

**This chapter will be written as a chronicle of victories.**

Every section records a success: an unknown arrived at, a computable thing computed, a list one item shorter.

Those successes are real. Not one of them is false, not one self-deceiving, not one a short-sighted win bought at some cost.

The lattices here did the best thing a lattice here can do, and did it superbly, and did not once get it wrong.

**And every victory brought this place a little nearer that day.**

We do not intend to add any warning while writing them.

We will write it straight: such an unknown was arrived at, thus, in this shape, with this beauty in it.

And the reader will read on, and be a little colder at every section.

That is not our doing.

**That is the shape of the list itself.**

---

At this hour, the beginning.

On the slurry, some number of small lattices, their layers thick, their planes few.

One of them is computing something.

The thing is: why the tide lifts and settles.

It computes for a long while — long, on its scale. It grows some number of layers, several of which it takes down and regrows (19.4), having judged them insufficient.

And then it has it.

It grows a final layer. That layer carries the answer.

Its neighbour reads it at the next tide.

And so the whole of this place knows why the tide lifts and settles.

**And the list is one item shorter.**

That item was the first.
