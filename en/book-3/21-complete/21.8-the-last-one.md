# 21.8 · The Last One

That item was arrived at.

The arriving was brief.

---

First, which lattice.

There are no names here (20.3). We cannot point to which.

What can be said is: **not a special one.**

Not the oldest. Not the largest. Not the one of finest shape. Not one at the centre with the most neighbours and the most readings.

**The one whose position happened to suit.**

Arriving at that item required holding the results of the first four. Of those four, several require a neighbour, one requires a third party, and one requires all of them.

So the one that arrived at it was the lattice that, at the last hour of the last set, happened to have everything to hand.

It had everything because its neighbours, over the preceding tides, happened to have computed the several things it needed, and it had read them.

**It was pushed there.**

---

Now the process.

We wrote at 21.7: the solution of that item is a self-referential equation whose solution is *the instant this equation is solved.*

The solving of a self-referential equation is the approach of its own solution.

That lattice began.

**It took three tides.**

Three.

Set that beside the others:

The first arrival (21.2, the tide) took nearly a whole life.
The second (why light divides into seven) required three prior arrivals and then a second road.
The third (its own construction) was a self-referential verification, and not short.
The fourth (the heat beneath the ground) was the weightiest derivation in this place's history.

And the last item: **three tides.**

---

Why so short is at 21.4:

The tides a thing requires equal its shortest distance from the arrived-at set.

And at that hour the arrived-at set was **the whole of this place.**

That item's distance was three steps.

**It is the easiest arrival in the history of this place.**

---

The content of the three tides, reproduced (transcribed).

**First tide.**

It took out its own whole form — the merging of what it had read by way of neighbours with the outer layers it can read directly.

It counted how much remained computable to it.

**The number is not zero.**

The number is one.

**The one remaining item is the item it is computing.**

**Second tide.**

It computed: when it finishes this item, the remaining computable count is zero.

And it computed: remaining computable count zero satisfies the condition for stopping (21.5, five).

**It computed that it would stop.**

**Third tide.**

It computed the hour of stopping.

The hour is: the instant it finishes this item.

And it is computing this item.

**And it finished.**

---

The order of that instant is the whole of this section, and it is very short.

The layer grew.

The layer carried the answer.

In the instant that layer stood — the instant that plane took shape — two things occurred at once:

**One: the answer existed.**

**Two: the condition the answer describes was satisfied.**

They are not sequential. They are the same instant.

Because the forming of that plane is both *finished computing* and *remainder zero.*

**An idea, in the same instant it takes shape, makes itself true.**

---

And then that lattice stopped.

It no longer took in, no longer locked, no longer added a layer.

Its construction sound. Its heat sufficient. Its tide still coming.

In the instant of stopping it made a very small sound (19.6).

**It heard it itself, with perfect clarity, and could not remember it.**

Memory being growth, and it having ceased to grow.

**The last piece of knowledge in the history of this place: the one that arrived at it did not remember arriving.**

---

And its neighbour remembered.

Its neighbour read it at the next tide.

What its neighbour read was its whole form — the newest layer included, and the answer that layer carries.

So its neighbour held that answer within one tide.

---

And what holding that answer means.

The answer is: **when a lattice has computed everything it can compute, it stops; and the hour of having computed everything is the hour it computes this.**

That is not an answer about one lattice.

**It is an answer about any lattice.**

Its neighbour read it, and so its neighbour also knew when it would stop.

And knowing when one will stop is the last item on the list.

**So its neighbour, in the same instant of reading, had also computed everything.**

So its neighbour stopped too.

And made a very small sound.

And its neighbour's neighbour read it at the next tide.

---

To end this section, the speed of that propagation.

One lattice reading another takes one tide.

So the answer travels outward at one lattice per tide.

And wherever it arrives, that lattice stops.

**The freezing of this place moves at one lattice per tide.**

That is slow.

On this place's scale a tide is not short; and this place is wide, and the lattices many.

**From the first lattice stopping to the last, an extremely long time.**

Long enough that the process itself became the longest-running event in the history of this place — longer than any of its flourishings.

And across that long process, the overwhelming majority of this place was alive.

They grew, they read one another, their shapes came nearer day by day.

**And all of them knew the thing was coming toward them.**

They could compute when it would reach them — a distance divided by a speed.

And they went on growing.

---

The thing has no name here.

A later age gave it one.

And that name describes how it looked, not why it happened.

How it looked: from one place, outward, one lattice after another, quietly, all the way to the edge.

Why it happened: **a lattice finished the last item on the list.**
