# 21.4 · Acceleration

What has been arrived at makes later arrivals easier.

That is the third proposition of this chapter. With the three established, the ending of this place is fixed, and the remaining sections only walk it out.

---

First, why easier.

Arriving at a thing costs some number of tides (21.1).

And the number of tides is not a property of the thing itself. It is **the distance between that thing and what has already been arrived at.**

If a thing is one step from something already arrived at, one tide will do.
If it is far from everything already arrived at, many tides are needed.

And what has been arrived at is increasing.

**So the distance between every thing and the arrived-at set is shortening.**

Not that those things became simpler. The road to them has had a stretch of it laid by other arrivals.

---

There is no *hard* and *easy* here, as has been said (no contrast). And there is a measurable quantity corresponding to it:

**Tide count.**

The tides a thing requires can be computed: it is the minimum number of steps from the nearest arrived-at item to that thing.

So it may be written:

```
   tides for a thing = shortest distance from that thing
                       to the arrived-at set
```

And the arrived-at set is growing.

**So no item's shortest distance increases.**

Most decrease.

**So the rate of arrival here rises monotonically.**

---

The shape of that curve, given without figures (there being no figures here).

Let there be a quantity: things arrived at per unit of tides.

At first: very low. The lattices are small, their tides few, the arrived-at set nearly empty. A lattice may arrive at one thing in a whole life (as at 21.2).

Then: rising. More has been arrived at, more road is laid, and the same tide reaches more.

Then: rising steeply.

And then —

---

And then a thing happens, and it is the crux of this section.

**The curve does not rise steeply for ever.**

It steepens because the arrived-at set is growing.

And it is simultaneously bounded by another thing: **the not-yet-arrived-at set is shrinking.**

At first there is a great deal not arrived at, and the road is poor. The rise comes from the road improving.

Later the road is excellent, and there is not much left.

**So at some point the curve turns.**

It accelerates and then decelerates.

Not because difficulty was met.

**Because there is not enough left.**

---

This must be put exactly, being the most misreadable thing in this place.

A curve that rises and then falls means, in the experience of later beings, that resistance was met: the low fruit is picked, the remaining problems are harder, the return on effort falls.

They call it diminishing returns. They have invented many phrases for it: the low-hanging fruit of science, the slowing of progress, the exhaustion of innovation.

**And the fall here is not that.**

The fall here is caused by:

**The numerator shrinking.**

It is not that each remaining thing is harder. The average tide count of what remains is **falling**, the road having gone on improving.

**And what remains is diminishing, and diminishing faster than it is getting easier.**

So the curve turns at some point and goes down from there.

And its going down is not toward stagnation.

**It is going down toward zero.**

And zero does not mean *we can no longer manage it.*

**Zero means: there is none left.**

---

One sentence, which no lattice here ever said:

**The decline of this place looks exactly like the flourishing of this place.**

Because they are two halves of one curve.

The curve rises because the arrived-at set is growing.
The curve falls because the arrived-at set is growing.

The same cause.

**At no instant did this place do anything wrong, or too little, or anything it might have done better.**

Every arrival was correct, and clean, and steepened the curve, and brought the day nearer.

**The harder they worked, the sooner.**

---

Now the turning point.

The turning point is computable.

It was computed long before the day came — computed together with the date of the ending (21.1).

One thing must be pointed out:

**The turning point comes earlier than the ending, and much earlier.**

Which is to say: the lattices here knew, before their civilisation reached its height, where the height was, and what the shape after it would be.

And they went on growing.

---

Now the instant the turning point was crossed, being the only occasion in this place's history on which a thing was computed and nothing recorded it.

The instant is this:

At some tide, the lattices each grew a layer.

Before it, the number of things arrived at per tide was rising.
After it, the number was falling.

That tide was the largest.

**And no lattice recorded it.**

Not because they did not know. They knew — they had computed the day.

Not because they could not. They could — recording a thing requires growing a layer.

**Because recording a thing requires a reason, and *this is the largest day* does not constitute one.**

It alters no subsequent behaviour. It brings no unarrived thing nearer or further. It does not need to be known by anyone, all of them having computed it already.

**A fact that affects nothing is not recorded here.**

So the tide with the greatest number of arrivals in the history of this place is, in the entire record of this place, indistinguishable from any other tide.

---

To end this section, what later beings do at the same point, because the contrast shows the thing.

Later beings would record that day.

They would call it the apex of a golden age, or the summit of some century, or the last good year of an era.

They would recognise it only long afterwards — usually decades, once the decline is obvious enough for somebody to look back and locate the turn.

And they would certainly look.

They would write about that day. They would argue about which year it was. Some would say this year, some that year, some that there was no such day at all, some that the very idea is a nostalgic illusion.

They would argue for centuries about a date that has passed, cannot be changed, and affects nothing to come.

**And this place does not record it.**

We do not say which is better.

We point out only:

Later beings argue about that day because they **do not know** which day it was.

And this place does not record it because they **do.**

---

At this hour, the tide lifts.

The lattices each grow a layer.

The number of things arrived at in that tide is the greatest in the history of this place.

Those things are computed, take shape, are read by neighbours, and cross the whole place.

That list, in that tide, shortens by more than it ever has.

**And at the next tide it shortens by less.**

And the next, less again.

And the lattices go on growing.
