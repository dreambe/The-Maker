# 5.1 · The Tracing Back

The one who would listen was called Han Yin.

That was what eleven weeks bought us. Eleven weeks, forty letters, three referrals to the same wrong department, and one suggestion that we contact the archival enquiries desk of the national geological repository.

She took forty minutes.

At the end of the forty minutes she said, "I understand. You need money."

I said yes.

She said, "Then let's talk about where money comes from. That's a technical problem."

---

I have to start with the building.

Because the building is the first real detail in this whole business. Until then the thing existed on my hard drive, in Lu Ting's drawer, in Ilan's seven years, and at one point under the Antarctic ice that had no business being warm. From that building onward, it was a thing with an address.

North side of the city. Grey, eight storeys, put up in the nineties. By the door, a board with seven lines on it, seven names of seven organisations. The fifth line was very long.

I remember the board because it was dirty. Not unwashed — unwashable. That northern dirt with coal in it, lodged in the strokes of the characters, twenty years deep.

I stood there reading the seven lines, looking for ours. Ilan was behind me in the coat he wears in Antarctica, which in a city makes a man look like he has walked onto the wrong set. Lu Ting had already gone in — he is seventy-four, he does not stand at doors reading boards, he goes inside and asks somebody.

There was a wind that day. March, and not yet the time of year when it has any business being warm.

---

Han Yin's office was on the fourth floor and had a window. Through the window was the wall of another building.

Fifty-one, hair short, cut with intent — the short of a woman who has finished revising her appearance. On her desk: a computer, two folders, a vacuum flask. Nothing else. No photographs, no plant, no ornament anyone had given her.

I have been in a great many offices like that since. There is a relationship between how much is on the desk and how much of what the person says you can use.

She read our material three times. I know it was three because each time she finished she turned back to the first page.

The second time she made one mark with a pen.

The third time she made no marks. She only read.

Then she looked up, and her first question was:

"This hot spot. If someone had measured it twenty years ago, would they have found it?"

I said yes.

"Then why did nobody report it twenty years ago?"

Ilan said, "Somebody did."

She said, "Who."

Ilan said, "Nineteen ninety-eight. A Russian party. The anomaly is in their report, annotated as instrument calibration error."

She nodded and wrote one line on a pad.

I saw that what she wrote was the year.

---

Then she said the thing about money, and then she began on categories.

I want to set down what she said as exactly as I can, because it is the whole of this section, and because it has cost me more sleep than any datum we brought into that room.

She said: government money is divided into budget categories. Before any sum can be disbursed it must first belong to a category. A category is not a label. A category is a set of definitions — it stipulates what the money may buy, whom it may employ, what kind of report it must produce, and who reads that report.

She said: therefore the first question is not *is this important.*

The first question is *which category does this belong to.*

And this thing of yours, she said, belongs to no scientific category.

I asked why.

She said: because a scientific category requires you to state, in the application, which hypothesis you intend to test. And your hypothesis is *humanity was made.* Put that sentence on the form and the reviewers will classify it as religion, or as a question about your mental health.

She was not joking when she said it. She was reporting a fact about a process.

I asked what we do.

She said, "I have found you a category."

---

The first time I heard its name I thought I had misheard.

**Long-term continuity risk.**

She explained. The category was created in 2011, out of a set of assessment requirements concerning low-probability high-consequence events: asteroid impact, supervolcanism, pandemic, gamma-ray burst, extreme solar event, and a class of thing written down as *technological threats of non-natural origin.*

The money in that category is not large, but it is steady, and — this is the point — **it does not require you to test a hypothesis. It requires you to assess a risk.**

She said, "A risk assessment application is permitted to say *if this thing is true, here are the consequences.* It does not require you to prove first that it is true."

She said, "Written as a risk assessment, your thing gets funded."

---

I sat in that office with no view, with the radiators turned up too far, in a jumper, sweating along my back, holding a cup of tea that had gone cold — cold enough to finish in one swallow.

I understood her. I also understood the part of her meaning that I did not want to understand.

I will write it down.

**Humanity was willing to pay for this not out of curiosity.**

It paid because the thing had been filed in the same column as asteroid impact.

The first time the checksum came out was four in the morning. For the fourteen months after that I thought I was chasing a question. I thought that if our species ever discovered it had been made, the first thing it would want to know was **who**, and **why**, and **how we are supposed to think about this.**

And our species' first reaction — the one that got onto paper, the one that could be attached to money — was:

**Is it dangerous.**

---

Han Yin is not a bad person. I want that on the record, because people have said otherwise since.

She is not. She was the only person in that city willing to spend forty minutes reading the material three times. She found us the one road that went anywhere. She took a good deal of damage for it later and never once mentioned it in front of us.

She was working inside a system that speaks in categories. What she told us was that system's honest truth.

I learned something from her I am still using:

**When a system has only one language, you either learn that language or your thing does not exist.**

---

I wrote the application.

Forty-one days. Thirty-two thousand words. The most painful document of my life.

Painful not because I had to lie — I did not tell one lie. Every datum in it is real, every citation solid, every uncertainty flagged as uncertain.

Painful because of the **ordering.**

In a risk assessment, *this structure has held a constant temperature beneath the Antarctic ice for three hundred thousand years* is not a miracle. It is a **parameter.**

*The human genome contains a non-coding segment carrying a checksum* is not a discovery. It is **indicative evidence.**

And the sentence *humanity may not be a product of natural evolution* does not appear in the body of that document at all.

It appears in Appendix 3, as one of the premises of Scenario C.

Scenario C reads, in full:

> Should the structure prove to be an artefact of non-human origin, and should a causal relation obtain between it and the anomalous sequence in the human genome, then the origin of the local species (humanity) may involve exogenous design intervention. For risk assessment under this scenario see 3.7.4.

I wrote that sentence. I took the largest thing in my life and made it a subordinate clause.

The day I finished I sat at the screen and read that paragraph three times, and shut the machine down, and went to the kitchen and cooked a bowl of noodles.

I stood by the pan waiting for the water.

I remember the sound the pan made. Just before water boils there is a small dense noise at the bottom of it, like rain a long way off.

What I thought, standing there, was: of course.

**This is how the thing gets admitted into human affairs. First as a clause, then as a category, then as a sum of money.**

**It cannot come in as something magnificent. It can only come in as something dangerous.**

---

Approval came seven months later.

Forty per cent less money than we asked for, two fewer posts than we asked for, and the equipment budget — the thing Ilan needed — untouched, to the last unit.

I understood later why the equipment survived: equipment can be written off as an asset. People cannot.

---

We did not name it.

I want that clear, because everyone assumes afterward that *the Tracing Back* was thought up by somebody one night, with something poetic in mind.

It was not. It came out of a document.

Our project's formal designation in the founding papers is:

> Office for Long-Term Continuity Risk Assessment · Fourth Working Group · Special Assessment for the Attribution and Tracing Back of Anomalous Evidence Concerning the Origin of the Local Species

*Attribution and tracing back* is a fixed phrase in administrative language. It means *establish the source and the responsibility.* It is normally used in accident investigations.

The name was too long. At the first meeting, the young man taking minutes shortened it in the header.

He shortened it to two words.

**The Tracing Back.**

---

The first time I saw those two words was in the header of a set of minutes.

I looked at them for a long time.

I do not know whether the young man ever thought about what he had made. *Tracing* is following. *Back* is against the direction of the thing.

**To go back, and to go against the current.**

That is what we were setting out to do. And the person who named it was an intern typing minutes, whose only thought was that the header would not fit.

We used that name from then on.

In every formal document it is still the forty-one-word title. In our own mouths, from the first day, it was the Tracing Back.

---

The day the first money arrived we were given a room on the third floor of the grey building.

Eighteen square metres, two desks, one shelf, an air conditioner that would not cool and could only blow, and a whiteboard.

The whiteboard was old. It carried what the previous occupants had left on it — that pale grey ghost of writing that has soaked into the surface and will not come off.

I went close once. You could not tell what the words were. You could tell it was a run of equations, written dense and fast, with a circled number at the bottom right.

I do not know who they were. I do not know what they were calculating. I do not know when their project ended, or whether it ended because they finished.

**There was a piece of somebody's life on that board, and it could no longer be read.**

We began work on that board.

The last day of that March, afternoon. On the wall of the building outside the window there was a small patch of sun, climbing slowly.

Ilan was running network cable. Lu Ting was in the one chair with a back to it, looking at the whiteboard.

I remember what he said, because it was the first time I heard him make a joke.

He said:

"Fifty years in palaeontology. My whole life I have been up against one thing: the strata lay themselves down, and we can only dig from the top."

He knocked on the patch of writing that would not wash off.

"Now they have given me a whiteboard that already has strata on it."
