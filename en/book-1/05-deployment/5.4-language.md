# 5.4 · Language

Ye Qi talked for forty minutes.

Before she began she said, "None of this is mine. All of it is other people's work, all of it published. I have only put it side by side."

Lu Ting said, "That is the hardest thing there is."

---

Her first sentence was:

"Language is not one thing."

She went to the board and, in the space left among the forty-one items — a small patch at the bottom right was all that remained — wrote seven lines.

> one · fine control of breathing
> two · the shape of the vocal tract (the position of the larynx)
> three · high-speed coordination of tongue and lips
> four · auditory tuning to the speech band
> five · imitation: to hear a sound and reproduce it
> six · to make a sign correspond to a thing
> seven · syntax: to combine signs by rules, the combinations nesting

She said, "Lack any one of these and you do not have *slightly worse language.* You have **no language.**"

"Lack one, and you can think but cannot say a continuous sentence — you get three words out and have to breathe."

"Lack two, and your tract cannot produce enough distinguishable vowels; your vocabulary has a ceiling, and that ceiling is a few dozen."

"Lack three, and you speak ten times slower. Human speech runs at ten to fifteen phonemes a second, and that requires tongue, lips, velum and larynx coordinated at the millisecond."

"Lack four, and you cannot hear what is said to you."

"Lack five, and you cannot learn. Every generation starts from nothing."

"Lack six, and you have sounds and no words."

"Lack seven," she paused, "lack seven, and you have words and no speech. You can say *water.* You can say *fire.* You cannot say *there is water under that rock over there, and there was fire yesterday, don't go.*"

She put the pen down.

"These seven are seven entirely distinct sets of anatomical and neural structures. They are distributed across the thorax, the larynx, the mouth, the inner ear, the motor cortex, the temporal lobe, the frontal lobe."

"There is no reason whatsoever for them to appear together."

---

"And that is what I want to talk about," she said.

"Not how they appeared."

"But: **when each of them appeared, what was each of them for.**"

---

She went a long while, one at a time.

I set down only her conclusions here, because each of those calculations cost her years, and I cannot reproduce them at that grain.

Her method was this: suppose that of the seven, only the nth is present and the other six are not. Then what does that individual have more of than its kind, and what less.

**What it has more of is revenue. What it has less of is cost.**

**The first (breath control):** alone, revenue near zero — fine control of exhalation with no modulable tract gets you as far as whistling. Cost: enlargement of the thoracic spinal canal, altered vertebral structure, energetic and structural expense, non-zero.

**The second (position of the larynx):** alone, revenue zero. Cost: airway crossing the food passage, lifetime choking risk, killing mainly the young. This is the highest cost of the seven.

**The third (tongue and lips):** alone, revenue very small — food handling. Cost: increased innervation density, cortical area occupied.

**The fourth (auditory tuning):** alone, revenue slightly above zero — greater sensitivity to the non-linguistic calls of one's kind. Cost: low.

**The fifth (imitation):** alone, revenue not small — one can imitate other animals' calls, useful in hunting and in deception. Cost: low. **This is the one of the seven that pays for itself on its own.**

**The sixth (sign-to-thing):** alone, revenue moderate — given the fifth, a system of a few dozen words is possible, roughly what certain apes reach under experimental conditions. Cost: moderate.

**The seventh (syntax):** alone — here she stopped.

She said, "The seventh cannot appear alone."

"Syntax is a rule of combination. A rule needs things to combine. Without words, syntax operates on the empty set."

"So the seventh can only come after the sixth."

"And that is the only one of the seven with a definite ordering constraint."

---

"So," she said, "sort the seven by whether they pay for themselves alone."

She set two columns on the board.

> **pays:** five (imitation), six (signs), four (hearing)
> **does not pay:** one (breath), two (larynx), three (tongue and lips)
> **cannot appear alone:** seven (syntax)

"Three pay, three do not, and one can only arrive last."

She moved the pen to her left hand and back again — her hands were shaking at that point, very slightly; I do not know whether anyone else in the room saw it.

"So the question is: how were the three that do not pay selected for."

---

"I know the standard answer," she said.

"The standard answer is **exaptation.** A structure appears for reason A and is later co-opted for B."

"Feathers were insulation before they were flight."

"That mechanism is real. It has been confirmed over and over in biology. It is a correct and powerful explanation."

She turned round.

"So I spent two years looking for the A of each of the three that do not pay."

---

"The third — tongue and lips — I found."

"A is food handling. The human diet changed over that period; processed, cooked, soft food requires different oral manipulation. That one goes through. It is a good example of exaptation."

"The first — breath control — I found a candidate."

"A is long-distance running. Breathing decouples from stride in a running human, and that needs control. And long-distance running has independent, definite returns — item 6, the sweat glands, the midday chase."

She said, "I give that one seven out of ten. It isn't perfect, because the precision of breath control that running needs is two orders of magnitude below what speech needs. But it is a real A."

Then she stopped.

"The second, I did not find."

---

She drew a circle round the second on the board.

"The descent of the larynx."

"I looked for two years. I read everything I could get. I wrote to fourteen people; five wrote back."

"There are four candidate As."

"**One: swallowing efficiency.** Fails — the descent makes swallowing more dangerous, not less."

"**Two: a by-product of upright posture.** Partly holds — a changed cranial base angle carries the larynx with it. But the quantities don't work: the displacement from uprightness accounts for a small part, and the remainder is an active, independent change."

"**Three: body size and airway calibre.** Fails — the size-correlated part does not appear in other large primates."

"**Four: loudness.** This one is interesting. The descent does lengthen the tract and enlarge the resonating cavity, which allows louder and lower sounds. And low frequencies carry."

She wrote *four* on the board and a figure beside it.

"The trouble with this one is that low-frequency long-distance calling occurs in a great many species, and **not one of them lowered its larynx.** They use other means — air sacs, nasal resonance, sheer size."

"And none of those means requires paying the choking cost."

She put the pen down.

"So for the second, I did not find an A."

"I cannot say there isn't one. I can only say that after two years I have not found it, and I no longer see where to look."

---

Ilan said something. He said it quietly, and it was the only interruption in those forty minutes.

He said, "So our species accepted, in order to talk, a design that chokes several thousand people a year to death."

Ye Qi said, "Not *in order to.*"

Ilan said, "Meaning."

Ye Qi said:

"*In order to* implies a purpose. And evolution has no purpose — that is the core of the theory. It is a mechanism that looks only at present returns."

"So the accurate statement is: this species' larynx came down, **and at that time speech did not yet exist.**"

"The cost was paid before the revenue appeared."

---

**"Evolution does not pay in advance."**

That was the last complete sentence she said that day.

She said:

"That is the hardest thing in the theory. Evolution has no credit, no loans, no *invest now and be repaid.* It is a system that trades only in cash."

"A structure has to pay in the generation in which it appears, or at least not run at a loss. What runs at a loss is rarer in the second generation and gone by the tenth."

"So when I see a cost paid out and the revenue arriving several hundred thousand years later —"

She stopped there.

She did not finish the sentence.

She picked up her cup and drank. When she set it down the base knocked against the desk — not hard, but it was not how she usually moves.

Then she did something.

She raised her right hand and touched her own throat.

Not as a gesture. She was not indicating anything. She simply touched it, two fingers, down from under the jaw, resting where the larynx is.

Then she put her hand down.

---

Nobody spoke.

I know how long that silence was because I was counting sounds outside — somebody rode a bicycle past below, the chain ticking, left to right, and then it was gone.

Then Lu Ting raised a hand and touched his own throat.

He is seventy-four. There are age spots on the back of his hand. His fingers pressed at that place on his own neck.

Then Ilan.

Then Marcus — who did not even notice he was doing it; he was looking at the board, and his hand went up by itself.

Then me.

Five people, in an eighteen-square-metre room with an air conditioner that could only blow warm, at two desks pushed together with a two-finger gap between them, each touching their own throat.

Touching the place none of us chose, none of us agreed to, and none of us knows who lowered.

---

None of us ever mentioned it afterwards.

I walked home alone that evening, forty minutes. On the way there was a stall selling roast sweet potatoes and I bought one.

I carried it a while and then, at a crossing, I broke it open.

The steam came out. A March evening; you can see steam.

I bit into it. Too hot. I held it in my mouth and waited for it to cool.

And in those few seconds I knew one thing with complete clarity:

**This thing in my mouth, if it takes the wrong road, will kill me.**

**And that road is there, has always been there, has been there for three hundred thousand years.**

I finished the sweet potato.

I chewed carefully the whole way, very carefully, like somebody who has just learned how.
