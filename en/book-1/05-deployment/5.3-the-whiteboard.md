# 5.3 · The Whiteboard

We listed for three days.

Lu Ting set the rules. There were two.

The first: **list only what cannot be explained. Do not list explanations.**

The second: **anyone may add. No one may erase.**

---

The morning of the first day the board was empty (except for the ghost of writing that would not come off — we never wiped that, and never wrote over it, except for the expression Marcus put there on his first day, which he later moved himself).

The five of us sat round the two desks pushed together, with the two-finger gap down the middle.

Ilan said, "Where do we start."

Lu Ting said, "Start at the dullest thing. Anyone can think of the interesting thing. It's the dull thing that gets skipped."

So the first item was not the checksum, not Antarctica, not the fossils.

The first item was what Lu Ting stood up, walked to the board, and wrote:

> **1. Wisdom teeth.**

He sat back down and said, "The human jaw shortened over two million years. The teeth did not keep pace. So a substantial fraction of the species grows four last teeth with nowhere to put them and has them removed surgically."

Ilan said, "That's just evolutionary lag. There's a standard explanation for that one."

Lu Ting said, "Yes. There's a standard explanation." He pointed at the line. "Which is why it's first."

I did not understand him then. I understood on the third day.

---

Fourteen items on the first day.

I copy them as they stood, because the order of the writing on that board is closer to what was in our heads than any report we wrote afterward.

> **1. Wisdom teeth.**
> **2. The appendix.**
> **3. The lumbar spine.** (Lu Ting's. He braced his own back while writing it.)
> **4. The birth canal.** — Brain volume tripled in two million years; the pelvis did not correspondingly change. Human childbirth is the most dangerous among mammals.
> **5. The infant.** — A human newborn is, in motor terms, at the stage of another primate's foetus. This species gives birth to something unfinished.
> **6. Body hair.** — Gone. And subcutaneous fat thickened. And sweat gland density absurdly high.
> **7. The position of the larynx.** — Descended. The cost is that the airway crosses the food passage, and people choke to death. Other mammals essentially do not.
> **8. The sclera.** — White. Every other primate's is dark. A white sclera makes the direction of an individual's gaze visible to its own kind.
> **9. Genetic diversity.** — The whole of humanity is less diverse than one troop of chimpanzees.
> **10. Language.** — See Ye Qi. (Hers. Those three words and nothing else.)
> **11. Brain metabolism.** — Two per cent of body mass, twenty per cent of resting energy expenditure.
> **12. Childhood.** — Unreasonably long. A stage in which the animal cannot feed itself, lasting over ten years.
> **13. Menopause.** — A species that lives thirty or forty years past the end of its reproductive span.
> **14. Sleep.** — In deep sleep, nearly unrousable. In an environment containing large carnivores, this is suicide.

The fourteenth was Ilan's. He stood looking at the board a while after writing it, then said:

"I've slept in tents in Antarctica. One night there was wind and I didn't wake once. Next morning I came out and three metres from the tent there was a crevasse that had opened in the night."

He sat down.

"I heard nothing."

---

At four on the first afternoon we stopped.

We stopped because Ye Qi said something that turned the whole room.

She said, "The things we're listing have one thing in common, and none of us has said it yet."

Marcus said, "They all have standard explanations."

Ye Qi said, "No."

She went to the board and ran a finger down the fourteen lines.

She said:

"Eleven of these fourteen are not *impossible.*"

"They are **expensive.**"

She turned round.

"Wisdom teeth need surgery. The appendix kills. The lumbar spine hurts for a lifetime. The birth canal kills women. The infant has to be carried for ten years. The larynx chokes people. Deep sleep gets you eaten."

"These are not defects. These are **costs.**"

She paused.

"And every cost ought to answer to a revenue. Evolution does not do charity."

She took a pen and drew a vertical line down the right-hand side of the board, dividing it.

On the left she wrote: **cost.**

On the right: **revenue.**

"We move these fourteen across," she said, "and then we fill in the right."

---

That took the whole of the second day.

On the second day we added nothing new. We filled in the right.

Some went easily:

**Sclera (cost: gaze exposed) → revenue: one animal can follow another's gaze, i.e. joint attention. And joint attention is the precondition of teaching.**

**Body hair and sweat glands (cost: poor insulation, rapid water loss) → revenue: you can run an animal down at midday until it overheats.**

**Menopause (cost: consumes resources without reproducing) → revenue: a grandmother raises grandchildren, and grandchild survival rises.**

We were all in reasonable spirits when those three went in.

**At lunch Ilan said that at this rate we'd have humanity explained by Thursday and could go and apply for a Nobel. Lu Ting said our budget category is long-term continuity risk assessment and a Nobel is not a reimbursable item.**

Then in the afternoon we stuck.

---

We stuck on item 7: the position of the larynx.

Cost: the airway crosses the food passage; lifetime risk of choking. That cost is quantifiable and it is not small — we looked it up. Even in a society with modern medicine it still kills thousands a year, and in an environment without medicine what it mainly kills is small children.

Revenue: speech.

That looks tidy enough written down.

And Ye Qi asked a question.

She asked, "Which came first."

The room went quiet for a moment.

Marcus got there first. He said, "You're asking whether the larynx dropped before speech or after."

Ye Qi said, "Yes."

Marcus said, "What difference does the order make."

Ye Qi said:

"If speech came first and the larynx dropped after, then the descent was selected by the returns on language, and everything goes through."

"If the larynx dropped first and speech came after —"

She did not finish.

She only put the pen down.

---

That was a little after three on the second afternoon.

I know the time because the patch of sun on the wall of the building outside the window had climbed high and was nearly gone. The air conditioner that would not cool was blowing, and blowing warm, because it is a fan.

Marcus got up, went to the board, and struck out the word *speech* on the right of item 7.

He wrote nothing in its place.

He stood there afterwards.

Lu Ting said, "Deng. Why did you cross it out."

Marcus said:

"Because we don't yet know that it's revenue."

"If it was paid out first and the revenue arrived several hundred thousand years later, then for those several hundred thousand years it was only a cost."

"And a thing that is only a cost gets eliminated."

He turned round and, for the first time, looked one of us in the eye — Ye Qi.

"That's what you meant. Correct."

Ye Qi nodded.

---

On the third day we went back to the left and kept listing.

That day went fast, and it went badly.

> **15. Hair.** — Grows without stopping. Almost nothing else in the animal kingdom is built this way.
> **16. Beyond the white sclera, the eyebrows.** — The count of facial muscles. The human face has more muscles of expression than any other animal, and a substantial fraction of them are used only on its own kind.
> **17. The hand.** — Thumb opposition is more precise than any known use requires.
> **18. Music.** — A sequence of sounds with no information content that produces physiological effects.
> **19. Weeping.** — The only animal that sheds tears from emotion. There is no known necessary link between the physiology of tear production and emotion.
> **20. Forgetting.** — See Ye Qi. (Her second entry of two words.)
> **21. The gap in the fossil record.** (Lu Ting's. He spent an hour of the third morning writing it, because he was compressing thirty years of manuscript into seven lines.)
> **22. The sequence.** (Mine.)
> **23. The checksum.** (Mine. I gave it its own line, because it is not the same thing as 22.)
> **24. Antarctica.** (Ilan's. One word, and a dot drawn beside it.)
> …

On to the forty-first.

---

The forty-first was Marcus's, at some time after eight on the third evening, when none of us was talking much.

He wrote:

> **41. There is more information in there than a signature requires.**

He stepped back and looked at the board.

Ilan said, "That's the same as 23, surely."

Marcus said, "No."

Ilan said, "How is it not."

Marcus said:

"Twenty-three says there is a checksum in there."

"Forty-one says there is something in there besides the checksum."

Ilan said, "What."

Marcus said, "Don't know."

He capped the pen and laid it on the desk beside the gap.

"Which is why it's an item."

---

By nine that evening the board was full.

Forty-one items, top left to bottom right, five hands, three colours (three pens still had ink).

The right-hand column — the one Ye Qi drew on the first afternoon, the one headed *revenue* — had nineteen entries.

**Twenty-two were blank.**

Lu Ting was the last to leave, and before he did he did something I did not expect.

He counted them.

Not in his head. With a finger, one line at a time, like a schoolchild.

When he had finished he said:

"Forty-one."

He stood there and braced his back.

"Fifty years in palaeontology. Three unexplained things in a discipline will fill a year of conferences."

He took his coat off the chair.

"Forty-one."

At the door he looked back at the board.

"He," he said. "Has it occurred to you that a normal discipline does not have forty-one unexplained things."

I said, "Meaning what."

He said:

"Meaning either forty of these forty-one are us being stupid."

"Or they are one."
