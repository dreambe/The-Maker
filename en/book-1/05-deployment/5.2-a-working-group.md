# 5.2 · A Working Group

The establishment was five.

We had three: me, Ilan, Lu Ting.

So we had to find two.

For those two posts I contacted eleven people.

Nine said no.

---

I want the nine refusals written down, because they were all different, and because together they are the most complete lesson I have had about my profession.

**The first** was a population geneticist, in his forties, a senior of mine from graduate school. He listened on the phone for six minutes and then said, "He, answer me one thing first. If this is true, does my grant get renewed next year?"

I said I didn't know.

He said, "Then it doesn't."

He was not refusing the thing. He was refusing the consequence, and he said so with such completeness that I respect him to this day.

**The second** was a palaeoclimatologist, fifty-nine, two years from retiring. She said, "I believe you. And I would like to see it. But if I jump in now, my students go down with me. They have only just started."

I said I understood.

She said, "You don't. You don't have students."

She was right. I did not have students. It had not yet occurred to me that this was the only reason I could do the work at all.

**The third** was silence. Six weeks, not one reply. Later we met at a conference and he greeted me with enormous warmth and talked for ten minutes about a paper he had just published and did not mention my six letters. That is the commonest refusal in this profession and the easiest to bear — because it does not even give you anyone to be angry at.

**The fourth** said something I still have not worked out. He said, "I don't dare." I asked what he didn't dare. He said, "I don't dare to know." Then he changed the subject. It was only after I met Ye Qi that I began to think that sentence may not have been cowardice.

**The fifth** asked to see the data. I sent it. He read for two days and then wrote three thousand words demonstrating, point by point, that I was wrong. Four of his points were correct — those four helped us a great deal later. He was serious to the last sentence, and he did not come.

**The sixth** required joint first authorship and required that the project be renamed.

**The seventh** was a good man. He wanted to come. His wife had just had a baby, there was a mortgage, and the job he had paid twice what we could. He apologised three times for refusing. I told him not to apologise and he apologised again. Before he hung up he said, "When my kid's a bit bigger." I said all right. Nothing ever came of it and I have never held it against him.

**The eighth** laughed on the phone. Not a mocking laugh — a tired one, the laugh of a man who has heard it often. He said, "Do you know how many emails like this I get in a year?" I said no. He said, "Fourteen. The last one said the moon is hollow." Before he hung up he said, "Good luck. I mean that."

**The ninth** is the best in our field. I thought for three days before sending him the material. His reply was two sentences.

The first: "Your checksum analysis is correct."

The second: "Which is why I will not take part."

I still do not know what the relation between those two sentences is. And I suspect he was the only one of the nine who had fully understood.

---

Neither of the two who came was found by me.

---

Ye Qi came to us.

I do not know how she learned of it — the founding papers were not public and we had published nothing. She told me afterwards: someone in the office next to Han Yin's was a senior of hers.

"Administration leaks faster than academia," she said.

Her first email to me had no introduction and no courtesies. The subject line was empty. The body was three lines:

> I hear you are working on origins.
> I have a question. I have been asking it for four years. Nobody treats it as a question.
> May I come and ask you.

I wrote back: yes.

She came the next day.

---

Thirty-three, not tall, in a grey shirt washed soft, hair in a ponytail, carrying a rucksack — an actual rucksack, not a laptop bag; the kind an undergraduate carries, one strap worn pale.

She came in and looked at the eighteen square metres, and at the air conditioner that would not cool, and at the ghost of writing on the whiteboard, and sat down.

She did not make conversation. She put the rucksack on her knees and said:

"I work on the clearance of memory."

"The brain actively deletes. That is not news; it has been known since the eighties. Clearance is a process. It has enzymes, proteins, dedicated pathways. It costs energy."

"What I want to ask is: **it is done too well.**"

She took a sheaf of paper out of the rucksack — printed, the corners curled, pencil over it.

"A system that degrades fails randomly. Human forgetting is not random."

"What does a person lose first? Detail. Repetitions. The routine. What does a person lose last? The first language. Walking. The look of a room from very early on. A melody."

"And that order is very nearly the same in every person."

She pushed the sheaf into the middle of the desk.

"A randomly failing system has no order. A thing that has an order has priorities. A thing that has priorities has been ranked by somebody."

Then she stopped. She sat there with her hands on the rucksack, waiting for us.

I remember how quiet the room was.

Lu Ting spoke first. He is seventy-four; he does not respect disciplinary boundaries. He asked:

"Four years you've been asking this. What have people been answering you for four years?"

Ye Qi said:

"That different types of memory are stored in different regions, and different regions differ in their resistance to damage."

Lu Ting said, "Isn't that an answer?"

Ye Qi said:

"That is a mechanism. That is not an answer."

"It only converts *why is there an order* into *why does the damage-resistance of the regions happen to run in exactly that order.*"

"I have pushed the question down for four years. Every layer, the wording changes."

"At the bottom layer, the wording is: *because evolution.*"

She looked up.

"And evolution is a word that requires no reason. Put anything after it and it no longer has to be explained."

---

I have to set one thing down here, because it is a fork in this whole book, and I did not see it as a fork at the time.

Ye Qi stayed in that room four hours that afternoon. She finished her part, and we gave her ours — the checksum, the gap in the fossil record, the point under the Antarctic ice that was still spending energy.

When we had finished she was quiet for a long while.

Then she said one thing.

I remember every word of it, because it was unlike every other reaction in the room.

All of us — me, Ilan, Lu Ting — on understanding the thing, had the same first thought: **we have to know more.**

Ye Qi's first sentence was:

"If people were made, then human forgetting was designed."

"Which means," she said, "that I may have spent four years taking apart something that was deliberately fitted."

She looked down at her hands on the rucksack.

"I need to think about whether I should go on taking it apart."

---

She came the next day. She worked in that room for three years.

And she never withdrew that sentence.

---

Marcus Deng was not found, and did not come to us.

He was placed.

Our second post was stuck for two months. Han Yin's explanation was that it required *a young researcher with a mathematical background*, and that nobody both qualified and willing existed.

Then one day she sent an email saying there was one.

The CV was attached. Twenty-nine, mathematics, three years out of his doctorate, no postdoc, no post, four papers, none in the last two years.

I telephoned him.

He answered.

I talked for thirty seconds, saying who we were.

He cut in.

He said, "That sequence of yours. Two hundred and forty-seven base pairs, correct."

I said yes.

He said, "Have you computed its information capacity."

I said I had.

He said, "What is it."

I told him.

He said, "Have you computed how many bits a checksum requires."

I said I had.

He said, "How many."

I told him.

Then there was a silence.

Not an awkward silence — the kind of silence in which you can hear a man doing arithmetic.

Then he said:

"Then you know there is surplus in there."

"Then you know it isn't only a signature."

"When can I come."

---

He was the youngest of us and the only one who was never once moved by what the thing meant.

He does not care whether humanity was made. He told me the question was, in his view, "already settled" — "your checksum passed, therefore made; that step doesn't need discussing."

He cares about one thing:

**How much is actually written in there.**

The first day he came he wore a shirt that plainly did not fit and carried a laptop bag and a half-finished bottle of water, and his hair was the kind a man cuts himself. He does not look at your eyes. When he talks to you he looks at the wall behind you.

His first words on entering were not hello.

They were:

"Can I use the board."

I said yes.

He went over, looked once at the writing that would not wash off, and wrote an expression on top of it.

Then he stepped back and looked at the board.

That was the one thing he repeated for the next three years: write, step back, look.

---

The five of us sat at one desk for the first time on the eleventh of April.

The desk did not hold five. We carried another in from the corridor and pushed them together, and at the join there was a gap two fingers wide, and anything anyone put down in the middle fell through it. In three years that gap swallowed three pens, a memory stick, a pair of glasses, and one of Lu Ting's tablets.

That day, on the desk:

Ilan's laptop, with a sticker half torn off it;

Lu Ting's thirty-year unpublished manuscript, bound with a rubber band that had gone tacky;

Ye Qi's sheaf of curled printouts;

nothing at all of Marcus's — he brings nothing, he says what he needs is in his head and all he needs is a board;

and my manila folder, holding thirty-two thousand words written in forty-one days, in whose body the sentence *humanity may not be a product of natural evolution* does not once appear.

Five people.

Not the best five in the country. Not the best five in the field.

**The five, out of eleven, who were willing to sit down.**

It took me a long time to see that this was not bad luck.

**The nature of the thing determined that only such people could do it.**

A person with prospects does not come. A person with students does not come. A person with a new baby does not come. A person who knows he gets fourteen of these a year does not come.

Who comes is: a woman who has just been politely buried at a mainstream conference; a man of seventy-four who has stopped caring about his prospects; a glacial archaeologist, marginalised seven years, sober, divorced; a young researcher who has asked the same question for four years and been answered for four years without once being taken seriously; and an unemployed mathematician who cares only how many bits are in two hundred and forty-seven base pairs.

**We were not selected. We were what was left.**

---

Before we broke up that afternoon, Lu Ting said something.

He got up out of the one chair with a back — his back was bad that season, and he had to brace himself before standing — and looked at the four of us.

He said:

"Fifty years in this business. The largest project I was ever on had three hundred and seventy people."

"Three hundred and seventy. We thought numbers were strength."

He slid the tacky rubber band off the manuscript and wound it round a finger.

"Later I understood something. Whether a project produces anything does not depend on how many people are willing to come in."

"It depends on how many **cannot leave.**"

He looked at us.

"None of you four has anywhere to go. Correct."

Nobody answered.

He put the band back on the manuscript.

"Good," he said. "Then we start."
