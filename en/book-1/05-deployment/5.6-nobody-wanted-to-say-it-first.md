# 5.6 · Nobody Wanted to Say It First

After Marcus capped the pen, the room was quiet.

I have counted that quiet many times since. It was not long — about three minutes.

And in those three minutes something very strange was true:

**All five of us already knew what the word was.**

**And not one of us said it.**

---

I want to set down what I saw in those three minutes, because I have never since been in a room where that happened.

**Marcus** stood in front of the whiteboard with his back to us.

He did not come back and sit down. He stood where he had written the number, looking at it. His right hand hung at his side, still holding the cap of the pen — the cap is a separate object; he had capped the pen and put the pen in the tray under the board, and the cap was still in his hand.

He held that cap for three minutes.

He was waiting for one of us to speak.

I know he was waiting because he did not walk away. His habit, having finished explaining a thing, is to walk away at once and look at something else — he does not look at people, he does not wait for reactions. That time he did not go.

**He would not say it himself, because he is twenty-nine, because he had been unemployed three years, because he was placed here.**

**And he would not walk away, because he knew what that number meant, and he wanted to watch us admit it.**

---

**Ye Qi** had her head down.

Her hands were on the rucksack on her knees. Her rucksack was on her knees — in three years in that room she never put it on the floor; she kept it on her knees with both hands on it.

She was looking down at her hands.

She was not looking at the board.

And I want to write one thing here that I only worked out later:

**Of the five of us, Ye Qi was the only one who did not need that number.**

Her seven, she had already priced. On some evening two years earlier, alone in a laboratory, she had already got that far. She had not computed zero point four one; she had computed something else, and she had arrived at the same place.

So that afternoon, listening to Marcus's nineteen minutes, she was not hearing a new thing.

**She was hearing a place somebody else had also reached.**

**And she did not say it, because she had already not been saying it for two years.**

**A person who has held it in for two years does not open her mouth in the third minute.**

---

**Ilan** was moving.

He was the only one of us moving in those three minutes.

First he rolled his pen on the desk a few times. Then he put it down and closed his laptop — it had been open, with his Antarctic data on the screen. He closed it.

Then he stood up, went to the door, opened it, and stood in the doorway.

He did not go out. He stood in the doorway and looked down the corridor.

There was nobody in the corridor. At the end of it there was a window, and that afternoon the window was dark.

**He stood in the doorway because he wanted the option of walking out.**

**And he did not walk out, because it had taken him seven years to send that email.**

I asked him later what he was thinking in those three minutes. He said he was thinking about his ex-wife.

I asked why.

He said, "Because she told me once I'd ruin my life on an idea."

He said, "Standing in that doorway, what I thought was: she was right, and it's already too late."

---

**Lu Ting** was writing.

I did not notice at the time. I only learned it much later, when I found the sheet of paper among his things.

For those three minutes Lu Ting sat in the one chair with a back, pulled a page out of his own manuscript, turned it over, and wrote on the back in pencil.

I copy what he wrote.

I have that sheet now.

It reads:

> August 1974. I was in a pit in Shaanxi, brushing out a bone.
>
> The shape of that bone was wrong.
>
> I was twenty-two. I asked my teacher. He said: Lu, when the shape is wrong there are three possibilities. One, you have damaged it. Two, it is something else. Three, you are lucky.
>
> I asked which.
>
> He said, the first two you can check. The third you cannot check, so don't think about it.
>
> I have remembered that for fifty years.
>
> My whole life I have been checking the first two.
>
> This afternoon a man of twenty-nine wrote a number on a whiteboard.
>
> That number rules out the first two.

The last line is in smaller writing, crowded in, as though he decided to add it only when he had got that far:

> I waited fifty years for a piece of luck I did not want.

**He would not say it, because he is seventy-four, and because he knew that the moment the word came out of his mouth the room would hear it as an old man's lifelong obsession finally finding its outlet.**

**And he wanted the word to come from someone younger, with less history behind it.**

---

**Me.**

I want to write my three minutes.

I was sitting in my place, and my place faces the window, so I could see the wall of the building outside. That afternoon there was no sun on it. It was late.

I was thinking about my mother.

Not for any reason that can be explained. I was not connecting the two things. She was in a care facility then, and it was a Friday; I had visited on the Wednesday.

What I was thinking about was the pill box.

Seven compartments. Four tablets in each. The Wednesday compartment.

**What I was thinking was: the tablets in that box were put there by me.**

**And I put them in in an order: left side first, then right; top row first, then bottom.**

**And while I was doing it I was thinking about something else.**

**And when she takes them, she does not know that somebody arranged the order.**

---

That is what I was thinking in those three minutes.

And I did not speak, because the moment I opened my mouth I would say the word, and the moment I said the word the thing would be true.

Until those three minutes, we had forty-one unexplained items, a sequence carrying a checksum, a hot spot under the ice, and a figure computed by a man of twenty-nine.

Those are all **things.**

And a word is not a thing.

**A word is a position — you say it, and you are standing in that position, and you cannot get back to the place where you had not said it.**

I knew what the word was.

I had known since the third evening, from the moment Lu Ting finished counting the forty-one items and said *or they are one.*

**I did not say it because I run this project, and because I knew one thing: the moment the word came out of my mouth, it would go into the minutes.**

---

Three minutes.

The sky outside was going. Nobody had turned on the light — none of us had gone to turn it on, and the room was dark enough that the right-hand column of the board, the one headed *revenue*, could no longer be read.

The air conditioner that would not cool was still blowing.

Ilan was still in the doorway.

Ye Qi was still looking at her hands.

Marcus was still holding the cap, still with his back to us, still standing where he had written the number.

Lu Ting finished the sheet, folded it, and put it back among the pages of his manuscript.

Then he braced his back.

Then he stood up.
