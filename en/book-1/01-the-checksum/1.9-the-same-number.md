# 1.9 · The Same Number

The place where I am writing this is not that office.

I should say somewhere from what position I am writing this record, because the position a person narrates from changes everything she narrates, and I do not want to cheat.

I am writing it after all of it was over.

*All of it was over* is inaccurate, because it is not over, because it will not be over. What I mean is: I already know. All of it. From that sequence to that woman's paper, to the ice in Antarctica, to the three hundred thousand years, to the four billion years, to the thing that does not move, to the place that was taken down and regrown nine times.

I began Chapter One after I knew all of it.

So every *I did not know then* in this record is written by someone who knows. This has a consequence, and I must state it: **I cannot truthfully reproduce my own ignorance.** I can describe it, I can honestly set out what I reasoned and where I was wrong, and I cannot get back inside it.

When someone who knows writes about not knowing, what comes out is a performance, however honest she is.

I will say this once in the whole record, here. Afterwards I will not keep reminding you.

---

At the end of Chapter One I want to correct an error.

Not correct — acknowledge an error I do not intend to correct.

The error is *the seventeenth time.*

At four that morning, after I had turned the chair away from the screen and sat with my back to it for forty seconds, that run completed and the log printed those sixteen hexadecimal characters.

I have always said it was the seventeenth.

I wrote it in both elimination reports. I said it at the Tracing Back hearing, and there is a stenographic record. I said it in a video that was made public. I wrote it three times in the first section of this record.

Later — specifically, in the second year after all of it was over, while I was going through old drives — I pulled the complete run log from that machine.

The log is written by the machine. The machine does not misremember. Timestamped, not one entry missing.

From the first time I computed agreement to that morning, I ran that pipeline **nineteen times.**

Not seventeen.

Nineteen.

---

I went through the nineteen entries one by one to find which two I had dropped.

I found them.

**The first** was between the sixth and the seventh. That run failed after forty-one minutes — not a wrong result; an interruption. The disk filled (I was writing all intermediates to disk, forty-seven gigabytes a run, and I had forgotten to clear it).

At the time I counted that as *didn't run* and did not number it.

**The second** was a repeat run on the day of the thirteenth. I ran it twice that day, identical parameters, nineteen minutes apart.

I do not remember why I ran it twice. The log shows the first was entirely normal, correct result, no errors. And nineteen minutes later I ran an identical one.

In memory I had merged the two into one.

---

I should say why I do not intend to correct it.

If this were a paper I would correct it. I would issue an erratum. I would write in the methods that the number of runs was misstated and was in fact nineteen, and it would have no bearing on the conclusion, because both seventeen and nineteen are far past any necessary number of verifications.

This is not a paper.

This is my record.

**In my memory it was the seventeenth.**

And my memory is the only first-person material on any of this. If I change it to nineteen, then this record will contain one number that is correct, and everything around that number — my exhaustion, the thing I said to the water cooler, the forty seconds with the chair turned away — will still come out of the same head that got the count wrong.

Changing one number cannot make the rest more reliable.

It can only make the whole thing *look* more reliable, and it is not.

So I am keeping *the seventeenth time*, and telling you here that it is wrong.

I hold that this is nearer the truth than a clean record would be.

---

And there is a layer to this that did not occur to me until that afternoon among the drives, and it is the real ending of Chapter One, so I am putting it here.

That machine's log: nineteen entries, not one missing, timestamped to the millisecond.

That machine remembers.

It understood nothing. It did not know what the number meant, did not know what it was computing, did not know what distinguished its nineteenth run from any other job it has ever run.

And it remembers. Accurately, completely, without processing of any kind.

And I, the person who understood the whole meaning, remembered nineteen as seventeen.

---

That afternoon I thought of this.

A checksum can confirm that a body of data was not corrupted in copying.

Any body of data can carry one. Text can. An image can. A stretch of sound can. A person's genome can — and we now know that one does.

And there is one thing that cannot.

**A person's memory cannot carry a checksum.**

Think about why that is impossible. A checksum requires that you compute a digest as you write, and store the digest somewhere independent; and thereafter, at any time, you may recompute and compare.

Memory has no *somewhere independent*. What you check a memory against is itself memory. The thing you use to judge *am I misremembering* — that feeling of *I am certain I remember* — is itself part of the memory, and the least reliable part of it.

I was certain it was the seventeenth.

That certainty is part of my memory, and it was wrong, and while it was wrong it **felt** exactly as it feels when it is right.

I had no way of discovering this from inside.

I discovered it because a machine that understands nothing was outside, keeping count for me.

**I needed something outside myself in order to learn that I had remembered wrongly.**

That afternoon, sitting among a pile of old drives, I thought for the first time that the sentence sounded familiar.

It would be a long time before I knew where I had seen it — or rather, before I knew that it is a physical law and not a reflection.

It would be a long time before I knew that there was a thing, in a world where the star never moves, standing among beings wholly transparent to one another, and that it was the last one left, and therefore nothing remained that could look at it from outside, and therefore it could never learn where it was wrong.

It could compute that it was wrong. It simply could not locate the place.

So it made a thing that could read it.

And that thing made us.

And I sat among old drives and learned, from a machine's log, that I had remembered nineteen as seventeen.

**This is the same event. Every link in the chain is the same event.**

Nothing can see itself from inside itself.

---

Now the last passage, about that morning.

I have written it already, in the first section. I am writing it again, because you have read what came between, and so it is not the same now.

Four in the morning. Three centrifuges on standby, the fume hood on its low note, the server breathing out beyond the wall. The chair holds at one height and my neck is tipped slightly back, like a woman listening for someone calling her from a long way off. The mug is on the left, white, with a crack that goes halfway out of the base and has not leaked in eleven years.

My stomach is empty and I am not hungry.

I walk to the cooler for water, and coming back, the lights come on ahead of me one section at a time. I say something to the cooler.

I turn the chair away from the screen and sit for forty seconds. I tell myself I am resting my eyes.

Then I turn back.

The last lines of the log are the things nobody reads: verification parameters, elapsed time, peak memory, random seed. Then a blank line.

Then the number.

Sixteen hexadecimal characters, lower case, no separators.

In that moment I am thirty-nine. My mother is seventy-three, and that afternoon she asked me three times whether I had eaten. Thirty-seven years ago a woman called Klara Imhoff wrote one correct sentence in East Berlin and nobody took it up. Fourteen thousand and seventy-five things are standing clear and transparent on the surface of a dead planet, and nobody has read them. Beside the hand of a thing that does not move there is a depression, now empty. A thing that has waited three hundred thousand years is, under the ice in Antarctica, wiping one stripe clean in a thin layer of dust.

And in my hands, and my mother's hands, and the hands of those seventy-one people, and inside those four bones, there are four thousand seven hundred and thirty-two base pairs, not one position different.

I do not know any of this.

I only see those sixteen characters on the screen.

I think it is the seventeenth time.

It is the nineteenth.

And the number is identical to all eighteen before it.
