# 1.1 · The Seventeenth Time

By the seventeenth time I had stopped hoping it was wrong. I only hoped it would be wrong in a different way.

The first six runs, I was looking for my own mistake. Runs seven through twelve, I was looking for the machine's. From the thirteenth I stopped looking for anything. I just let it run — the way a person keeps pressing a switch that no longer turns anything on, not because they believe it will, but because the hand needs something to do.

A laboratory at four in the morning has a particular sound. Not silence. Silence is something else; silence does not exist at four. It is the standby hum of three centrifuges, the low note of the fume hood, and the server cabinet on the far side of the wall breathing out and out and out. You cannot hear any of it during the day, because during the day there are people. At four it surfaces, the way stones surface when the tide goes out. You begin to separate the sounds from one another, and then you notice you are counting them. This is not a good sign, and I had been counting them for three weeks.

My chair was broken. The gas lift leaked and it would only hold at one height, two fingers higher than I needed. For three weeks my neck had been tipped very slightly back, like a woman listening for someone calling her from a long way off. I knew I should replace the chair. I knew a great many things needed doing.

My stomach was empty and I was not hungry. After the third week those two stop being the same thing.

The mug was to my left. Eleven years old, white, with a crack in the base that starts on the inside and travels halfway out and stops. It does not leak. Every year I assume this is the year it will leak, and every year it does not. Zhou Yan said once, *that's you*, and did not explain what he meant, and I did not ask. We were already at the stage of not asking.

On the screen was the progress bar of the seventeenth run. It was moving slowly, because I had set the verification depth far deeper than necessary — a superstitious act, and I knew it, and I was treating it as penance. If I made the arithmetic slow enough and painful enough, perhaps the result would take pity on me.

---

I should say what it was I was calculating.

What I do has a name that is not popular in my field: palaeogenetic linguistics. It sounds like two unrelated things nailed together, and that is roughly accurate. My training is molecular evolution. My work is reading.

I read the genome as text. Not as program.

That sentence needs unpacking, because everything that happened to me afterwards comes out of it.

Nearly everyone who reads a genome reads for function: what does this stretch do, what does it regulate, what breaks if you take it out. That is an entirely legitimate way to read. It is also useful; it cures things; it attracts money. I read for something else. Syntax. Redundancy. Repetition. The distribution of copying errors. Which regions look as though they have been altered, and which look as though they have been carefully left alone. I do not ask what a stretch of sequence *does*. I ask what it *looks like*.

An epigrapher looking at a broken stele does not need to know whether the thing written on it is true. He reads the pressure of the cutter's hand, the period of the letterforms, the line where the spacing suddenly tightens — because the man doing the cutting had misjudged the width of the stone. Out of that he can reconstruct an afternoon.

I wanted to reconstruct an afternoon inside the genome.

In review this is generally rendered as *the author's methodology is suggestive but lacks testable predictions.* I have received that sentence seven times, phrased almost identically each time. I suspect they copy one another.

Three weeks earlier, my methodology had produced a testable prediction.

It had passed. It is the thing I least wanted to happen in my life.

---

The sequence itself is unremarkable.

Chromosome eleven, four thousand seven hundred and thirty-two base pairs, sitting in the middle of a gene desert — meaning that for a few hundred thousand bases in either direction there is no known coding gene. It expresses no protein. It regulates nothing known. No database annotates it as a functional element. I have knocked it out — in cells, not in a person; I am not that far gone — and nothing happened.

By rights, a thing nobody touches should be worn down into noise. Evolution does not protect the useless; it simply declines to interfere, and declining to interfere means handing the thing to time, and time flattens everything. Any sequence under no functional pressure becomes unrecognisable within a few million years.

It had not been worn down.

I aligned seventy-one modern human genomes, plus four ancient samples: two Neanderthal, one Denisovan, and one I should not have had, given to me privately by a colleague. All identical. Not *highly similar*. Identical. Four thousand seven hundred and thirty-two positions, not one of them different.

That is strange enough. But strange is not a crime. The genome is full of strange things; we call them *outstanding questions* and leave them that way for decades. If that had been all, I would have written a short paper, placed it in a middling journal, been cited eleven times, and forgotten about it.

What actually made me sit down, three weeks earlier, was something else.

The last quarter of that sequence can be computed from the first three.

---

I need to stop here, because I have practised the next paragraph in front of a mirror, and every time I finish practising it I want to wash my hands.

Suppose you have a string of digits. At the end you append a few more, and the way you choose them is this: you run every preceding digit through a fixed rule and put the result at the end. Afterwards, at any point, you can rerun the rule over the front and see whether it agrees with the back. If it agrees, nothing has been altered. If it does not, something somewhere went wrong.

The thing at the end is called a checksum. The last digit of your identity number is one. There is one inside the barcode at the bottom of a supermarket receipt. Every time you open a webpage, every packet thrown back and forth between your machine and someone else's carries one.

It has exactly one purpose. **It checks whether a copy was made correctly.**

Which means there was a copying. Which means somebody was worried about copying wrong.

Nature does not do this. Nature is full of redundancy, full of error correction, full of backups — DNA repair is one of the most beautiful pieces of engineering I know of. But all of it is *local*, *chemical*, and *in situ*. There is no known biological mechanism that reads a stretch of sequence in its entirety, computes a digest of it, and writes the digest somewhere else. That requires something capable of reading a thing *entire*. Cells do not read anything entire. A cell is a blind man running his hands along a very long rope.

And the thing I computed agreed.

The first time it agreed it was twenty to four on a bright afternoon three weeks earlier, and someone was talking on the phone in the corridor. My first thought, word for word, was: *Shen He, you have made a mistake.*

That is a healthy thought. I am still proud of it. How many chances does a person get, in the second she is struck by something that could make her name, to think first that she is wrong — I got one, and it did not save me.

---

The first six runs were a hunt for my own error.

I rewrote the script. I changed implementation language. I did a stretch by hand — genuinely by hand, on paper, in pencil, two and a half days, until there was a callus on the side of my index finger. I asked a graduate student I trust, who had no idea what any of it was for, to redo the same thing along her own lines. She got it. She asked me what it was. I said it was an exercise.

That was my first lie. It was small. I did not notice at the time that it was the first.

Runs seven through twelve were a hunt for the machine's error. Floating point. Byte order. Some known defect in some version of some library. I replaced the entire software stack. I reran it on a machine of a completely different architecture. I invented a reason to use the university's old server that nobody likes, because it is old, because its errors ought to be different from a new machine's.

Its errors were the same as a new machine's.

From the thirteenth run I no longer had a hypothesis.

I just let it run. I set the depth deeper, turned the logging up to its most verbose, wrote every intermediate result to disk — forty-seven gigabytes of intermediates per run, all of it material I was never going to look at. In hindsight the behaviour is legible: I was lengthening the time before the number appeared.

A person can do a very stupid thing in a very clever way, and the cleverness only makes the stupidity look more respectable.

---

At eighty per cent I got up for water.

The cooler is at the end of the corridor. The lights are on sensors, so they come on a section at a time; walking down, the sections behind me went out one after another, and walking back, the sections ahead came on one after another, as though something were following me whose only courtesy was to light the floor at my feet.

I stood at the cooler for a while. It was still dark outside, but it was the kind of dark that is about to stop — not ink, but deep water, with something underneath. A city is at its most honest at that hour. Not one lit window is lit for anyone else's benefit.

I said something to the cooler. I do not remember what. Something along the lines of *you're up too.* A person three weeks short of sleep begins talking to objects. It is nothing. I have done less dignified things.

When I sat down again the bar was at ninety-seven per cent.

And then I did something I would rather not admit to. I turned the chair away from the screen and sat with my back to it for forty seconds.

I told myself I was resting my eyes.

---

It finished.

I turned back. The last lines of the log were the usual things nobody reads: verification parameters, elapsed time, peak memory, random seed. Then a blank line. Then the number.

Sixteen hexadecimal characters, lower case, no separators.

Identical to the previous sixteen runs.

Identical to what I had computed in pencil, on paper, three weeks before.

Identical to what the last one thousand one hundred and eighty-three base pairs of that sequence translate into.

---

Here is the part I have never told anyone, including the people who came later with government paper and sat me in rooms and asked me to go through *the process of discovery* once more. I gave them a different version. A respectable version, with rigorous elimination and measured awe in it.

What actually happened is that my hands knew first.

Before I had finished reading those sixteen characters — before my mind had identified them and matched them against the string in my memory — both hands had already come up off the keyboard. They came up to roughly the height of my chest, palms down, fingers slightly spread, as though someone were about to take something out of my arms and I were cooperating.

I do not know what that gesture is. It does not belong to any emotion I can name. I looked for it afterwards in a great many places — I watched a lot of footage, the kind from operating theatres and courtrooms and earthquakes, trying to find one person making the same movement, so that I would know what it was called.

I never found it.

Much later I described it to Keeper. This was long afterwards, under the ice, when I already knew nearly all the answers and this was the last small thing I did not have one for. I made the gesture for it.

It watched for a long time. Then it said: *That is something we did not design for you.*

It said it in exactly the tone it says everything in. It only has the one tone. And in that second I felt something for it for the first time, and I do not know what that is called either.

---

The pain in my stomach started about three minutes later.

That is a physical fact and I am recording it, because a great deal of what follows in this account will not sound true, and I need you to believe me. There is a line in my notes from 5:14 that morning. It reads, in full: *hurts on the left, not sour.*

I finished the water in the mug. It was room temperature, because I never press the hot button, because the hot button has to be held for three seconds and it beeps, and I do not like it beeping.

Then I did a correct thing. I encrypted the results.

Not because I had thought through the consequences — three weeks in, I could not have imagined any consequences — but for a smaller and dirtier reason. I did not want anyone else publishing first. That is who I am. Faced with the largest thing in my life, my first grown-up thought was priority.

I do not intend to apologise for it. Everyone I know would have done the same. They would only have told the story differently.

---

Daylight arrives without a process.

You sit in a lit room with a black window, and then for some length of time you do not look at the window — four minutes, twelve, you are looking at something else — and when you look again the window is grey. Nobody has ever seen the part in between. I sat through three weeks of sunrises in that laboratory and never once caught the moment.

The grey starts in the gaps between buildings. First the gaps, then the sky, and only afterwards the things on the ground. A bird begins somewhere in there, one bird, singing with no necessity whatsoever.

That morning I noticed for the first time that the wall of the building across from ours carries a long water stain, running from the eighth floor down to the third, shaped like the tributary of a river. I had sat in that room for six years. The stain had certainly been there all along.

A person can spend six years in a place and then see it for the first time on some particular morning.

I remember thinking: if I had not seen it this morning, I might never have seen it. And it was there the whole time.

I know now that this was the first time in my life I thought about something the right way. I was simply thinking about the wrong thing.

---

I did not go home.

At seven forty there were footsteps in the corridor. The first to arrive is a postdoc from the group next door; he arrives at seven forty every day; I have never exchanged more than four sentences with him. He passed the doorway, saw me, nodded, and said, *early.*

I said, *mm.*

That was my second lie, and slightly larger than the first.

At ten past eight I went and washed my face. There is an old scratch on the washroom mirror, exactly at the level of my left eye. Six years. Every morning my left eye is cracked. I lifted my head and looked at the face. Three weeks. Thinner. Bad colour. The skin under the eyes gone thin — thin, not dark; around forty, what you get from not sleeping is thinness, not darkness.

I said to the face: *all right, then.*

I did not know what I was agreeing to.

---

The screen had gone dark by the time I got back. I moved the mouse.

It came up.

Sixteen hexadecimal characters, lower case, no separators.

The seventeenth time.

The same number.
