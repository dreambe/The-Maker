# 1.7 · A Second Person

Seventy-one genomes.

I wrote that figure eleven times in each of the two elimination reports. I have said it from a podium. I have produced it without thinking when asked about sample size.

And it was the fourth month before it occurred to me for the first time: that is seventy-one people.

---

Genomes in public databases come with metadata.

Not much. For reasons of privacy, nearly all identifying information is stripped. What remains is usually: an anonymised number, year of collection, sex, an age band in ten-year intervals, a broad self-reported ancestry, the project the collection belonged to, and a code for the type of consent form signed.

One afternoon in the fourth month I pulled the metadata for all seventy-one and made a table.

I do not know why I made it. It was of no use to my analysis. I could not even put it in a report — *the author examined the age distribution of the samples* is a methodologically meaningless sentence.

I made the table and then I read it, for about an hour.

It looks like this:

```
number        year    sex     age band    project
─────────────────────────────────────────────────
sample 007    2009    F       50–59       A
sample 011    2009    M       20–29       A
sample 014    2010    F       70–79       A
sample 019    2011    M       30–39       B
...
```

The youngest band is ten to nineteen. There are two.

The oldest band is eighty to eighty-nine. There is one.

That one was collected in 2008.

Which is to say that if they were in their eighties then, sixteen years on —

I stayed on that line for a long time.

---

Then I did a thing of no methodological significance whatever and of enormous significance in my own history: I went to the project's public materials to see whether it was possible to learn whether that donor was still alive.

It is not. Of course it is not. The entire apparatus is designed to make it impossible.

So I went and looked at the project's consent form instead — that is public; projects generally release the template.

I read it.

It is a good consent form. Clear, honest in tone, without those sentences a legal department inserts to shift all liability onto the donor. It states what the sample will be used for, states that the data will be public, states that a donor may withdraw at any time but that data already released cannot be recalled.

And one paragraph runs:

> Your data may be used for research purposes that cannot presently be foreseen. The direction of science is not predictable. We cannot promise you that your data will be used only for the research described in this project. We can promise you only this: that whoever uses it will be bound by the same ethical standards as we are.

*Research purposes that cannot presently be foreseen.*

On some day in 2008, in some phlebotomy room, that person read that sentence and signed.

They were in their eighties.

They **could not possibly have foreseen** that sixteen years later a thirty-nine-year-old woman with a bad neck and a broken chair would use their blood to test whether the human species was manufactured.

I read that sentence seven times.

I wrote a line in my notes:

> That consent form is the only place in this entire affair where somebody made room for the unforeseeable.

---

That evening I did another thing.

I printed the seventy-one sample numbers in a single column, seventy-one lines.

And beside each line, by hand, I wrote something.

Not a name — I have no names. I wrote the most concrete thing derivable from the metadata, in a phrase.

Like this:

```
sample 007    she gave blood in her fifties
sample 011    he was in his twenties
sample 014    she was in her seventies, 2010
sample 019    he was in his thirties
...
sample 063    she was a teenager
...
sample 070    he or she was in their eighties, 2008
```

Seventy-one lines.

Then I folded the sheet in half and tucked it inside the back of that notebook.

It is still there. I have never shown it to anyone, because in any professional setting it would make me look unstable.

And I have always thought it was the most correct thing I did in that first year.

Because until then I had been testing a hypothesis.

After it, I knew I was testing something about seventy-one people, none of whom had any idea.

---

Then I made a seventy-second.

---

I should set out the technical procedure, because it was against the rules.

Not illegal. I broke no law. What I broke was an internal rule of our institute concerning autologous samples: research staff shall not include their own biological material in research they lead, on grounds of conflict of interest and risk to data integrity. The rule is correct. Anybody who thinks about it for a moment can see why it exists.

That night I did not think about it.

The procedure.

There are lancets in the lab drawer, and microcapillary tubes, and buffer. Ordinary consumables; always there.

I washed my hands. I wiped the tip of my left middle finger with alcohol.

I pricked it.

Too shallow; nothing came. I pricked again, slightly to one side. That one worked.

I squeezed out about three drops.

I did the whole thing alone: extraction, quality check, library, sequencer. For the run I used the institute's shared rota and gave it a false sample name — `NC-19`, which looks like a negative control.

I waited twenty-six hours.

---

The data came back at two forty in the morning.

I was in the office. I remember I had not put the overhead light on, only the desk lamp. I remember it was cold and I was sitting in my coat.

I ran the pipeline.

Four thousand seven hundred and thirty-two positions.

The first three thousand five hundred and forty-nine, read out.

The last one thousand one hundred and eighty-three, read out.

I pressed return.

Sixteen hexadecimal characters, lower case, no separators.

Identical to the one from the seventeenth run.

---

I did not cry.

I had always assumed I would cry at a moment like that. I had imagined the scene many times in those months — not that exact scene, but its kind: a truth landing, a person alone in a room, and then tears.

I did not cry.

I got up and went to the washroom.

I washed my hands.

I washed them for a long time. I used the pink soap that smells bad and worked it up three times. I washed between the fingers, under the nails, up over the wrists.

Somewhere in the fourth washing I saw the mirror.

There is an old scratch on that mirror exactly at the level of my left eye, so my left eye is cracked.

I watched the woman in the mirror washing her hands.

And then I understood what I was doing.

I was trying to wash it off.

---

I turned off the water.

I stood there with the lather still on my hands. I lifted them, to about the height of my chest, palms toward me.

And I looked at my own hands.

They are thirty-nine-year-old hands. There was a fresh small opening on the tip of the left middle finger, from the prick, not yet closed.

Nails short, because I cut them short. A vein standing on the back of the hand, more visible on the right. A patch of hard skin at the base of the thumb, from holding a pen — I am still the kind of person who works things out on paper.

Every cell in those hands carries it.

Every cell contains those four thousand seven hundred and thirty-two base pairs, not one position different, and the last one thousand one hundred and eighty-three of them equal the result of a definite operation over the first three thousand five hundred and forty-nine.

I have carried it since I was born.

I carried it in the womb.

I carried it at seventeen, memorising a biology textbook. I carried it on that building site, looking at two stones. I carried it on the day I married and on the day I divorced.

For thirty-nine years I did not know.

**And it passed every time.**

Every time one of my cells divided, that stretch was copied. And every time, had there been a check to run, it would have passed.

A whole life. Every time.

---

I have no idea how long I stood in front of that mirror.

Eventually I rinsed the lather off, dried my hands on paper, and went back to the office.

I encrypted the data and put it in the directory called `the two stones`.

I did not name the file `NC-19`.

I named it after myself.

Then I took the folded sheet out of the back of the paper notebook and opened it.

Seventy-one lines.

I wrote a seventy-second.

It reads:

```
sample 072    she was thirty-nine, April, two forty a.m., pricked it herself
```

Then I folded it again and put it back.

---

One last thing here, though it did not come to me straight away. It came on a Wednesday, a few days later.

That Wednesday I was at my mother's.

I was cutting her nails. She sat in the cane chair with her hand in mine, cooperative, like a child. Her hand is smaller than the one in my memory. Her nails are hard and difficult; old people's nails thicken.

I stopped for a moment at the little finger of her left hand.

Because it occurred to me: she carries it too.

Every cell in her body has those four thousand seven hundred and thirty-two base pairs. All her life, every division, that stretch copied, and every time it passed.

Not once wrong. Seventy-three years, not once wrong.

And inside that same person, in her head, another set of things is being cleared.

That set is her. It is her students' names, and how she draws a contour line, and what her sister looked like, and her daughter's name, and the map that did not get wet when she slid down a slope at twenty-six.

That set is being lost, a little each day, precisely, by priority, in some order.

And those four thousand seven hundred and thirty-two bases: not one of them different.

**In one body, one thing has been preserved perfectly for seventy-three years, and another thing is being systematically cleared.**

And the thing that was preserved does nothing.

I put her hand down and said: *done.*

She said: *that was quick.*

I said: *mm.*

She said: *you should do yours too, yours are long.*

I said all right.

She watched me do it. She watched closely, like an inspector.

When I finished she said: *bit short.*

I said: *short is better, easier to work.*

She said: *true.*

Then we watched an hour of television. A programme about fruit from somewhere. Three times in the middle of it she asked whether I had eaten.

Three times I said I had.
