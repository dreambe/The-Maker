# 1.3 · A Thing That Does Nothing

I did not discover that sequence.

A great many accounts have got this wrong, and I have corrected it every time, until nobody wanted to hear the correction any more. I understand why they do not want it. A person discovering a thing that changes everything is a story. A thing lying in a public database for thirty-seven years, downloaded and looked at and aligned and skipped over by at least tens of thousands of people, is not a story. It is a fact that makes everyone uncomfortable.

It has a number.

`HCE-11q4732`.

HCE for Highly Conserved Element. 11q for its position, the long arm of chromosome eleven. 4732 for its length. That number was assigned in 2006 during a large reannotation. Before that it had three other numbers, in three different systems, two of which are now defunct.

The first time I pulled its full annotation history was nine days after that afternoon. It took me a whole day. What I read was not the sequence. It was the annotations — the things other people had written about it across thirty-seven years.

That was the quietest day of my professional life, and the nearest I came to crying, and I did not cry, because there were people in the lab.

---

Its history runs like this.

**1984.** It enters the record for the first time. Nobody was sequencing it — a group was sequencing the gene next door, the reaction ran on further than intended, and the surplus was recorded in passing. This was ordinary at the time. That stretch had no name. It is line fourteen of a table in the supplementary material of a paper about something else.

**1987.** Somebody describes it on its own for the first time. I will come to that, because it is the heaviest thing in this whole account.

**1993.** An American group annotates it as *possible pseudogene*. A pseudogene means: a thing that was once a gene, then broke, and does not work now. It is a very reasonable guess, and it is convenient — once a stretch of something is called a pseudogene, it stops being *a thing requiring explanation* and becomes *a thing already explained*.

That annotation stood for thirteen years.

**2006.** Large reannotation. Somebody ran a pipeline over it and found it fitted no known pseudogene profile: no traces of inactivating mutation, no corresponding functional homologue, no recognisable wreckage of a promoter. It is not a broken thing. It was never that kind of thing.

So *possible pseudogene* was deleted and replaced with `HCE`: highly conserved element, function unknown.

Let me be fair here: the 2006 reannotation was right. The people who did it were careful. They changed a wrong answer into an honest blank.

And an honest blank cannot survive in a research system, because a blank cannot be funded.

**2011.** A review of conserved elements in the human genome mentions it in one sentence, as one example among *several elements of anomalously high conservation and unknown function*. A footnote.

**2014.** A large population-genetics project lists it under *regions of zero polymorphism*. There are one hundred and forty-seven entries on that list. It is first.

It is first. The list is ordered from lowest polymorphism upward, and it is zero — absolute zero — and none of the other one hundred and forty-six is absolute zero.

I looked at that list for a long time. The gap between first and second place is larger than the gap between second and one hundred and forty-seventh.

The body of the paper does not discuss this, the project having had a different purpose.

**2019.** A preprint uses it for a methodological demonstration. The authors were validating a new alignment algorithm and needed a stretch of *known complete conservation* as a positive control.

It was used as a ruler.

When I got to that line, that afternoon, I laughed. Actually laughed out loud; the student at the next desk turned round.

We took an impossible thing and calibrated our instruments against it.

---

Now 1987.

That year, a journal called *Zeitschrift für Molekulare Systematik* published an eight-page paper. Sole author, signed **K. Imhoff**, at an institute in East Berlin. The institute was dissolved in 1992.

The journal itself folded in 1990.

The paper is in German. It has never been digitised.

Here is how I found it. I was following the citation chain backwards through the annotations, and I got to the 1993 American paper with the pseudogene call, and its reference forty-one is that German paper. I went to look it up. Every database has the record and none has the text. Three weeks.

In the end it was in the basement stacks of our own old library. That building went up in 1958; the stacks are on the second basement level; you need a key and a member of staff to accompany you.

The volume was on the bottom shelf. 1987. Blue cloth. Almost no gilt left on the spine.

When I pulled it out, dust came down on the back of my hand.

Inside the front cover was a lending card — the old kind, a stiff card in a kraft-paper pocket, borrowers and dates written by hand.

There were two lines on the card.

The first: March 1988. A name I did not know. The stamp of a department that no longer exists.

The second was blank. I filled it in.

Which is to say: from March 1988 until that day — thirty-six years — that book was borrowed zero times.

---

I do not read German.

I sat in the stacks, on a wooden stool nobody is supposed to sit on, and photographed the eight pages with my phone, one at a time. The light was bad; I did it three times before they were legible. Then I went upstairs, ran it through a machine, and corrected it sentence by sentence.

Two days of correcting. Because I do not trust machines, and because I was afraid it would translate something important wrongly.

The structure of the paper is extremely plain. She describes what she observed in a stretch of sequence, gives all the data she was able to give (which, for that era, is pitifully little), eliminates what she was able to eliminate, and then writes two pages of discussion.

What she noticed and what I noticed thirty-seven years later are two halves of the same thing.

She noticed that the sequence was identical across every sample she could obtain. She could obtain about twenty people — all East Germans, all blood from herself or her colleagues. In that place, in that era, that is what a researcher could get.

Twenty people. Four thousand seven hundred and thirty-two positions. All identical.

Then she did something rather clever: she estimated how much variation the sequence ought to have accumulated in the absence of any selective pressure. Her method looks crude now; her direction was exactly right. Her conclusion was that observation and expectation differed by at least two orders of magnitude.

And then she did a thing that almost nobody would have done at the time. She did not go looking for its function.

The first sentence of her discussion reads as follows. I corrected this sentence seven times, because I had to be certain I had not translated it into something stronger than she wrote:

> The customary procedure, upon observing the anomalous conservation of a sequence, is to search for its function, since we assume that conservation arises from function and that function imposes selective pressure. In the overwhelming majority of cases the assumption holds. The purpose of this paper is to point out that whether it holds *here* is a question capable of independent examination, and not a premise.

Eight pages. Two pages of discussion. The last paragraph runs:

> If this sequence is indeed so conserved, then the question is not what it does.
>
> The question is: **who is not permitting it to change.**

---

I sat in the office until very late that afternoon.

I was not thinking about the sequence. I was thinking about a person.

1987, East Berlin. A woman. Sole author. Twenty blood samples. A sequencing setup that looks like a toy now. She looked at a photograph of a gel, worked out an order of magnitude, and wrote down a sentence that is still, thirty-seven years later, the most accurate statement of this entire matter.

And she had no instruments. No seventy-one genomes. No four ancient samples. No machine that runs a full verification in four hours. She could not even take the next step, because what the next step required did not exist in 1987.

So she wrote the sentence and did the only thing she could do: published it, in a journal that folded three years later, in a language most of her field did not read.

And then she waited.

---

I looked her up.

**Klara Imhoff**, born 1943. Molecular biology. The 1987 paper is her ninth. It is also her last.

In 1991 her institute was assessed, restructured, dissolved. She was forty-eight. She never entered another research institution. Afterwards the record has her doing popular-science translation and teaching at a secondary school.

She died in 2004, aged sixty-one.

I found one photograph, on an alumni memorial page, badly scanned. A middle-aged woman, short hair, seated behind a desk with papers on it. She is not looking at the camera. She is looking at what is on the desk. Whoever took it had evidently called her over on the spur of the moment, and she had not come all the way out of what she was doing.

I know that expression. I see it in the mirror every day.

---

Then I did something unprofessional.

I wrote to the archival successor of that institute and asked whether they held Imhoff's working notebooks.

Forty-one days later I had a reply. They did not. In the course of the 1992 dissolution, non-core records were destroyed as required.

I wrote again and asked whether, under the requirement in question, working notebooks counted as non-core.

They did not reply.

I understand. It is not their fault. It is one of several thousand comparable decisions made while a system came apart thirty years ago, and whoever made it may well be gone too.

I only wanted to know whether she ever thought about it again.

1991 to 2004. Thirteen years. Teaching secondary school. Translating popular science. Did she know that the question she asked in 1987 had not been taken up by one single person on earth?

Did she ever, some evening, having finished marking a stack of homework, suddenly think of that photograph of a gel?

I will never know.

---

In my own notes, from that day on, I gave the sequence a name.

I do not call it `HCE-11q4732`. That number is for pipelines, not for people.

In my notes I call it **the stele**.

The reason does not need explaining. Two stones standing side by side, one character wrong at the break, a man who copied it, and someone who three hundred years later put them next to each other.

And this stone stood in a database for thirty-seven years. Tens of thousands of people walked past it. Someone gave it a number. Someone guessed wrongly what it was. Someone put it first on a list and did not stop. Someone used it as a ruler.

One person read it, in 1987, and had no instruments, and so wrote down the question and left.

I have instruments.

That is the only difference between her and me. The difference is not ability, or intelligence, or nerve. It is thirty-seven years of technical progress and a contract that was not dissolved.

Had I been born in Leipzig in 1943, I would be her. Had she been born here in 1986, she would have computed that checksum three weeks ago, and faster than I did.

---

I made a clean document of the eight pages, with my notes on the translation.

I put it in the same directory as my encrypted results.

I had typed some throwaway name for the directory at first. Later I changed it.

I changed it to three words.

`the two stones`
