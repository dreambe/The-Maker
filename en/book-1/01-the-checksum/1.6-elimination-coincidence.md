# 1.6 · Elimination (ii): Coincidence

I did the probability three times.

I will give the results, then say why three times, then say why three times was not enough.

---

**First pass: the naive arithmetic.**

The sequence is four thousand seven hundred and thirty-two base pairs long. The last one thousand one hundred and eighty-three are the result of a definite operation over the first three thousand five hundred and forty-nine.

If the bases at those one thousand one hundred and eighty-three positions were random, the probability that they happen to equal that result is four to the power of minus one thousand one hundred and eighty-three.

In decimal, about ten to the minus seven hundred and twelfth.

When I wrote that number out on paper, I wrote a 1, then a decimal point, then seven hundred and eleven zeroes, then a few digits. It took me about four minutes. Then I looked at the page for a while.

I did not do that for rigour. Rigour does not require writing out the zeroes. I wanted to see how long it was.

It took a page and a half.

---

I should show how meaningless that number is, and the way to show it is with comparisons.

The number of atoms in the observable universe is about ten to the eightieth.

The age of the universe, counted in Planck times, is about ten to the sixtieth Planck times.

If you took every atom in the observable universe, made each one a machine, and had every machine make one independent attempt in every Planck time from the beginning of the universe until today — you would have made about ten to the one hundred and fortieth attempts.

Ten to the one hundred and fortieth.

And I need ten to the seven hundred and twelfth.

Which is to say: spend the entire matter and the entire time of the universe on this one thing, and you are still five hundred and seventy orders of magnitude short.

I wrote a line in my notes:

> This is not a low-probability event. This is an event with no probability. Probability is for things that can happen.

---

**Second pass: why the first does not count.**

The first pass has a serious problem, and I found it myself the morning after I finished it, and I was rather pleased — finding my own errors was a comfort in those weeks.

The problem: **bases are not random.**

Genomic base composition is biased. GC content varies widely between regions. Some short motifs occur far more often than chance, others far less. And more to the point, the region this sequence sits in has its own statistical character.

By using one quarter per position I was assuming a uniform random background, and the real background is not uniform.

What does non-uniformity do? It raises the probability. Because if bases have preferences, some combinations are easier to get than random would suggest.

So I recomputed.

I took five million bases either side of the sequence as background, estimated local base composition, dinucleotide frequencies, and trinucleotide frequencies, built a third-order Markov model, and recomputed the probability of agreement under it.

Result: ten to the minus four hundred and thirty-first.

*Only* ten to the minus four hundred and thirty-first.

The difference between that indulgent estimate and the strict one — two hundred and eighty orders of magnitude — is of no practical significance whatever. Both are far outside the reach of any physical process. The distinction is like asking someone whether they need to walk to the moon or to the sun. Both answers are *you cannot walk there.*

I wrote the two numbers side by side in my notes, and beside them, three words:

> makes no difference.

---

**Third pass: multiple comparisons.**

This is the pass that actually matters, and it did not occur to me until two full weeks after the second. It occurred to me in the shower. I did not turn the water off. I stood there thinking for about eight minutes, and when I came out the floor was flooded.

The problem is this.

Suppose I search everywhere in the genome. Three billion positions, each admitting any number of window lengths, each length admitting any number of candidate *checksum functions*.

If the search space is large enough, then even if the probability of any single hit is minuscule, I will **necessarily** get some.

This is the most classical trap in statistics. Test ten thousand genes for association with a disease at a five per cent threshold and you will *find* five hundred of them, even if all ten thousand are irrelevant.

The trap is called multiple comparisons. It kills a great deal of published work every year, including a great deal of honest work.

And what makes it dangerous is this: **you cannot tell from the result whether you fell into it.** A true positive and a multiple-comparisons false positive look identical in the data.

The difference is only in **how you came to find it.**

Which is why this section is written more carefully than any other.

---

I did three things.

**One: I own my search history.**

I did not scan the genome. I started from Imhoff's paper — that is, the sequence was **pointed out by someone in 1987**, and her reason for pointing at it had nothing to do with checksums. Her reason was conservation.

This matters enormously. It means the sequence was **not selected by me out of three billion candidates.** It was handed to me, thirty-seven years earlier, by somebody else, for a wholly independent reason.

That reduces the search space from three billion to one.

**Two: I own my choice of function.**

The operation is not mine, and not picked out of infinitely many. It is an existing, standard, widely used class of check function. And before testing, I fixed the candidate list: seventeen functions, all standard, none of my own construction.

Seventeen candidates.

One passes.

The other sixteen do not — and their failure is not *near misses*. Their outputs bear no relation whatever to those one thousand one hundred and eighty-three bases.

That is: I did not tune until it agreed. One of seventeen agreed and the other sixteen were nowhere.

**Three: a whole-genome negative control.**

This was the heaviest thing I have done, and it consumed six weeks of machine time.

I applied all seventeen candidate functions to the entire human genome.

Method: from every position, take windows from one thousand to ten thousand in length, split in the same proportion (the first three quarters predicting the last), run all seventeen, and check agreement.

About four hundred billion tests in total.

Result.

Positions passing: **one.**

That one.

The negative control is clean. Four hundred billion tests, zero false positives.

I should say why that result is itself strange: on pure statistical expectation, four hundred billion tests should have produced some incidental agreements — windows overlap, sequence repeats, some regions have extreme composition. Going in, I expected tens to hundreds of false positives, and expected to have to argue that the one differed from the hundreds.

And the false positive count is zero.

Not *few*. Zero.

I checked that twice, because zero usually means the code is broken. The code is not broken — I spiked artificially constructed positive sequences into the genome and the pipeline found every one of them.

Four hundred billion tests. One hit.

And the one hit is the stretch a woman in East Germany pointed me at thirty-seven years ago, for a reason entirely unconnected.

---

I wrote this up as a second elimination report. Twenty-seven pages.

The conclusion runs:

> The coincidence hypothesis requires all four of the following: (i) that the observed agreement occurs in a sequence not selected from the genome by this study, but specified by independent literature predating it by thirty-seven years on unrelated grounds; (ii) that the agreement occurs within a pre-fixed candidate set of seventeen standard functions, of which exactly one agrees and the outputs of the remaining sixteen bear no relation to the observation; (iii) that a whole-genome negative control of approximately four hundred billion tests yields a false-positive count of zero; and (iv) that under a local third-order Markov background the probability of chance agreement is bounded above by ten to the minus four hundred and thirty-first.
>
> The coincidence hypothesis is eliminated.
>
> This study notes that the foregoing constitutes no support for any alternative explanation. It establishes only that the observed phenomenon is not accidental.

I spent a long time on that last sentence. Six drafts.

Because it is the only honest sentence in the report.

**Elimination does not produce answers. Elimination only clears the ground.**

Five weeks to eliminate contamination. Two months to eliminate coincidence. By that point I had established two things: it is not dirty, and it is not accidental.

I was still infinitely far from *what it is*.

---

That night I finished the last pass, saved the output, and closed the window.

And then I did something: I sat down.

I need to explain that, because it sounds like nothing.

For three months I had been in motion. Running pipelines, writing scripts, chasing literature, going down into the basement stacks, writing to a German archive, thinking about multiple comparisons in the shower, rerunning at four in the morning for the seventeenth time. There was always a next thing.

Having a next thing is a form of protection.

And that night I was done.

I had welded on the two heaviest legs. I knew what came next — what came next was the fossil record, the gaps that should not be there — and what came next was **somebody else's field.** I would have to read things I did not understand and ask questions of people who would look down on me.

So for about twenty minutes that night, I had no next thing.

I sat in the chair that only holds at one height, my neck tipped slightly back. The screen had gone dark. The room held nothing but the standby hum of three centrifuges and the server breathing out and out beyond the wall.

And I let myself think about the question for the first time.

Not *what is it.* That question was too large; I could not hold it then.

I thought about a much smaller question, and it grows straight out of the definition of a checksum:

**If somebody was worried about copying wrong — what were they worried about copying wrong.**

What a checksum protects is what comes before it. The first three thousand five hundred and forty-nine bases.

Those three thousand five hundred and forty-nine bases do nothing. They do not express, do not regulate, do not leave a trace. They are a thing that does nothing.

And somebody considered them important enough to protect with a check.

In those twenty minutes I had, for the first time, a thought that got me out of the chair:

**There may be content in those three thousand five hundred and forty-nine bases.**

Not function. **Content.**

And I had no idea how to read it, because it is not a gene, it encodes no protein, it has no biological meaning whatsoever.

At that moment I did not know who to ask.

It would be nearly a year before I sat in a meeting room across from a twenty-nine-year-old mathematician who would listen for ten minutes and then say: *You have got one thing wrong. You keep asking what it does. What you should be asking is how much information it has.*

And I would say: aren't those the same question.

And he would say: not remotely.
