# 1.4 · Checksum

My student is called Shao Cen. Second-year master's, twenty-four.

She is cleverer than I am. I can say that without any pain at all — it took me until thirty-nine to be able to say it without pain, and it is one of my few accomplishments.

Her cleverness is not like mine. Hers is the clean kind, unemotional, extremely efficient: give her a method and in a day she will take it apart, understand it, point out two places where it could be improved, and then use it exactly as given, because improving it is not this week's task.

She is not interested in *why*.

I do not say that as a criticism. I have known far too many people who are interested in *why* and cannot produce anything, and I am one of them, and one of the bad ones. People like Shao Cen are the actual foundation of research. You need people who do not ask why, who do things correctly, for many years.

And the first time she left me with nothing to say, it was with a *why*.

Except that she did not think it was a *why*. She thought it was a technical question.

---

This was nine days after the two and a half days of pencil, when I had asked her to reproduce the thing independently.

I packaged the task like this: I said I was writing a short methodological note and needed an independent implementation to check mine against. I gave her a sequence (with its provenance stripped, the identifier changed, a false species tag attached), and an operation, and asked her to determine whether the last one thousand one hundred and eighty-three bases equalled the result of applying that operation to what preceded them.

She finished in a day and a half. Her answer was *equal*.

She sent it to me with a note: *That's a strange operation. Did you design it yourself?*

I said no, it was a standard one.

She said: *Oh, so it's a checksum.*

I sat in front of the screen for a moment.

I wrote back: how do you know.

She said: *Because it only has one use.*

---

She came to the office the next day. I made her tea, and poured water for myself into the cracked mug — I only have the one mug; guests get a teacup, I get mine.

I decided to explain it properly, because she had already guessed half, and a clever person who has guessed half will go and guess the other half on her own, and I did not want her guessing.

I said: when you buy something, that row of numbers under the barcode — do you know what the last digit is?

She said no.

I said: that digit is not part of the code. The digits in front are the code, and the last one is computed — weight each preceding digit by a fixed rule, sum them, take a remainder. When the till scans it, it reads all the digits, recomputes the last one from the front ones, and checks whether they agree.

She said: if they don't, the scan was bad.

I said: yes. Maybe the barcode is smudged, maybe the angle was wrong, maybe the ink went on unevenly at printing. The machine does not know which. The machine only knows **that this read cannot be trusted**, so it beeps and the cashier scans again.

She said: so that digit is for the machine.

I said yes.

She said: so every time we buy something, what we're actually doing is letting the machine confirm it hasn't misread.

I said yes.

She looked down and drank some tea. She said: *that's rather nice.*

That was the first sentence she said with feeling in it, and what she said it about was the check digit.

I liked her enormously in that second.

---

I told her where the thing came from. Twenty minutes or so. Far more than was necessary, because I had not spoken to anyone in three weeks and I needed to speak.

I started from the recent end.

1948, Bell Labs. A mathematician named Richard Hamming. The computers of that era took punched cards, and the card readers made mistakes.

Hamming could not get into the machine room at weekends. He would queue his job in the batch and come back Monday morning for the output.

Frequently the output was nothing — one card had misread a single bit, the machine detected the error, halted, skipped his job, and went on to the next person's.

His whole weekend, wasted.

Not once. Several weekends running.

He wrote about it himself later, and the sense of the line is: if the machine can detect the error, it knows where the error is; and if it knows where the error is, why can it not simply correct it.

So he invented the Hamming code — not merely detecting but correcting. That work is the root of modern digital communication. Your phone, your disk, every photograph a deep-space probe has ever sent home, all of it stands on that.

I said to Shao Cen: the entire theory of error correction begins with one man's weekends being wasted several times over, and him getting annoyed.

She laughed. She said: *now that's a proper motive.*

---

Then I went further back.

I said: checking is much older than machines.

Double-entry bookkeeping — every transaction recorded twice, and the two sides must be equal. That is a check. It appears in thirteenth-century Italy, and its purpose is not primarily to prevent embezzlement. It is to prevent **arithmetic error**. A hand copying figures gets figures wrong, and a wrong ledger goes on being wrong down the whole length of it, until nobody can trace it. So they invented a constraint: the two sides must balance. If they do not, there is an error somewhere behind you, and you can walk back and find it.

She said: that's the ancestor of the checksum.

I said: it is one of them. There is an older one, and it is closer.

I said: the scribes.

---

I told her about the copying of the Torah.

The rules are extraordinarily strict. The preparation of the parchment, the recipe of the ink, the material of the pen, how many lines to a column, how many letters to a line, which word must begin which column — all of it fixed.

And among them is this one:

**When you have finished, count.**

Count the letters on the page. Count the lines in the column. Count the occurrences of certain words in the whole scroll. Compare what you counted against what it should be.

If they do not agree, you have copied it wrong.

And if they do not agree — that parchment is discarded. It cannot be used, cannot be mended, cannot have one letter scraped out and rewritten. The whole sheet.

I said to Shao Cen: think about that for a moment. A man, in a room, copying a thing he believes must not be got wrong. He has no machine. He has no second exemplar. He has no way whatsoever, once he has finished, of going back and verifying that what he copied is correct.

He has one method available to him. **Count.**

A count carries no content. A count tells you only *complete* or *not complete*.

He counted for thousands of years.

And what he counted, and what the mathematician with the ruined weekends computed in 1948, and the beep the till makes when you buy milk, are the same thing.

Shao Cen said: so it isn't new.

I said: it is not remotely new. It may be one of the oldest abstractions our species has, only slightly younger than number itself.

She said: because as soon as people started copying things, they started being afraid of copying them wrong.

I said yes.

---

Then she asked the question.

She set the cup down and said: *I have a technical question.*

Go on, I said.

She said: *A checksum can only tell you whether it was copied wrong. It cannot tell you what the original said. Correct?*

I said: correct.

She said: *So if it doesn't agree, we know there's an error, but we don't know what the right thing was.*

I said: correct. You can only copy it again, or go and ask for the exemplar.

She said: *And if it does agree?*

I said: then it was not copied wrong.

She said: *We still don't know what the original said.*

---

I sat there.

The cracked mug was in my hand. I remember the water in it had gone cold. I remember there were clouds outside. I remember someone downstairs sounding a horn.

I remember all of that because that second was very long.

She was entirely right.

**A checksum confirms transmission, not content.**

A correct checksum tells you: this thing was copied across correctly. It tells you not one word about **what the thing is**.

You can hold a file that passes verification perfectly and have no idea what it says. Several trillion such files are moving across the world every second. Machines confirm that they are complete. Machines never read them.

For three weeks I had thought that what I had found was an **answer**.

In that second I understood that what I had found was a **receipt**.

A delivery note. It reads: *item received complete, no loss, no alteration.*

And it does not say what the item is.

---

I do not remember what I said to her. I think I said *good question*, and then we talked about something else. She washed the cup before she left — she washes it every time; I have told her three times not to; she washes it every time.

After she had gone I sat in the office for a long while.

It was dark before I put the light on.

I wrote two lines on a piece of paper. I still have it; it is tucked inside my notebook. Two lines:

> checksum = *what you received is what I sent*
>
> checksum ≠ *what I sent*

And then I added a third line, and my hand was shaking as I wrote it, so the writing is bad:

> then why sign it.

---

I should explain why that was the most important sentence available that day.

The existence of a checksum is already a strange thing: it means somebody was copying, and somebody was afraid of copying wrong.

But it also means something else.

**A checksum is for the recipient.**

You do not compute a checksum for yourself. You compute one because the thing is going to be delivered somewhere else, and that somewhere else needs to be able to confirm for itself that what arrived is complete.

A checksum is a device pointed at the future and at another party. Its entire existence points at a reader who has not appeared yet.

There is a checksum in that sequence.

So that sequence was copied *to* somebody.

And the checksum is there because whoever did the copying wanted **the receiving party to be able to verify that it had not been copied wrong.**

Which is to say: whoever did the copying anticipated a recipient capable of performing that verification.

And three weeks earlier, I performed it.

At twenty to four on a bright afternoon, with someone talking on the phone in the corridor, I completed a motion that had been reserved for me a very long time ago.

I did not discover it.

I **answered** it.

---

Marcus Deng told me later that those three lines on that piece of paper were the most important thinking anyone did in this whole affair, more important than computing the checksum.

I said hardly.

He said: no, genuinely. He said, computing the checksum was execution. Asking *why sign it* was posing a question. And the answer to that question is not inside the checksum, which is why you went looking for something else, which is why I had a job.

I said, that question wasn't mine.

He said: whose was it.

I said: a twenty-four-year-old master's student who does not care about any of this, and only wanted to know what the method guarantees technically.

Marcus thought about that for a while. Then he said something I have kept.

He said: *All the real questions come in that way. They are never thought up. Somebody knocks into them by accident, and the person who knocked usually walks off knowing nothing about it.*

I said: she knows now.

He said: was she pleased.

I said: she said *oh, I see*, and then asked when the data would be ready.
