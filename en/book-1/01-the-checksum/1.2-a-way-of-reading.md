# 1.2 · A Way of Reading

I went into biology because I was bad at chemistry.

I have said that in a great many rooms, and every time somebody laughs, and every time it is not a joke. In my final school year I could not make physics or chemistry work. Biology I could do, because at that stage biology is largely memorisation, and my memory was good. At seventeen I believed my gift was memory.

It took me a long time to learn that my gift was something else, and that it is the opposite of memory.

My gift is this. **I can see that a piece of something is wrong, and cannot say what would be right.**

This is useless in examinations. In research its value is doubtful. In personal relationships it is a catastrophe.

---

My supervisor's name was He. He was an extremely good man, good in a way that makes him impossible to blame. Molecular evolution, the standard road, one subject for a lifetime, done deeply, no errors and no brilliance. His students all turned out steady.

His assessment of me was one sentence, delivered three times, identically worded: *Your problem is that you think too much and do too little.*

He was right. In three years I produced less than one good paper's worth of work, and filled seven notebooks with ideas that never took a shape.

In my third year he sat me down properly. He said, *Shen He, you should be doing theory. Or philosophy. You do not belong in a wet lab.*

I said, *Professor He, I don't want to do philosophy.*

He said, *Why not.*

I said, *Because philosophy has no data.*

He looked at me for a long time. Then he said something I still have.

He said: *Then you are going to have to do a very hard thing. You will have to ask philosophical questions in a place where there is data. Very few people in this world manage it. The ones who don't manage it have an unhappy life.*

I said I knew.

I did not know.

---

After I finished I took a bad road: three postdocs, two countries, one near-exit from the field. From twenty-nine to thirty-four my CV was a string of respectable institutions and a string of unrespectable output. I learned two things in those five years. How to be useful inside somebody else's project, and how to hate myself late at night.

I got better at the second.

At thirty-four I came back. I got a position, neither good nor bad — a small independent group, two people, funding that just about held, review at six years. The afternoon I signed I sat in the office a long while, because I understood that I could no longer say *I'm on someone else's project.*

That office is the one I sat in for six years, up to the seventeenth rerun. The scratch on the mirror, the gas lift in the chair, the water stain on the building opposite — all of it starts that afternoon.

---

I want to describe the turn, because everyone has asked me about it since, and the answer I have always given is a respectable version.

The respectable version: while reading the literature on genomic repeats, I noticed that certain distributional features were statistically comparable to repetition phenomena in natural language, and I began borrowing tools from textual analysis.

That is true. It is also not true. The true part is that I did write it that way in a grant application. The untrue part is that it is not when I thought of it.

I thought of it on a building site.

Twenty-six, second year of the PhD, autumn. Zhou Yan was not yet my husband; he was a man I had known five months, a structural engineer, interning on a heritage restoration. He asked me to come and see the site, on the grounds that *you'll like it.* I know now that if a person says *you'll like it* to you, and is right, that thing will follow you for the rest of your life.

It was a Qing-dynasty ancestral hall being taken apart and rebuilt. The courtyard was stacked with numbered timbers, each with its position chalked on it. I looked at the numbers for a long while and said, *those are specimen labels.* He said, more or less, except these go back in.

Then he took me to see two steles.

Two of them, side by side against the foot of a wall, both commemorative, saying roughly the same thing. One was the original, Ming. One was a Qing recutting. The recutting existed because the lower half of the original had broken.

He said, *Look at this one.*

On the Qing stele one character was cut wrong. Not a wrong stroke — an entirely wrong character, a near-homograph in place of the right one.

I said, *Copying error.*

He said, *Yes. Now look at where the error is.*

I looked for a while and did not see it.

He said: *The character it got wrong sits exactly at the break on the original. On the original, the top half of that character is gone. The man cutting could not see the whole character. He guessed one from the half that was left.*

I stood there. Autumn sun, the smell of timber, someone hammering something a long way off.

The first thing I said was: *It's quite a reasonable guess.*

Zhou Yan laughed. He said, *It is. That's why the mistake got away with it for three hundred years — until somebody stood the two stones next to each other.*

---

I did not sleep that night.

What I lay there thinking about was not the stele. It was this: **inside an error is what the copyist could see at the time.**

That cutter could not see the whole character. There was a gap in his field. He filled the gap with a reasonable guess. And three hundred years later that guess is itself a record — a record not of what the stone originally said, but of **the shape of the gap.**

An error is a record.

What an error records is not content. It is the circumstances of the copying.

I was twenty-six and I understood nothing, but I sat up in bed, because of what came next:

**If the genome is copied, then every error in the genome records the circumstances under which it was copied.**

And the genome is copied. This is not metaphor. DNA replication is copying in the literal sense — base by base, for billions of years, across an unknown number of generations, each generation with its rate of getting it wrong. This is the first lecture in molecular biology. I had memorised it. I had been examined on it. Until that second I had never once actually thought about it.

We study the genome by reading what it **copied.**

Nobody was reading the **copying.**

---

I went to Professor He the next day.

I talked for about ten minutes, badly. He listened, and then asked me a question that in hindsight is devastating.

He said: *How will you distinguish a copying error from a mutation.*

I could not answer. I said, *aren't they the same thing.*

He said: *Not in your framework. Mutation is random and miscopying is random — but that stele of yours, that error was not random. That error was determined by **the shape of the gap.** What you are looking for is not random error. It is structured error. And evolutionary biology has spent a hundred years establishing that the errors in a genome are random.*

I said, *Then I'll establish that some of them are not.*

He sighed. He said what he always said: *You think too much.*

And then he gave me three months. *Go and do it,* he said. *If it doesn't work, come back and do your proper project.*

I did it for three months and produced nothing.

It took me thirteen years.

---

I should be precise about what the method is, because it has since been misreported in a variety of ways, the most popular being that I look for *language* in the genome.

I do not look for language. The genome is not a language. It has no semantics and nobody is speaking it. Anyone claiming to read *information* or *intent* or *a message* in DNA is, in the overwhelming majority of cases, lying, and in the small remainder, lying to themselves.

What I look for is **the traces of copying.**

Concretely. Hand someone a manuscript in a script they cannot read. Not one character. What can they know?

A great deal.

They can know whether one hand copied it, because one hand drifts systematically over hundreds of pages. They can know whether the hand changed, because at a change the spacing jumps. They can know which passages the copyist did not understand, because unfamiliar material is copied slowly, and slow copying is neater — unnaturally neat. They can know which passage was copied twice, because the error distribution across duplicated passages goes mirrored. They can know where he was interrupted, because the first line after an interruption sits a little further left.

All of that, and not one character read.

That is what I do.

I do not ask what a stretch of sequence does. I ask: how was this stretch copied. Does the distribution of its errors tell me anything about what was happening while it was being copied. Was it copied twice. Is there anywhere in it that is unnaturally neat.

Unnaturally neat. Those two words are the source of every difficulty in my life.

---

In thirteen years I published nineteen papers.

Of the nineteen, fourteen were honest work: standard methods on standard problems, in order to stay alive. Five were the thing I actually wanted to do.

The fate of the five: one landed in a very good journal, because one of the reviewers happened also to read rubbings of inscriptions. One landed in a middling journal, cited six times, four of them by me. Two were rejected more than seven times each and ended up in the kind of place nobody reads. And one I have never submitted, because I do not trust its conclusion myself.

Among those rejections, the line that hurt most was not *lacks testable predictions.* The line that hurt most was from an anonymous reviewer, and it read:

> The author is evidently a scientist of literary sensibility. This is a good thing, but it does not belong in the Results section.

I printed that out and taped it to the back of my door. It stayed there four years, until I changed offices and tore it down.

Tearing it down, I found the wall behind it was whiter than the wall around it.

---

I want to say something fair here, because if this record contains only my grievance it is not worth writing.

The reviewers were mostly right.

Almost everything my method produced in thirteen years was unfalsifiable. I look at a stretch of sequence and say *the regularity here is anomalous*, and *anomalous* requires a baseline, and the baseline was set by me. I could have gone on like that for ever, for ever seeing things, for ever unable to prove one of them. That is rhetoric, not science. I know it. I always knew it.

That is why the checksum destroyed me.

Because a checksum needs no baseline.

A checksum is a **yes or a no.**

You compute, you compare, it agrees or it does not. There is no *significance*, no *confidence interval*, no *the author suggests*. The last one thousand one hundred and eighty-three base pairs of four thousand seven hundred and thirty-two either equal the result of a definite operation over the preceding three thousand five hundred and forty-nine, or they do not.

For thirteen years I did a thing that could not be falsified.

And it handed me a result that could be.

It was not falsified.

---

That morning after the seventeenth run, when I said *all right, then* to the face in the washroom mirror, there was another sentence in me that I did not say aloud.

It was: *Professor He, I did the very hard thing.*

He died two years ago. Pancreas, quickly. I went to the memorial and did not speak; I am no good in that room.

I always thought the greatest kindness he did me was those three months. He knew perfectly well I would produce nothing, and he gave me three months anyway.

I think now the greatest kindness he did me was the question.

**How will you distinguish a copying error from a mutation.**

It took me thirteen years to answer. The answer is: a mutation is worn in by time, and a copying error is cut in by circumstance. The first is random. The second has a shape.

And if you find a shape in a stretch of something, then there were circumstances.

And if that shape is a checksum — then the circumstances were: **somebody was worried about copying it wrong.**
