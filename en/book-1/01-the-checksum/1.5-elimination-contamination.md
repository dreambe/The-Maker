# 1.5 · Elimination (i): Contamination

In my field, *contamination* is a moral word.

Technically it means something plain: nucleic acid that does not belong in this sample got into this sample. In practice, saying a result *might be contamination* is saying *you do not work clean*, and in a laboratory that carries far more force than *you did the arithmetic wrong*. Arithmetic is your head. Dirt is your hands. And this field believes, somewhere below the level of speech, that hands tell you more about a person than heads do.

So I knew on the first day that I would have to eliminate this one more thoroughly than anything else. Not because it was the most likely. Because until it was eliminated, nobody would listen to my second sentence.

It took five weeks.

---

First: under what circumstances can contamination produce a checksum.

The answer is: **it cannot.**

I knew that from the beginning, and I still spent five weeks, and that fact deserves an explanation of its own.

Contamination is addition. It adds sequence that does not belong. What is added produces three recognisable consequences: polymorphic sites where there should be none; anomalies in coverage depth; and chimeric reads at alignment — half from this sample, half foreign, not matching at the seam.

All three point at **mess.**

What I was looking at was not mess. It was an arithmetic relation holding exactly.

Contamination can make a sequence dirty. Contamination cannot make a dirtied sequence happen to equal, across its final one thousand one hundred and eighty-three positions, a definite function of the three thousand five hundred and forty-nine before it.

In probability that is not *small*. It is *does not occur*.

So for the contamination hypothesis to stand there is exactly one route: **the contaminating source itself carries the checksum.**

Which is to say: what I am seeing is real, it just does not come from this sample. It comes from somewhere else — some nucleic acid, already carrying this thing, that got into my material.

And you only have to say that hypothesis out loud to see what is wrong with it.

**It explains nothing. It moves the problem to a different address.**

If the contaminating source carries the checksum, the source requires explanation. And the sequence in that source came from a person.

That person's genome has it.

And we are back where we started.

---

I wrote those three paragraphs on paper. It took about ten minutes.

Then I spent five weeks doing experiments.

That is research. You know the answer, you derive the answer in ten minutes, and then you spend five weeks welding a leg onto every side of it, because when you come to write it up the reviewers will not read your reasoning. They will ask whether you ran a blank.

And they are right. I hate that they are right.

---

**Week one: reagents and workflow.**

I pulled the complete library-preparation records for all seventy-one modern samples. I did not generate most of that data — it comes from three public projects, and for two of them I used raw reads rather than anyone's analysis. That matters: if I had used somebody else's alignments, I might have been reproducing somebody else's error.

I reran from raw reads.

Reagent lots: seventy-one samples across eleven lots. If the contamination came from a reagent lot, samples within that lot should show features peculiar to it.

I grouped by lot and aligned.

Eleven lots. All identical.

Which is to say: if this were reagent contamination, then eleven different lots, from three different projects, spanning six years, had all been contaminated by the same thing, to precisely the same degree, at precisely the same positions.

That is not contamination. That is a miracle — and a harder miracle than the thing I was trying to establish.

At the end of week one I wrote a line in my notes: **the contamination hypothesis requires something less likely than the thing it is meant to explain.**

I struck it out the next day for sounding too much like rhetoric.

I am copying it here now, because it is correct.

---

**Weeks two and three: indices and crossover.**

Sequencing now runs many samples together; each gets a short tag called an index, and afterwards the reads are separated by index. There is a known problem here called index hopping — a very small fraction of reads are assigned to the wrong sample. It is real, it is universally acknowledged, there is literature, there are estimates of magnitude.

If what I was seeing came from index hopping, it should have one signature: **it should be related to the contents of other samples in the same run.**

So I did this. For every original sequencing run, I pulled **every** sample in it, including ones with nothing to do with me — other species, environmental samples, controls.

And I looked for that sequence in all of them.

The results fall in two groups.

In human samples it is there, and identical.

In non-human samples it is absent. Not one.

If this were index hopping, non-human samples should contain trace human reads, that sequence among them. They did contain trace human reads — this is normal; every run does. And within those trace human reads, the proportion in which that sequence appeared matched its proportion in the human genome.

Which is to say: hopping is present, hopping is at the normal level, and hopping shows no bias in content.

It had not been hopped across preferentially. It had simply been hopped across a little, like everything else in the genome.

That leg welded very cleanly. I was almost cheerful.

---

**Week four: I did a bad thing.**

I suspected a person.

The library-prep instrument is shared with another group; the rota is on the door. That group has a postdoc. I will not give his name, because this episode is entirely my problem and not his.

His project involves a panel of human cell lines. Cell lines are the most classical contamination source there is — those things have been passaged for decades, mixed up, contaminated, and their histories are filthy.

One evening in week four I looked at the instrument's usage log. And then I did the following.

I laid his sessions against my sample-processing dates.

There were three overlaps.

And then I did the following.

I logged into the shared record system and looked at what he had done on those three occasions.

That step was over a line. My permissions allowed it, the instrument being shared, but *allowed* and *should* are two different things. I knew what I was doing as I clicked.

What he had done on those three occasions was extremely ordinary work, with no intersection with anything of mine whatever. His records are meticulous — more meticulous than mine. On one occasion he had noted *centrifuge 3 making an unusual noise, maintenance requested.*

I closed the window and sat in the chair for a while.

That was the only time in those five weeks that I felt dirty.

Not because I read his records; strictly, that was permitted. Because of what was in my head at that moment. **I wanted it to be him.**

I wanted his hands to have been dirty. I wanted his cell lines to have drifted into my material. I wanted all of this to have an ordinary explanation attributable to some particular person. And if that explanation held, the price of it was that he would be in trouble.

To avoid facing a thing I was afraid of, I was willing for a man I had exchanged fewer than ten sentences with to be in trouble.

I passed him in the corridor the next day. He said *morning*. I said *morning*.

I have never told him.

I do not intend to. Telling him would be for my benefit and of no use to him whatsoever.

---

**Week five: the ancient samples.**

This is the week that welded the leg shut.

I had four: two Neanderthal, one Denisovan, and one whose provenance I am not at liberty to state.

Ancient DNA is another world entirely.

A modern lab is clean. An ancient-DNA lab is **paranoid.**

Positive-pressure clean room. Full protection. A fixed sequence from changing to entering. Dedicated, decontaminated reagents. A blank with every batch. Every operation on UV-treated surfaces. And the outer few millimetres of the bone milled away before anything else, because those millimetres have been touched by modern hands.

And the crucial point: **ancient DNA has its own fingerprint.**

Ancient DNA is broken. The fragments are very short, typically tens of bases. And it carries a characteristic damage pattern — deamination of cytosines at the ends, producing a quantifiable slope that steepens with age. That slope cannot be faked, and modern DNA cannot produce it: modern DNA is long, intact, and undamaged in that particular way.

So in ancient DNA you can **tell** which reads are ancient and which are modern contamination. It is the single most important capability this field has built in thirty years.

What I did was: use only reads carrying unambiguous ancient damage signatures, and cover that sequence with those alone.

Which is to say: I threw away everything that could possibly be modern contamination and kept only the fragments that **must** have come from that bone.

Four samples. Coverage was low in all of them — ancient DNA is like that, and more so once I had kept only damaged reads.

Neanderthal one: seventy-one per cent of the sequence covered. Every covered position identical.

Neanderthal two: forty-four per cent. All identical.

Denisovan: thirty-one per cent. All identical.

The fourth: eighty-eight per cent. All identical.

And the fourth's coverage included nine hundred and seventy-six of the final one thousand one hundred and eighty-three positions.

Nine hundred and seventy-six positions. Every one of them consistent with the checksum's expected value.

A forty-thousand-year-old bone, in a paranoid room, milled down, handled with dedicated reagents, reduced to only those fragments bearing deamination damage.

And those fragments passed a verification.

---

I wrote the elimination report on the last day of week five.

Nineteen pages. Locations of all raw data, all scripts, all batch information, damage-slope plots for the four ancient samples.

I wrote the conclusion three times. The first was too excited, the second too cautious, and the third came out like this:

> The contamination hypothesis requires a contaminating source which itself carries the checksum relation. The hypothesis therefore does not constitute an explanation of the observation but a displacement of what requires explaining. In addition, the hypothesis cannot simultaneously satisfy the following four observations: consistency across eleven reagent lots; absence of content bias in index hopping; absence in non-human samples; and consistency across four ancient samples under the condition that only deamination-damaged reads are retained.
>
> The contamination hypothesis is eliminated.

I encrypted it and put it in the directory called `the two stones`.

At that point the directory held three things: my verification results, my corrected translation of the Imhoff paper, and those nineteen pages.

Later that directory held over six hundred files.

I have always thought those three were still the important ones.

---

I did not go home that night.

I printed the four damage-slope plots and spread them on the desk and looked at them for a long time.

The four curves are almost the same shape. Four individuals, three different species or subspecies, tens of thousands of years apart, dead in different places, dug up by different people, processed in different laboratories.

Four identical curves. That is the signature time leaves in nucleic acid, and it is extremely reliable, because it is chemistry and not biology — chemistry does not care who you are.

Looking at those curves I thought: time did the same thing to all four of these bones.

And on another axis of the very same reads, there is another thing that is also identical across all four of these bones.

One of them was left by time.

The other was not.

I remember running a finger along one of the curves, from one end to the other, and then leaving my hand on the paper without moving it.

There is one sentence in my notes from that day that is not a technical record — the only one in five weeks:

**Not contamination. This room is clean. The problem is not in this room.**
