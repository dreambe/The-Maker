# 1.8 · She Tells No One

I encrypted the results on the first day.

Technically it is simple: a local encrypted volume, a long passphrase, no network, no sync, no backup to any cloud. I did not write the key down — I encoded it as a sentence, and the sentence is a particular line on a particular page of one of my mother's old lesson plans, which I memorised as a child. I keep the page number and the line number in my head.

That scheme has an obvious flaw: if I die, the directory can never be opened.

It occurred to me one day in the third month. I thought about it for perhaps ten minutes, and then what I did was: nothing.

I know now that this was not an oversight. It was a decision, and I was unwilling at the time to admit it was one.

The decision was: **if I die, this ends here.**

For those three months, some part of me wanted it to end there.

---

I will take the lies in order.

**The first lie**, to Shao Cen, day nine. She asked what the sequence was and I said it was an exercise.

A very small lie, barely one. I have never felt bad about it.

**The second lie**, to the postdoc next door who arrives at seven forty, day twenty-two. He passed the doorway, saw me, said *early.* I said *mm.*

I had been sitting there fourteen hours.

**The third lie**, to my group.

My group is three people: me, Shao Cen, and a technician, Lao Chen, fifty-one, twenty-six years in this institute — far more senior than I am, far better with the instruments, far better at surviving in this building.

Day thirty-one, Lao Chen asked why I kept booking night sessions alone.

I said I was running a methodological control comparing reproducibility across batches, and needed isolated sessions to avoid interference.

That is a well-made lie. It has technical detail, it has a legitimate reason, it does not invite follow-up.

Lao Chen looked at me and said: *ah.*

And then he said: *you look bad.*

I said it was nothing.

That was the fourth.

---

**The fifth lie** was to the ethics committee.

In week eleven I submitted an amendment so that I could legitimately access the raw data from the ancient samples. An amendment requires a statement of research purpose.

What I wrote was: *To investigate patterns of concordance in highly conserved non-coding regions of the human genome between ancient and modern samples, and their potential implications for molecular clock calibration.*

Every word of that sentence is true.

It completely, accurately, and without a single false statement, leaves out the entire point.

It is the least respectable thing I have done — less respectable than reading that postdoc's records in week four. Because reading the records was an impulse, and that sentence took me an afternoon. I revised it four times. Each revision made it cleaner.

It was approved. Three weeks. Whoever reviewed it may have spent ten minutes. They had no reason to think twice — a properly worded, reasonably motivated data-access amendment, no human intervention, no privacy risk, ought to be approved in three weeks.

The system did not fail.

The system simply has no field in which to write *I may have found that the human species was manufactured and I need to look at those bones.*

---

The insomnia I should describe concretely, because it is the only thing that changed continuously across that half-year.

**Month one:** difficulty falling asleep. One to two hours lying down before sleep comes. Then four or five hours. Able to get up and work.

At that stage I still considered myself within the normal range. Half the people I know are like that.

**Month two:** early waking. Awake after three, unable to get back. Lying there with the pipeline running in my head. I tried counting breaths. I tried those recordings of rain. I tried drinking — drinking was the most effective and the least viable, because the following day is a write-off.

**Month three:** I started going to the lab at night.

The logic of the transition is clean: if I am awake at three and cannot sleep, I may as well work. And once I was going to the lab at night, my hours came apart entirely.

By the end of month three I could not have told you which part of it was daytime.

**Month four:** the body began sending the bill.

Stomach, on the left, not sour. I wrote that in my notes on the day of the seventeenth run, and it settled afterwards into a background — not severe, not disabling, just always there.

Hands going numb at night. That is the neck; I knew it; I also knew I should replace the chair.

Dry eyes; after long enough at a screen I had to shut them for a while.

And one thing I have never written anywhere: I began forgetting things.

Not seriously. The kind where — I put the car key down and then spent ten minutes looking for it. I answered an email and then thought I had not. I bought the same milk twice at the supermarket.

One day in month four I was standing in front of the refrigerator holding the second identical carton of milk, and I sat down.

I sat on the kitchen floor with my back against the refrigerator, holding the milk, for about twenty minutes.

It was not that I was afraid I was ill. I am thirty-nine; that was sleep deprivation; I knew it was sleep deprivation.

I sat down because in that second I thought: **I am doing the thing my mother is doing.**

Except hers is being cleared, and mine I did to myself.

---

Now the Wednesday.

Month five, end of April, warm already. I went to my mother's — the same bus, the same ninety-six stairs, the same first way of opening the door: she was behind it, waiting.

She was in good condition that day. *Good* meaning she could produce three or four related sentences in a row, she knew me, she knew it was Wednesday.

We ate. She cooked. Two dishes, both over-salted. I finished all of it.

Afterwards we sat in the front room. The television was on, low, a programme about fruit from somewhere — I suspect that channel broadcasts nothing else, or my mother stops only on that.

She suddenly asked: *is work busy.*

I said: *busy.*

She said: *busy with what.*

---

I am going to write down very precisely what happened in my head over the following dozen seconds, because it is the most shameful thing in my life, and I hold that shameful things should be remembered accurately, or the shame is wasted.

In that second, I wanted to tell her.

Not the vague urge to *unburden*. A very specific, very clear, almost fully formed action: I was going to tell her all of it, five months, start to finish.

And what made that action possible was one thought.

The thought was:

**She will not remember.**

I could say it. I could say all of it. I could say the checksum, and Imhoff, and the four hundred billion tests, and pricking my own finger, and washing my hands four times in the washroom. I could say the inference I did not dare say aloud to a single colleague.

I could finish and go home.

And when I came the following Wednesday, it would not exist for her.

She would not tell anyone, because she cannot hold it.
She would not be frightened by it, because she cannot hold it.
The way she looked at me would not change, because she cannot hold it.

I could speak to a person and have the words not remain in the world.

That is not confiding. Confiding requires someone who will remember.

That is **emptying out.**

In that second, I saw my mother as a place to pour something into.

---

I did not say it.

What I said was: *a data thing. Very dull.*

She said: *ah.*

She said: *don't wear yourself out.*

I said: *mm.*

She said: *have you eaten.*

I said: *I have. Just now. You made it.*

She said: *oh, yes.*

And then she laughed a little, slightly embarrassed — the *I've forgotten again* embarrassment. She still has that embarrassment, which means part of her still knows she is forgetting.

That embarrassment is the worst of it. The forgetting is not the hard part. Forgetting and knowing you are forgetting is the hard part.

I said: *normal. I forget things constantly.*

She said: *you're not old.*

I said: *thirty-nine.*

She said: *thirty-nine.*

There was something in her voice on those two words that I cannot name. Like checking a figure. Like surprise. Like remembering something.

Then she said: *then I'm seventy-three.*

I said: *mm.*

She said: *it goes fast.*

We did not speak again that afternoon. We watched the fruit programme. Afterwards I washed up, took the rubbish down, filled her pill organiser, and left.

---

On the concrete step at the bottom of the building, I sat down.

There was sun that day.

I sat there a long time. Longer than usual.

What I thought about was not the checksum.

What I thought about was: if I had said it, what would I have got.

The answer is: an hour of relief.

An hour later I would go home, and not one thing would be smaller. And what I had done would still be entirely on my side — because she cannot hold it, so nothing I had done would have been shared. It would only have been **poured out.**

Pouring out is not sharing.

Sharing requires the other to catch it, to carry it onward, to look at me the following Wednesday and say *that thing you told me — I've been thinking about it.*

And my mother cannot catch anything any more.

On that step, for the first time, I understood one thing completely:

**My loneliness is not that there is nobody to tell. It is that the people who could understand cannot remember it for me, and the person who would remember it for me can no longer understand.**

I did not know then how exactly true that sentence would become.

I also did not know that five months later, under the ice in Antarctica, I would meet a thing that could understand and would never forget anything at all.

And that after meeting it I would find out: that is not sharing either.

That is a different and more thorough loneliness, and it needs a new name, and the one who names it is not me.
