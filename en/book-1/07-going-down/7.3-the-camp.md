# 7.3 · The Camp

At two in the afternoon on the eleventh of January we went onto the ice.

And this section is the part of those forty-six days that does not go in the report.

**Because what goes in the report is drilling progress.**

**And a person there spends most of their time not drilling.**

---

## What the place is like

Our camp was at a place called Station Seven.

That is not a base. **A base is a place with buildings.**

It is a camp: six demountable modules, a generator, a drill platform, four fuel tanks, a flagpole (the pole is not a symbol; it is a wind indicator, and a reference for finding the camp again in a whiteout).

The camp is about eighty metres across.

**And beyond those eighty metres is two thousand kilometres of white.**

---

The hardest thing to adjust to on my first day was not the cold.

**It was the absence of distance.**

On an entirely white, featureless plane you cannot judge how far away anything is.

That afternoon I saw a black dot in the distance and judged it two kilometres off.

**It was eleven metres off.**

**It was an empty drum.**

---

And that has a practical consequence, which nearly killed a man on the twenty-ninth day, and that is 7.4.

---

## Cold

I will not write *very cold.*

I will write numbers, and their consequences.

```
outside temperature    daytime -24 to -31 Celsius
                       night (sun present, low angle) -33 to -38
wind                   mean 6 m/s, gusts to 21
wind chill equivalent  at -31 with 21 m/s, about -52
```

And what a wind chill equivalent of -52 actually means:

**Exposed skin, under those conditions, takes ninety seconds to suffer frostbite.**

---

**So your face cannot be exposed.**

**So your hands cannot leave their gloves.**

Both of which look like common sense.

**And their consequences are the core of this section.**

---

## Gloves

I write about gloves, because gloves were the thing all of us hated most in those forty-six days.

A polar glove has three layers: liner (wicking), mid (insulation), shell (windproof and waterproof).

**With all three on, the diameter of your fingers is twice a bare hand's.**

And at twice the diameter, what can you do:

```
can        carry things, turn large valves, hold a rope, steady yourself walking
cannot     turn a screw, connect a wire, use a phone, write, use a keyboard,
           pick up anything smaller than two centimetres
```

And about half of all operations on a drill platform are in the second class.

---

**So every one of those operations means taking a glove off.**

**And every time you take a glove off you have ninety seconds.**

---

How we handled those ninety seconds is the most engineered thing about those forty-six days, and it was Ilan's.

**He broke every bare-handed operation into segments of no more than forty seconds.**

That list is in his notebook; I copied it later. Seventy-one items.

For instance:

```
replacing a sensor connector
   segment 1 (32 s)   right glove off · loosen old connector · glove back on
   segment 2 (recovery)  60 s, both hands inside the jacket
   segment 3 (38 s)   right glove off · fit new connector · glove back on
   segment 4 (recovery)  60 s
   segment 5 (21 s)   right glove off · tighten · glove back on
```

**An operation that takes forty seconds indoors takes four minutes and eleven seconds there.**

---

And Ilan had a name for the method.

He called it **the ration.**

**And he never explained the word.**

And I thought of it afterwards — after I had read those records.

**Because *ration* is that thing's word.**

And Ilan could not have known.

**He took it from engineering.**

---

## The lavatory

I write this because it says more about what being there is like than anything else, and it is in no expedition report.

The Environmental Protocol requires that **all waste be returned in full.**

All. Including that.

**So the camp's lavatory is an apparatus.**

Its principle: freeze the waste, seal it in drums, number them, store them, and take them out on the ship at withdrawal.

And that apparatus is in a module, and that module is not heated (heating would defeat the freezing).

**So the inside of that module is at the outside temperature.**

---

One sentence, only one:

**In a module at minus thirty-one degrees, removing the lower half of six layers of clothing takes three minutes.**

**And putting it back on takes five.**

**So one visit is eight minutes.**

**And a person goes two or three times a day.**

**So in forty-six days I spent about fourteen hours on that.**

---

And the second layer of this, which is the real one.

**Nobody complained.**

In those forty-six days, the six of us (our four, plus two technicians the voyage assigned us) — not one complained about it.

**And not because we are hardy.**

**Because in that place complaint has no object.**

In a city you complain about a thing because that thing **could have been otherwise** — somebody could have fixed it, somebody could have spent more, somebody could have designed it better.

**And there, that apparatus is what it is, because the treaty requires full return, because freezing requires no heat, because physics.**

**Complaint requires somebody to complain to.**

**And there is nobody there.**

---

## Smell

Nobody has written this, and it is what I remember most clearly.

**That camp has a smell, and it is not any smell I expected.**

It is not foul.

It is not diesel (although diesel is everywhere).

It is a **dry** smell.

---

I looked up why afterwards.

Antarctica's absolute humidity is the lowest on Earth — lower than the Sahara's. Because at those temperatures almost all the water vapour has condensed out.

And inside a module, an oil-fired heater blows constantly.

**So the relative humidity inside is about seven per cent.**

---

**And at seven per cent humidity, every organic thing is slowly dehydrating.**

Our skin was dehydrating. Our lips split. Our noses bled (every one of us had a nosebleed in the first five days).

**And those things — dry skin, cracked wooden crates, dehydrated cardboard, heated diesel, and six people forty-six days without a proper wash — together make a smell.**

And I have no word for it.

**And the nearest thing to it is a room full of old books that has not been opened for a long time.**

---

**And that smell was gone when we went down to that place under the ice.**

**There is no smell down there.**

**And it is the first thing I noticed down there, before the temperature, before the sound.**

---

## Sleep

In January the Antarctic sun does not set.

**It goes round the horizon.**

At midnight it is in the south, low, orange.

Which means: **your module is lit twenty-four hours a day.**

We had blackout blinds. They keep out most of the light.

**And there is one thing they do not keep out.**

---

That thing is why I slept worst in those forty-six days, and it is not the light.

**It is that no day ever ends.**

A person's sleep does not run on light alone. It runs on the feeling that **the day is over.**

And that feeling is built out of many small things: it got dark, the street quietened, people went home, the programmes changed, you washed up.

**And there, not one of those things happens.**

The generator sounds the same twenty-four hours a day.

The drill platform runs twenty-four hours a day (three shifts — forty-six days; we could not stop).

The sun is always there.

**So the day does not end. There is simply a moment at which you decide to sleep.**

---

And in those forty-six days I averaged four hours twenty minutes.

And that is not the worst.

**The worst was Ye Qi — and she was not on the ice.**

She was at home. And she had one call with us every day, and our operating hours were seven hours off from hers.

**So for those forty-six days she lived on our time.**

---

## Radio

Last, and the one good thing in this section.

We had two links: a satellite data link (expensive, billed hourly; our budget covered forty minutes a day) and shortwave radio (cheap, unreliable, dependent on the ionosphere).

And shortwave has a property:

**It is not directional.**

**So anybody on the right frequency can hear you.**

---

And in Antarctica a great many people are listening.

Other countries' stations, ships passing, several radio amateurs, and one man in some South American port whose identity we never knew.

And in those forty-six days something happened on our shortwave frequency.

---

On the nineteenth day a piece of equipment failed.

A readout unit for a pressure transducer. Its display died, and the spare was on the ship, and the ship had gone.

Ilan mentioned it on shortwave — not as a request, just mentioned it, because on that frequency people say what they ran into that day.

And forty minutes later somebody answered.

---

The man spoke Russian.

One of our technicians has some Russian. He translated.

The man said:

**"We have one the same. From 1991. We don't use it."**

**"Your position?"**

---

That station was a hundred and forty kilometres north-east of us.

And a hundred and forty kilometres, in Antarctica, is three days by snow tractor.

---

Ilan's reaction surprised me.

**He said no.**

He said on the radio: thank you, no, we have another way.

And he switched the set off, and turned round and said to me:

"We can't let them come."

I said, "Why."

He said:

"Because on paper our project is a depth variation on an ice-core drilling task."

"And they would see that platform."

"And the equipment on that platform is not for coring ice."

---

And in that moment I understood something.

**We were there on condition of not being seen.**

And not because anybody had ordered secrecy.

**Because ten months earlier Han Yin had filed us as a variation within scope.**

**And I signed that document.**

---

## That night

That night on the nineteenth day (*night* being what we decided it was), I sat in that module.

Ilan was fixing the readout unit — he solved it in the end with a digital multimeter and a handwritten conversion table, which the formal report calls *an alternative reading arrangement was adopted.*

And I sat there.

And I thought about the man who spoke Russian.

---

I thought: he is a hundred and forty kilometres away, in a camp like ours, in a module like ours, next to an unheated lavatory like ours.

**And he has a readout unit from 1991 that he does not use.**

**And he was willing to drive three days to bring it.**

**And we said no.**

---

I sat in that module, in that dry smell, in that unvarying generator sound, for a long time.

**And I was not thinking about secrecy, or the document, or Han Yin.**

**I was thinking: if one day we have to speak to a thing — a thing that is not a person.**

**And it is very far away, on a frequency we do not understand, and it says something.**

**And what it says means *we have one the same, and we don't use it.***

---

**Will we understand it.**

---

I sat there very late.

The sun was in the south, low, orange.

And that was not night.

**That was only the moment at which I decided to sleep.**
