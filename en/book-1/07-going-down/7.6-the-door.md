# 7.6 · The Door

Two thousand and ninety-seven metres.

The cage stopped.

---

## Why it stopped at two thousand and ninety-seven

The plan was two thousand one hundred.

And at two thousand and ninety-seven, the cable tension went **to zero.**

And tension at zero means the cage is not hanging — it is standing on something.

And why that is a profoundly abnormal thing.

The hole we drilled is two thousand one hundred metres deep.

And the bottom of the hole, by the ice-thickness figures, should be the interface between the ice and the thing beneath.

**And our drill head stopped at two thousand one hundred at twenty past one on the afternoon of day forty-one because it could go no deeper — that is the hot-water drill's limit (7.4).**

**So the bottom of the hole is water.**

**And water bears no weight.**

---

**And the cage was standing on something.**

---

What I did in that moment.

**I pressed to speak.**

I said, "Tension zero. Depth two thousand and ninety-seven."

Above (Marija) answered: "Zero confirmed. You've stopped."

I said, "We're standing on something."

She said — very level — she said:

**"That is not possible. There is water in that hole."**

I said, "I know."

She said:

"Dr Shen, listen to me. Tension zero has two possible causes. One is that you are standing on something. The other is that the cable has parted and you are in free fall, and in free fall the tension is also zero."

She said:

**"Is the depth reading still changing."**

---

I looked.

**It was not.**

Two thousand and ninety-seven point one metres, steady.

---

I said, "Not changing."

She said:

"Then you are standing on something."

Then she said the one thing in those seven hours forty that was not calm.

She said:

**"Standing on what."**

---

## That interface

And afterwards we knew what it was.

It is the top of that shaft (the *cavity* of 7.4, at one thousand eight hundred and forty metres) — no.

That is wrong.

I have to write this spatial relation carefully, because it is the hardest thing in this section to explain.

That shaft has an eleven-metre section at one thousand eight hundred and forty metres (the section we drilled through on the twenty-ninth day).

**And that shaft continues downward from one thousand eight hundred and forty metres.**

**All the way to that thing.**

And our hole was drilled from above, to two thousand one hundred metres.

And the two met at one thousand eight hundred and forty.

---

**So from one thousand eight hundred and forty metres downward, our drill head was not in its own hole.**

**It was in that shaft.**

---

And we measured that shaft's diameter later:

```
0.51 m
```

**And our bore is 0.5 m.**

**And our cage's outer diameter is 0.44 m.**

---

I want to stop here, because this figure is the one in this whole book I least want to think about.

**Zero point five one metres.**

**And our drill head is a zero point zero three metre nozzle making a zero point five metre hole.**

**And that shaft is zero point five one.**

**A difference of zero point zero one.**

---

**So at one thousand eight hundred and forty metres, our head passed from a zero point five metre hole into a zero point five one metre shaft.**

**And it did not touch the wall.**

**And it kept drilling for another two hundred and sixty metres.**

**And it was not drilling ice.**

**It was spraying two hundred and sixty metres of hot water inside a shaft that already existed.**

---

Which means:

**Those two hundred and fifty-seven metres from one thousand eight hundred and forty to two thousand and ninety-seven did not need drilling.**

**And we drilled them.**

**And we spent those ten days widening eleven metres because we thought it was a cavity requiring treatment.**

---

**And those ten days we added to ourselves.**

---

## That thing

And what the cage was standing on is the bottom of that shaft.

And that bottom is not ice.

How we knew, because Ilan did it, and it took eleven minutes.

---

The cage's floor opens.

And below the opened floor is that thing.

And Ilan opened it.

And when he opened it my back was to him, and I could not see.

**And I heard it.**

---

That sound, because it is the first sound at two thousand metres down that we did not make ourselves.

The floor is hydraulic, and it makes a soft noise opening.

**And after that noise there was another sound.**

The sound was:

**"Tak."**

One.

Short.

**Crisp.**

---

I said, "What is that."

Ilan did not answer.

I said again, "Ilan."

He said:

**"Wait."**

---

Eleven minutes.

In those eleven minutes he did three things, and he did them without speaking.

And in those eleven minutes I stood with my back to him in a vessel zero point four four metres wide, listening.

---

## Those three things

I saw his record afterwards (he used a camera fixed to the cage wall, and that camera became the most important file the Tracing Back has).

---

**One: he struck it with a probe.**

The probe is standard equipment: zero point eight metres, steel, a ball at the tip.

And he struck.

And that *tak* is the ball meeting that thing.

**And he struck eleven times.**

At a different place each time — he was probing the uniformity of the surface.

---

And the result of those strikes, because it is the only datum we had in that moment.

The eleven sounds were **identical.**

Same pitch, same decay, same ring.

**And that is not possible.**

Why not.

Strike ice at eleven different places and the sound differs — because ice has bubbles, fissures, uneven density.

**And metal, if it is uniform enough, sounds the same.**

**And nothing in nature has identical acoustic response at eleven points on a zero point five metre disc.**

---

**Two: he measured the temperature.**

And this is the most important thing of those eleven minutes.

Our hole is full of water.

**And water at that depth is at zero point one degrees** (pressure lowers the freezing point, and meltwater sits near it).

**And that surface is at:**

```
four point one degrees
```

---

`Four point one degrees.`

**Four degrees above.**

And four degrees, in that place, is an enormous number.

Because a body four degrees above its surroundings, in that environment, melts the ice around it continuously.

**And it has been there three hundred thousand years.**

**And the ice around it has not been melted through — because its meltwater rises, and above is two thousand metres of ice, and the water refreezes on the way.**

**So it sits in a stable, balanced jacket of water of its own making.**

---

**And it is maintaining those four degrees.**

**Three hundred thousand years.**

---

**Three: he found the thing.**

And this took the last seven of those eleven minutes.

He worked the probe across that surface, from the centre outward, circle by circle.

**And at zero point one nine metres from the centre, the probe went down.**

---

## That is not a door

At the end of this section, this, and it is the whole of this section.

What the probe went into is an opening.

And that opening's shape, measured later:

```
shape        circular
diameter     0.41 m
edge         sharp · unchamfered · unworn
```

---

And our cage's outer diameter is zero point four four.

**So the cage cannot go in.**

---

**And our bodies can.**

---

What *that is not a door* means, exactly.

---

A door has three characteristics.

**One: it can be shut.** A door has a leaf, or a cover, or something that can close the opening.

**And that opening has none.**

We probed it. We photographed it. We saw its far side later, within those forty-one hours.

**It has no cover. It never had one. Its edge has no hinge, no seat, no sealing face, and no wear.**

**So it is not a door.**

---

**Two: a door has a direction.** A door exists to let something pass from one side to the other, and usually that direction is preferred — outward-opening or inward-opening.

**And that opening is a pure circular hole, symmetrical on both sides.**

---

**Three: a door exists to control passage.** A door's function is: sometimes let through, sometimes not.

**And that opening has been open for three hundred thousand years.**

---

**So it is not a door.**

**It is an entrance.**

And the difference between an entrance and a door I did not think of then; I thought of it later.

And the difference is:

**A door exists to make passage controllable.**

**An entrance exists to make passage possible.**

---

**And that opening is possible.**

**And it has been possible all along.**

**Three hundred thousand years, every day, every hour.**

---

## That minute

Having found the opening, Ilan drew the probe back.

Then he spoke.

And what he said first was not about the opening.

He said:

**"He."**

That is the first time he called me that. (Before, it was *Dr Shen*, or nothing at all.)

I said, "Mm."

He said:

**"It's open."**

I said, "I heard."

He said:

**"It's been open all along."**

---

Then he said the heaviest sentence I heard in the whole of the Tracing Back.

He said:

"There's a thing I've been computing for seven years."

"What I was computing was: if it's there, why hasn't it come out."

He said:

**"And I had the question wrong."**

---

I said, "Meaning."

He said:

**"It isn't that it can't come out."**

**"It's that it hasn't."**

---

**"And it left the door open."**

---

## That number

And one number at the end of this section, which we did not know then and knew after those forty-one hours.

That opening's diameter: zero point four one metres.

And the smallest circular aperture an adult in necessary equipment can pass through is zero point three eight metres (a standardised figure, used in designing manholes for industrial plant).

**And zero point four one is that figure plus zero point zero three.**

---

**And zero point zero three metres is our cage's single-side clearance in the bore (7.5).**

---

**Coincidence.**

---

**And when I was forty-one, down there, I asked that thing about this.**

**And it answered.**

**And its answer is in Chapter Eight.**
