# 7.2 · Going South

Boarded the third of January.

Arrived the eleventh.

Eight days.

**This section is those eight days, and in it I am not presentable.**

---

## The ship

An icebreaker, launched 1993, a hundred and sixty-seven metres, twenty-one thousand tonnes displacement.

It has a property I did not know before I boarded.

**An icebreaker's hull is round underneath.**

An ordinary cargo ship has a keel and stabiliser fins, and it is steady at sea.

**And an icebreaker cannot have those — a keel gets caught in ice, and fins get shaved off by it.**

So its underside is an arc.

**So in an ice-free sea it is a log floating in water.**

---

And between where we boarded and Antarctica there is a stretch of sea.

That stretch has a name, and I did not know its weight in that trade before I boarded.

**The Drake Passage.**

---

## That sea

What Ilan said before we boarded, because it is the only warning in this section and I did not understand it.

He said, "Do you get seasick."

I said, "I don't know. I've never been on a ship."

He said, "Ah."

Then he said:

"You'll find out."

---

The Drake Passage is not *a place where the seas are rather big.*

Around the Antarctic continent there is a current: the Antarctic Circumpolar Current.

It is the strongest current on Earth. Its flow is more than a hundred times that of all the world's rivers together.

And it runs round Antarctica with no land in its way.

**Except at one place.**

Between the southern tip of South America and the northern tip of the Antarctic Peninsula there is a gap.

That gap is about eight hundred kilometres wide.

**And that current has to squeeze through it.**

---

**So the water there is squeezed water.**

And what happens to water when it is squeezed, I learned in those eight days with my body.

---

## Day one

Day one was good.

We sailed in the afternoon, clear weather, a flat sea.

I was on deck a long time. It was the first time I had seen that kind of sea — not the sea from a shore, but the sea with no shore.

And one thing about that, because it bears on everything after:

**A sea with no shore is a different colour.**

Not blue. A very deep, nearly blackened grey-blue. And it has no glare — or rather, its glare is not on the surface; it is somewhere underneath.

I stood there an hour.

Ilan came and stood beside me.

He said, "Remember today."

I said, "Why."

He said:

"Because for the next three days you won't remember anything."

---

## Day two

At three in the afternoon on the second day we entered the gap.

**And how it began has to be exact, because it was not gradual.**

At midday the sea was manageable. There was swell, and the ship moved, and that movement had a rhythm — up, down, up, down. You could predict it.

And at about three, the rhythm stopped.

---

**It became a motion with no period.**

The ship would lean one way, and hold there a second or two, and come back, and lean further the other way, and then drop.

And *drop* has to be explained: a ship of twenty-one thousand tonnes can descend four metres in one second.

**And you are inside it.**

---

I vomited first at ten past four.

In my cabin. I know the time because I was looking at the time on my phone — I was working out how long was left.

---

I am going to write those three days, and write them unpresentably, because that is what they were.

**I will not write *I felt unwell.***

**I will write what happened.**

---

## Those three days

**First: I vomited forty-one times.**

I counted. Not because I wanted to.

**Because counting was the only structured thing I could do in those three days.**

I could not read, could not write, could not hold any thought longer than two sentences.

**And I could count.**

So I counted. Forty-one.

(I noticed the number afterwards, and I want to be clear: it is a coincidence. What I counted was forty-one. I considered changing it here to something else, because forty-one appears too often in this record. And I did not change it, because it was forty-one.)

---

**Second: after the second day I had nothing left to bring up.**

And a person with nothing left to bring up still brings it up.

That is called retching.

And it is much worse, because it has no terminus — vomiting has one; when you have finished you get a little while.

**And retching does not.**

---

**Third: I injured myself.**

Not by falling.

**A tooth.**

Retching tightens the jaw involuntarily. And in one of those tightenings I bit down on something — I do not know what; perhaps my own tongue, perhaps nothing.

And on the third morning I found a crack in my lower right second molar.

That tooth was extracted after I got back.

**And it is in that envelope now.**

---

I want to stop and say this.

That envelope holds: Lu Ting's page, the table of eight, the sheet with nine identifiers, Pei Yan's sheet, the six-item list.

**And a tooth.**

Wrapped in a scrap of paper.

I do not know why I kept it.

And I think I do — **because it is the first price my body paid for this, and it is one you can hold in your hand.**

---

**Fourth: on the second night I wanted to give up.**

I want that clear, because it is true and it will not appear in any report.

The second night, about two in the morning. In my bunk. The ship in that periodless motion. I had vomited some twenty-odd times.

**And at that point I thought, very clearly and very specifically, one thing:**

**I thought: when we arrive, I could say I am not physically up to it, and stay at the base, and not go down the hole.**

**Nobody would hold it against me.**

**Ilan goes down. Seven years; he should. Marcus goes down — he is young.**

**And I could sit in a room at the base, in a chair that does not move, and wait for them.**

---

In those two hours I worked that plan out completely.

**I worked out the wording I would use.**

**I worked out whom I would say it to.**

**I even worked out how I would write that passage in the report.**

---

And I did not do it.

And why I did not has nothing to do with courage.

---

**Not because I overcame anything.**

**Because on the third morning the sea flattened.**

That is all.

We were through the gap. The ship came into the far side of the current, where part of the water is covered by ice and the swell is far smaller.

And about two hours after the sea flattened, I sat up.

And having sat up, I drank half a glass of water, and it stayed down.

**And at that point the plan from the night before no longer existed.**

---

I write this because it is the least creditable lesson of the whole business:

**I had thought a person's resolve was a decision.**

**And on that ship I learned that part of a person's resolve is their stomach.**

**And the stomach has to do with the sea.**

**And the sea has to do with a gap eight hundred kilometres wide.**

---

## Day four

On the fourth day I came out of the cabin.

The first thing I saw is among the most beautiful things I have seen in my life, and it has nothing to do with anything in this book.

**I saw sea ice.**

Not icebergs. Sea ice — the kind the sea makes itself, in great plates, with lanes of water between them.

And it is white.

And that white is not the white of snow.

**The white of sea ice has thickness.**

You look at the edge of a plate and you can see how thick it is — thirty centimetres, fifty — and the cross-section of that thickness is blue.

**A very clean, almost electric blue.**

---

I stood on deck about two hours.

And in those two hours I did one thing.

**I took my hood down.**

Not the six-layer suit (that was still in its case). The hood of my ordinary shell.

**I took it down because I wanted to listen.**

And what I heard was:

plates of sea ice grinding against one another.

Very quiet, very continuous, very low.

**It is like rain a long way off.**

---

And in that moment I remembered something.

**I remembered the evening I finished those thirty-two thousand words in that eighteen-square-metre room, and cooked a bowl of noodles.**

**And stood by the pan waiting for the water.**

**And just before water boils there is a small dense noise at the bottom of it.**

**Like rain a long way off.**

---

On the sea in Antarctica I heard the same sound.

And I knew perfectly well in that moment that the two have no physical relation at all. One is bubbles nucleating in water; the other is tens of millions of tonnes of ice grinding against itself.

**And in my ear they are the same.**

---

## Day eight

At nine forty on the morning of the eleventh of January we saw the land.

My first reaction surprised me.

**I did not find it majestic.**

**I found it low.**

I had always thought Antarctica was a high, white, towering thing.

**And from the sea it is a very low, very long white line, almost against the water.**

It is not high. It simply **has no end.**

Look left: white line. Look right: white line.

**And it exceeds your field of view in both directions.**

---

Ilan stood beside me.

He looked at that white line.

He looked at it a long time without speaking.

And what he said first, because it was the first time in seven years he had seen this place, and what he said was nothing about this place at all.

He said:

**"It's under there."**

I said, "Mm."

He said:

**"Two thousand one hundred metres."**

I said, "Mm."

He said:

**"I was thirty-nine when I first computed that hot spot."**

He did not finish the sentence.

And he did not need to.

---

One last thing.

In those eight days, Marcus was not seasick.

**Not once.**

Through those three days, on that ship, in that periodless motion, at a small table fixed to a wall, he worked about thirty hours.

When I came out on the fourth day I asked him, "Why aren't you sick."

He said, "I don't know."

I said, "What are you doing."

He said:

"Computing the surplus."

---

**The *surplus* is item forty-one on the whiteboard.**

**The part where the information exceeds what a signature requires.**

**He computed it for thirty hours in the Drake Passage on a ship with a round bottom.**

---

And he told me afterwards that those three days were the most productive of his life.

I asked why.

He said:

**"Because the ship was moving, so I couldn't get up and walk."**

**"I walk when I'm thinking."**

**"Those three days I couldn't walk, so I had to finish thinking."**
