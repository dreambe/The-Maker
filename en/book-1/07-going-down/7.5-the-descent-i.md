# 7.5 · The Descent (I)

Day forty-one, twenty past one in the afternoon.

The hole reached two thousand one hundred metres.

And at eight the next morning we went down.

**This section is the first half of the descent.**

---

## The cage

What we went down in is not a lift.

It is a cage.

I write it out, because I was inside it for three hours forty.

```
shape          cylinder
outer diameter 0.44 m (bore 0.5 m, clearance 0.03 m per side)
height         2.1 m
capacity       2 persons (standing; cannot sit, cannot turn round)
wall           three layers: steel outer, insulating middle, nothing inner
floor          openable (swings down once at depth)
top            an aperture for the cable and lines
cable          0.019 m diameter
lines          power, communication, air supply, exhaust: 4
```

**Outer diameter zero point four four metres.**

Which means: two people in it stand back to back.

**And for three hours forty you cannot turn round.**

---

## Who goes down

The list was decided thus.

Six of us. Two places.

**And we argued for two days over those two places.**

---

I write those two days down, because they are not presentable, and they are true.

**Everyone wanted to go.**

And every reason given was technical, and every technical reason held.

---

**Ilan:** seven years. He found the hot spot. He designed the hole. He is the only one with subglacial experience (once in Greenland, a hundred and forty metres).

**The reason holds.**

**Marcus:** he brought the sequencer. And if there is anything down there that can be sequenced, only he can operate that machine. And it cannot be used later up top — transporting a sample from tens of degrees below zero to room temperature would destroy it.

**The reason holds.**

**Me:** I am the lead. And that thing, if it is there, if it responds to anything — then what it responds to should be the person who asked that question.

**And I want to be clear: that is not a technical reason.**

**That is a reason I invented.**

**And I used it to argue for two days.**

---

**The two technicians:** Zhou Zhen, and Marija (assigned by the voyage; Serbian, forty-four, nineteen years of polar drilling).

And their reason is the strongest.

Marija said:

"The three of you: one palaeontology — sorry, glacial archaeology; one mathematics; one genetics."

"And if there is a void down there, its roof is ice."

"And ice falls."

She said:

**"I have done this nineteen years. I know when to run upward."**

**"None of you three does."**

---

**Hers is the strongest of the five reasons.**

**And she did not go down.**

---

Why, because it is the conclusion of those two days, and it was made by Han Yin four thousand kilometres away.

---

That undertaking (7.1).

Marija and Zhou Zhen **had not signed.**

Because they are not on the list — they are technical support assigned by the voyage, and it is not in their contracts.

**So they cannot have access to controlled information.**

**And everything in that void is controlled information.**

---

I put it to Han Yin in the satellite window.

I said: Marija has nineteen years. The three of us have a hundred and forty metres between us.

I said: if that thing collapses down there, all three of us die, and she would not.

And in that forty-minute window Han Yin was silent for about twenty seconds.

**Then she said one thing.**

She said:

"Dr Shen, I will give you two answers."

"The first is: no. That is the rule. She has not signed, so she does not go."

She said:

"The second is: **you may let her go, and I will handle it at this end.**"

"And the way I handle it is to file this descent as an equipment fault investigation, with her as the investigating technician and the three of you accompanying."

"And if there really is something down there — then that report is a false report."

"And that false report becomes the first attackable point in the whole Tracing Back."

---

She said:

"You choose."

---

## What I chose

I chose not to let her go.

And I write her reaction, because it is the worst minute of those forty-six days.

---

I told her outside that unheated module. Evening (the evening we had decided on).

It took about thirty seconds.

When I had finished she nodded once.

Then she said:

"Okay."

Then she said something in English, and I do not know whether it is a Serbian idiom:

**"It's your rope."**

I said, "Meaning."

She thought a moment and said it in Chinese:

**"That is your rope."**

---

She said:

"Nineteen years. I have paid out rope for people perhaps two hundred times."

"And every time, the hands at the top of the rope were not mine."

She said:

**"Today the hands at the top are mine."**

She said:

"So while you are down there, I am up here."

"And I will watch that tension reading for three hours forty without blinking."

---

**And she did.**

That day she stood by the winch for seven hours forty (three forty down, plus our time below, plus the ascent).

**She did not hand over.**

And there was a handover arranged — Zhou Zhen was to relieve her after two hours.

**And she did not let him.**

---

## The two who went down

Ilan and me.

Marcus did not go down.

And he withdrew himself.

---

I write the manner of it, because it is the most characteristic thing Marcus does in this book.

On the second afternoon of those two days he came into the module and said three things.

First: "I've done the arithmetic."

Second: "I can use the sequencer up here."

Third: "As long as the sample is kept below freezing."

Then he said a fourth thing, and it is not a reason:

**"And I can't be in there for three hours forty."**

---

I said, "Meaning."

He said:

"Zero point four four metres of diameter. Back to back. Cannot turn round. Three hours forty."

He said:

**"I can't."**

**"I know I can't, because I tried when I was a child."**

He did not say what he tried when he was a child.

**And he did not explain.**

**And having said it, he left.**

---

And one thing here, because it is the reason my view of Marcus changed in that moment.

**He was not afraid.**

**He had computed.**

And what he computed was: if he starts failing at the second hour down there, the whole operation aborts, so the hole is wasted, so the season is over.

**And he treated his own risk as a parameter and computed it.**

**And the answer came out: do not put him in.**

**And he said the answer out loud.**

---

In the three years after I saw a great many people overestimate themselves at moments like that.

**And I have seen exactly one person compute himself and say he could not.**

---

## The descent

Eight in the morning. Into the cage.

Six layers. Hoods. Back to back.

Me above, Ilan below (there is a step in the cage, so that two heads are at different heights, for speech).

The floor closed.

The cable above tightened.

**Descent.**

---

## The first four hundred metres

The first four hundred metres at three metres a minute.

Slow. And it must be slow — because that cable is two thousand one hundred metres long, and a steel cable that long has enough elasticity to set the cage oscillating longitudinally as it descends.

And oscillation in a bore that narrow is fatal.

**So three metres a minute.**

**Two thousand one hundred metres takes seven hundred minutes.**

**Eleven hours forty.**

---

**And we had forty-one hours.**

And those forty-one hours are the closure time of the hole (7.1).

And they run from the moment we stopped the heat.

And we stopped the heat at twenty past one in the afternoon of day forty-one.

**So our forty-one hours began then.**

And when we entered the cage at eight the next morning, eighteen hours forty had gone.

---

**So we had twenty-two hours twenty left.**

**And going down takes eleven forty.**

**And coming up takes eleven forty.**

---

**Twenty-three hours twenty together.**

---

**One hour more than we had.**

---

## That hour

I write this hour because it is the one place in this whole business where we did something in plain breach of the safety procedure, knowing what we were doing.

That extra hour had three solutions.

**One: do not go down.** Wait for next season.

And next season is a year away. And by then that hole is solid ice. And redrilling takes forty-one days, and the money covers one attempt.

**So the first solution is abandonment.**

**Two: raise the ascent speed.**

The ascent can be faster than the descent — because on the way up the cable is in tension and its oscillation modes differ.

Marija had computed it: the ascent can be done at four and a half metres a minute.

**That makes the ascent seven hours forty.**

**Nineteen hours twenty in total.**

**Three hours to spare.**

**And the risk of the second solution, in Marija's numbers:**

```
ascent at 4.5 m/min
   probability the cage touches the wall        0.11
   if it touches, probability of cable damage   0.41
   if damaged, probability of parting           0.07
```

**Total risk: about 0.003.**

**Three in a thousand.**

---

**Three: spend less time below.**

We had planned six hours below.

And two hours below gives twenty-five hours twenty in total.

**Which is also not enough.**

---

We took the second.

And how we took it took four minutes.

Ilan said, "Three in a thousand."

Marija said, "Three in a thousand."

I said, "Then three in a thousand."

Then Marija said one thing:

"Dr Shen. Three in a thousand is per ascent."

"And in nineteen years I have paid out rope two hundred times."

**"At three in a thousand, I should have parted a cable zero point six times."**

She said:

**"And I have never parted one."**

She said:

"So that three in a thousand is a thing that does not happen, until it happens."

---

**And we went down.**

---

## Ears

At a hundred and forty metres my ears began to hurt.

Why, because it is not what I expected.

A hole, two thousand one hundred metres, full of water.

**And a water column has pressure.**

And our cage is not sealed. It is an open cage — the floor closes, and the side wall does not seal (a sealed thing at two thousand metres would be crushed).

**So the pressure inside the cage equals the water pressure outside.**

And the water pressure at two thousand one hundred metres is two hundred and ten atmospheres.

---

**And we are not in the water.**

I want that clear, because this is the cleverest part of the design.

The cage is filled with pressurised air.

And that pressure, controlled from above through the air line, is matched to our depth.

**So in the cage we are breathing air at two hundred and ten atmospheres.**

**No.**

**If we were, we would be dead.**

---

The real arrangement is this:

**The cage holds one atmosphere inside.**

And its wall carries two hundred and ten atmospheres from outside.

And that requires a very thick, very heavy wall.

And at that diameter, that thickness is zero point zero four one metres of steel.

**So our cage is a pressure vessel.**

**And it weighs six hundred and eighty kilograms.**

---

And why my ears hurt.

**Because that vessel is not perfect.**

A welded pressure vessel, as depth increases, **decreases slightly in internal volume** (steel compresses under load).

And a decrease in volume raises the internal pressure.

And at two thousand one hundred metres, that rise is:

```
internal pressure     1.00 → 1.11 atmospheres
```

**Eleven per cent.**

**And a human ear responds to an eleven per cent pressure change.**

---

And the response is: the eardrum draws inward.

And it hurts.

And the remedy is to swallow, or to pinch the nose and blow.

**And in those three hours forty I did that about four hundred times.**

---

## Sweat in the gloves

I write this because it is the only bodily detail I remember from those three hours forty.

In the cage we cannot move.

And not moving means we produce no heat.

And producing no heat means we get cold.

**So the cage is heated.**

And the heating power is calculated for *one motionless person maintaining body temperature in an environment at zero degrees.*

**And that calculation is wrong.**

Because whoever made it used an ambient temperature of zero degrees.

**And outside the cage is water, and water's thermal conductivity is twenty-five times air's.**

**So the actual heat loss is more than three times the design value.**

---

So the heater runs at full power.

And a heater at full power, in a vessel zero point four four metres across and two point one metres high.

**The temperature in that vessel, at a thousand metres down, was twenty-nine degrees.**

---

**So we stood for three hours forty in six layers of polar clothing, in a twenty-nine-degree, zero point four four metre wide vessel we could not turn round in.**

---

And at about twelve hundred metres I noticed there was water in my gloves.

And my first thought was: **seepage. The wall has failed. We are going to die.**

**And it was not water.**

**It was my own sweat.**

**And it had pooled in the fingertips, about two millilitres.**

---

I write what I did in that moment, because it is the only time I spoke in those three hours forty (apart from the routine depth confirmations).

I said, "Ilan."

He said, "Mm."

I said, "There's water in my gloves."

He said — with his back to me, so I could not see him — he said:

**"Mine too."**

Then he said:

**"An hour ago."**

I said, "Why didn't you say."

He said:

**"Because you'd think it was seepage."**

---

## Counting breaths

And at the end of this section, that.

At about sixteen hundred metres I began counting my breaths.

And why I counted is the same thing as counting vomits in the Drake Passage (7.2).

**Because counting is the only structured thing available at times like that.**

---

And I remember the number I reached.

**Because I stopped at a certain number.**

That number is:

**Four thousand one hundred.**

---

I stopped at it.

And I stopped not because of the number.

**Because I realised that I had been counting for about fifty minutes, and I did not remember the beginning.**

**I did not remember whether I had started at one, or at some number in the middle.**

**So that four thousand one hundred might not be four thousand one hundred.**

---

**And that is the only time in that cage I was frightened.**

Not by the depth. Not by the three in a thousand. Not by whatever was below.

**By finding that I had miscounted, and not knowing where.**

---

And what came into my head in that moment is a line I wrote at 1.9.

It is:

**"My memory carries no checksum."**

---

And there I was, in a two-thousand-metre column of water, in a zero point four four metre pressure vessel, in six layers, with two millilitres of my own sweat in my gloves, my ears aching, my back to a man I could not see.

**And what I thought was: if I read out everything in this cage right now, I could not read out that I had miscounted.**

**Because I have nothing to check against.**

---

**And the thing waiting below does.**

**It has a checksum.**

**It wrote it three hundred thousand years ago.**

**And it wrote it precisely so that something could check whether it had gone wrong.**

---

Depth reading: one thousand nine hundred and forty metres.

A hundred and sixty to go.

Fifty-three minutes.
