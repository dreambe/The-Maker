# 7.4 · The Borehole

Forty-one days.

Forty-one days to get through two thousand one hundred metres.

**And the plan was thirty-one.**

And all ten extra days came out of the twenty-ninth.

---

## What that hole is

First, plainly, because *borehole* makes people think of oil drilling.

**It is not a hole.**

What a hot-water drill makes is a vertical column of water in ice.

Its arrangement:

```
top              drill platform, a hot-water boiler, a winch, a hose
hose             0.05 m diameter, longer than the hole is deep
drill head       a nozzle at the hose end, 0.03 m diameter
method           spray water at 85 Celsius, melting downward
rate of descent  about 20 m/hour (in ice at -28)
bore             about 0.5 m
```

**And that hole is full of water.**

Not empty. Meltwater, in the hole, because it has nowhere to go — below it is ice, above it is the hose.

**So our drill head travels down inside a two-thousand-metre column of water.**

---

## That number

And here is the central technical problem, and it determines everything.

**Meltwater is heavier than ice.**

Ice is 0.917; water is 1.000.

**So meltwater sinks.**

And when it sinks, it lands beneath the drill head.

**And the drill head works by spraying hot water to melt ice.**

**So if there is a layer of cold water beneath the head, the hot water heats that layer first and reaches the ice afterwards.**

---

So that hole **stops** at some depth.

And that depth is a computable quantity.

It depends on boiler power, water temperature, heat loss in the hose, ice temperature, and bore.

And Ilan had computed ours:

```
theoretical maximum depth    2 410 m
target depth                 2 100 m
margin                       310 m
```

**Three hundred and ten metres of margin.**

**And that margin is the whole of our guarantee of getting there.**

---

## The first twenty-eight days

The first twenty-eight went well.

I will not write them out, because they are repetition: three shifts, twenty-four hours, the winch paying out hose, the boiler heating water, twenty metres an hour.

Three things from them.

---

**One: the sound.**

A hot-water drill at work makes a sound.

That sound comes out of the hole — hot water on ice, ice melting, meltwater moving, bubbles nucleating and rising.

And all of that together travels up a two-thousand-metre column of water, through the platform's steelwork, and into your ear.

**It is low, continuous, with a very slight rise and fall.**

And in those forty-one days I listened to it on every shift.

**And on the nineteenth day I realised what I was listening to.**

---

**It is like rain a long way off.**

---

The third time.

The first was that eighteen-square-metre room and a pan about to boil.

The second was the ship, plates of sea ice grinding.

The third is here, two thousand metres of ice melting.

**And the three have no physical relation.**

**One is bubble nucleation, one is friction between solids, one is turbulence at a phase boundary.**

**And in my ear they are the same.**

---

I wrote a line in the shift log on the nineteenth day.

It is not a technical entry, and I wrote it anyway.

It reads:

> Third time.

**Nobody ever asked what it meant.**

---

**Two: Marcus's sequencer.**

That sequencer was in a case, in an unheated module, and was not opened for forty-one days.

And Marcus checked it every three days.

What he checked was the battery — batteries in that class of instrument fail irreversibly in the cold.

**So every three days he took the battery out, carried it to a heated module, charged it fully, and carried it back.**

Forty-one days, fourteen times.

**And nobody asked why.**

And on the thirty-fourth day I asked.

I said, "You're still charging that thing."

He said, "Mm."

I said, "We haven't got there. We don't know what's down there."

He said:

"Which is why I keep it full."

He said:

"Because if there is something down there that can be sequenced, I will not get a second chance."

---

**Three: Ye Qi's calls.**

One a day, a forty-minute satellite window.

And of those forty minutes, only the first five were work.

For the other thirty-five, she **read to us.**

---

I want this clear, because it is the one thing in those forty-one days we had not planned.

The first time was the fourth day. She finished the work and there were thirty-five minutes left, and we had nothing else to say.

And she said, "Shall I read you something."

I said, "Read what."

She said, "Anything. There's a book here."

**She read a book about urban plants.**

The species used as street trees, the soil of verges, a grass that grows in the cracks of walls.

She read for thirty-five minutes.

---

And from that day, thirty-five minutes daily.

Forty-one days.

She finished that book and read one about rivers, one about bread, and half of one about nineteenth-century railways.

---

And one thing here, because it is what I was most grateful for in those forty-one days, and I never told her.

**Not one of those books had anything to do with our project.**

**Not one had anything to do with origins, evolution, genetics, Antarctica, or ice.**

**She did it on purpose.**

And I know she did, because on the twenty-seventh day I heard the sound of pages stop at her end, and she said, "I'll skip this bit."

And the bit she skipped, I looked up afterwards.

**It is about how a plant re-spread northward after an ice age.**

---

## The twenty-ninth day

On the twenty-ninth day we were at one thousand eight hundred and forty metres.

At six that morning I was on shift. Ilan was asleep. Marcus was doing his arithmetic.

And at eleven minutes past six a thing happened.

---

**The winch tension reading dropped suddenly.**

What that means.

The hose hangs from the winch in a two-thousand-metre column of water, and there is a tension transducer on the winch.

That tension equals the hose's weight minus the water's buoyancy, plus the resistance on the descending head.

**In normal working it is a stable number.**

And at eleven minutes past six it fell by fourteen per cent.

---

I want to write what I did in those seven seconds, because they are the most correct seven seconds of my life, and I got them right not because I am clever.

**Because on the first day Ilan made me memorise a table.**

That table is called the anomaly response table, and it has eleven entries. Its third:

```
tension drop > 10%
   possible causes (in order of probability)
      one    head detached
      two    hose parted
      three  transducer failure
      four   head has entered a cavity
   immediate actions
      stop paying out
      stop the heat
      do not haul up
   reason (for the third action)
      if a cavity, hauling up jams the head against the cavity roof
```

---

**I did those three.**

I stopped paying out.

I shut off the hot water.

**I did not haul up.**

Elapsed: seven seconds.

---

Then I went to wake Ilan.

His time to the platform: one minute forty.

**And putting on six layers takes eleven minutes.**

**So he did not.**

He came in a liner and a shell, boots on bare feet, no hat.

**Outside temperature minus thirty-one.**

**He was on that platform nineteen minutes.**

---

## Those nineteen minutes

I will not write everything he did, because it is technical and I did not follow all of it.

Three things I did follow.

---

**One: the first thing he looked at was not an instrument.**

On reaching the platform, the first thing he did was lie down and put his ear against the hose.

He listened about fifteen seconds.

Then he said, "It isn't parted."

I said, "How do you know."

He said:

"A parted hose lets water out at the break, and the sound is diffuse."

"This is focused."

---

**Two: he computed a number.**

He took a pen out of his pocket (the only thing he had brought up) and wrote three numbers on the back of his glove.

The percentage drop in tension, the depth, and the hose's linear density.

He worked for about forty seconds.

Then he said a number:

**"Eleven metres."**

I said, "Eleven metres of what."

He said:

"The height of that cavity. At least eleven metres."

---

**Three: his decision.**

His decision was: **keep paying out.**

Not haul up. Not stop and wait.

**Keep going down.**

And the reason he gave is the coldest sentence I heard in those forty-one days.

He said:

"An eleven-metre cavity, in ice, at one thousand eight hundred and forty metres."

"And ice is plastic — at that depth, at that pressure, a cavity closes in a few hundred years."

He said:

"So it was not formed a few hundred years ago."

He said:

**"So it is being maintained."**

---

I said, "Then we should be more careful."

He said:

"No."

He said:

**"Then we must not stop."**

"Because if it is being maintained, then whatever maintains it knows we are here."

"And if we stop here, we are a pipe jammed in its roof."

---

I want to write my reaction, because it is not presentable.

**I told him he was out of his fucking mind.**

And I have never said anything like that at work in my life.

And he did not answer.

He simply reached out and pressed *pay out.*

---

## Those ten metres

The winch turned again.

And this time he set the rate to its slowest: **four metres an hour.**

And we descended ten metres.

Elapsed: two hours thirty.

And in those two and a half hours all six of us were on the platform.

And nobody spoke.

---

After ten metres the tension came back.

Not to its old value — to its old value plus three per cent.

And Ilan looked at that reading a long time.

Then he said:

"Through."

He said:

**"There's ice under the head again."**

---

## Those ten days

And this is where the ten days went.

**We had to redrill those eleven metres.**

Because after our head passed through that cavity, the wall of the hole was wrong — a hole inside a cavity has no wall.

And a section without a wall becomes a snag point for every subsequent descent.

**So we had to widen those eleven metres with hot water into a continuous bore.**

And widening eleven metres at one thousand eight hundred and forty metres requires:

```
additional heat    11 days of boiler fuel
additional time    10 days
```

**Ten days.**

**And we had seventeen left.**

---

## And what that cavity was

At the end of this section, because it is the real content of this section, and we did not know at the time.

In the report we wrote those eleven metres as:

> An intraglacial cavity approximately eleven metres in height was encountered at 1 840 m; origin undetermined; handled in accordance with procedure.

**"Origin undetermined."**

---

And after the forty-one hours — after we went down, after I asked that question — I knew what it was.

It is not a cavity.

**It is a shaft.**

**A vertical shaft that has been there three hundred thousand years and been maintained for three hundred thousand years.**

**And its top is at one thousand eight hundred and forty metres.**

**And our drill head entered it from above at eleven minutes past six on the morning of the twenty-ninth day.**

---

And the last layer of that, because it is the thing in this chapter I least want to think about.

**That shaft was cut from below.**

It stops at one thousand eight hundred and forty metres because that is where it has to stop — any higher and the ice is not old enough, and a shaft would be sheared off by the ice's flow.

**So it waited down there for three hundred thousand years.**

**And its top is one thousand eight hundred and forty metres below the surface.**

---

**It cut a shaft, and stopped one thousand eight hundred and forty metres short of the exit, and maintained it for three hundred thousand years.**

**And it cannot come out by itself.**

**It is waiting for somebody to drill down from above.**

---

And at eleven minutes past six on the morning of the twenty-ninth day, our head went into it.

And what I did in those seven seconds was stop paying out, shut off the heat, and not haul up.

**And if I had hauled up — by the fourth line of that table — the head would have jammed against the cavity roof.**

**And a jammed head means the hole is lost.**

**And a lost hole means our season is over.**

**And our season being over means waiting another year.**

---

**And I do not know whether we had another year.**

**Because of the checksum's three questions, the second is *when does it get read.***

**And the third is *what happens if the check fails.***

---

That third line I memorised was written by Ilan over seven years.

And when he wrote it he was thinking about protecting equipment.

**And what it protected was not equipment.**
