# 7.8 · Not a Ruin

That recognition took me nine minutes.

And this section is those nine minutes.

---

## Before

Before those nine minutes we had been in that cylinder about an hour twenty.

And in that hour twenty what we did was record.

Because that is the only thing we know how to do.

---

We recorded:

```
dimensions       diameter 11 m, height 4.1 m, ±0.02
wall material    (sampled; see below)
temperature      4.1 ± 0.01 Celsius (eleven points)
illumination     1.1 x 10^-4 lux (wall), 0.7 x 10^-4 at the centre
sound            41 Hz continuous, 21 dB; pulse at 11-second intervals
pressure         1.02 standard (slightly above the surface)
gas composition  (sampled; see below)
humidity         0.03 (extremely dry)
```

---

And two of those I write separately.

---

**Gas composition.**

We sampled the air in that space.

And its composition **differs** from the surface.

```
nitrogen        0.78     (surface 0.78)
oxygen          0.11     (surface 0.21)
argon           0.009    (surface 0.009)
carbon dioxide  0.0004   (surface 0.0004)
remainder       0.10     (surface 0.0001)
```

---

**The oxygen is half the surface's.**

**And the *remainder* is a thousand times the surface's.**

And what that remainder is we could not determine on the ice (our instruments measure only those four).

**And it was determined at home afterwards.**

And what it is I keep for Chapter Eight — because that matter belongs with what happened in those forty-one hours, and not with these nine minutes.

---

And the consequence of half the oxygen is concrete:

**At an oxygen partial pressure half the surface's, a person's maximum work capacity falls about thirty per cent, and cognitive performance about fifteen.**

**And we were there three hours twenty.**

---

**So the recognition I made in this section was made in a state of cognition fifteen per cent down.**

**And it took nine minutes.**

**And I computed afterwards: in a normal state it would have taken about three.**

---

**Humidity.**

Zero point zero three.

**And in the camp it is zero point zero seven (7.3), which is already the level at which every organic thing slowly dehydrates.**

**And here it is half of that.**

---

And that figure is the key to this section.

**And in those nine minutes I began from it.**

---

## Those nine minutes

Time: one minute past one in the afternoon.

I was crouched at the wall of that cylinder.

And I was doing one thing: **scraping the wall.**

With a standard sampling knife.

And the purpose of scraping was to take a little wall material into a sample tube.

---

And I scraped about forty seconds, and stopped.

**And I stopped because the knife took nothing off.**

---

I want this clear, because it is the first step of those nine minutes.

A steel sampling knife, scraping anything, takes a little of it off.

**Scrape metal and you get filings.**

**Scrape rock and you get powder.**

**Scrape ice and you get ice.**

**And scraping that wall, nothing.**

---

And I changed the angle. I bore down. I used the tip.

**Nothing.**

And I looked at the edge.

**There was a notch in the edge.**

---

**That wall had taken a piece out of my knife.**

**And nothing had come off it.**

---

## Minute one

My thought in that moment, set down honestly:

**this thing is harder than steel.**

And that is not a remarkable recognition. Many things are harder than steel — corundum, silicon carbide, diamond, various ceramics.

**And my second thought is where it really began.**

My second thought was:

**then why is there no dust on it.**

---

## Minute two

I want that thought written clearly, because it is the whole of those nine minutes.

---

A thing harder than steel does not wear.

**And not wearing is a different thing from not accumulating dust.**

---

And what came into my mind then was the camp above us.

Every surface in that camp — desks, instruments, cases, the whiteboard — has dust on it.

And where does that dust come from.

And in Antarctica there is no soil, no pollen, no industrial particulate.

**And the camp still has dust.**

And we had looked into its source at the camp (because it affects certain instruments):

```
human skin flakes     principal
clothing fibres       secondary
packaging debris      secondary
diesel combustion particles    trace
```

---

**So the principal source of dust is us.**

---

And in this cylinder, for three hundred thousand years, there have been no people.

**So no skin flakes.**

**So no clothing fibres.**

---

**So there should be no dust here at all.**

---

## Minute three

And in the third minute I did a thing.

**I turned the lamp and threw the light across the wall at a grazing angle.**

And grazing light is the best way to see dust — because dust scatters and a smooth surface reflects.

**And I saw dust.**

---

**There is dust.**

---

And it is very thin.

I computed the thickness afterwards (by a crude method I invented on the spot: I drew a line through the dust with the knife and measured the optical contrast of that line).

And the thickness:

```
about 1.1 x 10^-6 m
```

**One micrometre.**

---

## Minute four

And in the fourth minute what I did was arithmetic.

**I computed how long a micrometre takes.**

And computing it requires a deposition rate.

And in a sealed space with no people, no wind, and no soil, dust has one source:

**the space's own material, spalling extremely slowly.**

And every material spalls — from thermal cycling, from cosmic rays, from quantum tunnelling, because everything slowly comes apart.

And for a very stable material I used an order of magnitude I remembered:

```
about 10^-15 m/year
```

(A figure I remembered from solid surface physics, for material loss in ultra-high vacuum. And this is not ultra-high vacuum; the pressure is one point zero two standard — so the real rate should be higher. And I used that figure because it is the one I remembered.)

---

And then a micrometre takes:

```
1.1 x 10^-6 / 10^-15 = 1.1 x 10^9 years
```

---

**Eleven hundred million years.**

---

## Minute five

And in the fifth minute I realised what I had computed was meaningless.

**Because my deposition rate was a guess.**

And a time computed from a guessed rate is also a guess.

**So that eleven hundred million years shows nothing.**

---

And at that point I should have abandoned this line.

**And I did not abandon it, because of a very small thing.**

---

**I looked again at that line I had drawn.**

And that line is one I drew in the dust with a knife.

And on either side of it, dust.

**And in the middle of it, none.**

**Which is obvious — I had just drawn it.**

---

**And what I thought in that moment was: how long before that line is covered again.**

---

## Minute six

And that thought turned the whole thing over.

**Because that is not a question about *how long this dust took to accumulate.***

**That is a question about *how long dust takes to come back after it is cleared.***

---

And the difference between those two questions is the whole of this section.

**Question one: how long did this dust take.** — Needs a deposition rate, which I do not know.

**Question two: has this dust been accumulating since this space was built.** — This question **does not need** a deposition rate.

---

**Because if it has been accumulating since then, its thickness should equal the deposition rate times three hundred thousand years.**

**And three hundred thousand years is a figure we know.**

**So:**

```
if this dust is three hundred thousand years' worth
   then  deposition rate = 1.1 x 10^-6 m / 3.0 x 10^5 years
                        = 3.7 x 10^-12 m/year
```

---

**And that rate is three thousand times the rate I guessed.**

---

## Minute seven

And in the seventh minute I did the most useful thing I did in those three hours twenty.

**I stopped guessing that rate.**

**I went looking for something I could measure directly.**

---

And I found it.

**The edge of that line.**

---

I want the method written out, because it is the only improvised field measurement I made in the whole of the Tracing Back, and it is right.

A line drawn in dust has an edge, and that edge **blurs** with time.

Because dust grains slide from the edge (gravity), are moved by faint air disturbance, and diffuse by thermal motion.

**And the rate of that blurring is unrelated to the deposition rate.**

**It depends only on grain size, layer thickness, and the disturbance in the environment.**

**And all three I could measure on the spot.**

---

And what I did was:

I drew **eleven lines** in the dust.

And I drew them at different times — the first in the third minute, the last in the seventh.

**So their ages ran from four minutes to zero.**

---

And at the end of the seventh minute I looked at those eleven lines.

**And their sharpness differed.**

**The first (drawn four minutes earlier) had a slightly blurred edge.**

**The last (just drawn) was sharp.**

---

**So the blurring is measurable within four minutes.**

---

## Minute eight

And in the eighth minute I finished that measurement.

I photographed the eleven lines with the lamp at the same angle (using Ilan's camera).

Then I compared them by eye (I could not compute on the spot; I computed after I got back).

**And on the spot I saw the trend.**

And the trend is:

```
edge blur      proportional to    elapsed time since drawing
proportion     about 0.09 per minute
```

---

**So: a line will be entirely blurred after about eleven minutes.**

---

## Minute nine

And in the ninth minute I did the division.

---

**A line is entirely blurred after eleven minutes.**

**And the dust on that cylinder's wall is one micrometre thick.**

**And if that dust has been accumulating since the day this space was built — how many *lines* would there have been in three hundred thousand years?**

---

**No lines.**

**Because nobody drew any.**

---

**And there is something else.**

---

I want to write this step very slowly.

A dust layer, over three hundred thousand years, does not only get thicker.

**It gets disturbed.**

And the sources of disturbance in that space are three, and I had measured all three:

```
one     the 41 Hz vibration (that continuous hum)
        amplitude, measured on the wall: about 1.1 x 10^-7 m
two     the pulse every 11 seconds
        amplitude: about 4.1 x 10^-7 m
three   convection of the gas (a temperature difference — wall 4.1, centre slightly lower)
```

---

**And the amplitude of the second is four hundred nanometres.**

**And that dust layer is one thousand nanometres thick.**

---

**So every eleven seconds that layer is shaken through forty per cent of its own thickness.**

---

## The conclusion

And this is the conclusion.

**A one-micrometre dust layer, on a surface shaken four hundred nanometres every eleven seconds, cannot remain one micrometre thick.**

**It gets shaken apart.**

**It goes downhill.**

**It piles up where the wall's curvature sends it.**

---

**And three hundred thousand years is nine hundred million shakes.**

---

**So that dust layer, over three hundred thousand years, is shaken nine hundred million times.**

**And after nine hundred million shakes it is not a uniform micrometre.**

**It is all piled at the base of the wall.**

---

**And it is not.**

**It lies evenly across the wall.**

---

## Therefore

**Therefore that dust is not three hundred thousand years' worth.**

**It is recent.**

---

And how recent, I computed an upper bound on the spot.

```
if that dust stayed uniform through nine hundred million shakes
   then  the number of shakes it has undergone must be fewer than the number
         required to pile it
and the number required to pile it, estimated as a lower bound on the spot: about 10^5
so  the number of shakes that dust has undergone    < 10^5
so  the age of that dust                            < 10^5 x 11 seconds
                                                    = 1.1 x 10^6 seconds
                                                    = 12.7 days
```

---

**Twelve point seven days.**

---

## And in that moment

I stood up.

And standing up made me slightly dizzy (half the oxygen).

And I put a hand on the wall.

**And my hand was on a wall at four point one degrees.**

---

And I spoke.

I said:

**"Ilan."**

He was on the other side of that cylinder, also sampling.

He said, "Mm."

---

And what I said is the last thing in this section, and the most important thing I said in those three hours twenty.

I said:

**"It's not a ruin."**

---

He said, "What."

I said:

**"The dust on this wall is under thirteen days old."**

I said:

**"And this space has been here three hundred thousand years."**

I said:

**"So something is cleaning."**

---

And he did not answer at once.

And I heard him stand up.

And I heard him come over — the cylinder is eleven metres, so about eight paces.

**And he came and crouched beside me and looked at that dust.**

---

And he looked about twenty seconds.

Then he said something.

And it was not about the dust.

He said:

**"He."**

**"Then what about what we've scraped off."**

---

I said, "What."

He said:

**"You've just scraped that wall with a knife."**

**"You've drawn eleven lines."**

**"I've taken three samples over there. I've drilled three 0.01-metre holes in that wall."**

He said:

**"And something is cleaning."**

---

**"Will it clean up what we have done, too."**

---

I did not answer.

And the two of us crouched at two thousand and ninety-nine metres, at four point one degrees, at half the oxygen, in a seamless cylinder, looking at a layer of dust one micrometre thick and under thirteen days old.

**And every eleven seconds, that noise came.**

**And every eleven seconds, that dust was shaken.**

**And in that dust were eleven lines I had drawn, the oldest of them twelve minutes.**
