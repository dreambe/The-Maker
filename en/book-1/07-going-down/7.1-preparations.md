# 7.1 · Preparations

The eight lines were struck out on the seventeenth of March.

We went down on the eleventh of January the following year.

Ten months between.

**This section is those ten months.**

And one thing first, because it sets the register:

**In those ten months there was not one day on which I was chasing the truth.**

**In those ten months I was doing paperwork.**

---

## Money

The first money came from that long-term continuity risk category, forty per cent less than we asked for (5.1).

And was that enough to go to Antarctica.

**No. An order of magnitude short.**

I want to lay the figures out, because almost nobody knows what one trip to Antarctica costs.

```
return passage to the continent (transfers, ship berth, fuel surcharge)   410 000 per person
life support for one season on the ice (about 100 days)                 1 100 000 per person
drilling equipment (with transport, installation, removal)              11 000 000
drilling consumables (bits, fluids, casing)                              4 100 000
power and heating (mainly diesel, about 40 a litre landed)                7 000 000
communications (satellite bandwidth, billed hourly)                       1 900 000
contingency and medical reserve                                          3 000 000
insurance                                                                refused (see below)
```

And our annual budget was one eleventh of that total.

---

Han Yin solved it.

By a method I still think only somebody who has worked twenty years inside a large system could have thought of.

**She did not apply for more money.**

**She changed how one thing was classified.**

---

Specifically.

To go to Antarctica our project needed an independent research voyage. That is a whole ship, a whole season, a whole logistics train.

And there are many voyages to that continent every year.

And those voyages share one property: **they are almost never full.**

Because a ship's deadweight and berths are fixed, and a voyage's science tasks often do not fill them.

**So a great deal of capacity sails empty every year.**

---

What Han Yin did was reclassify us from *a project requiring an independent voyage* to *a project carried aboard.*

And a carried project costs seven per cent of an independent voyage.

**And a carried project has a price.**

---

I want that price clear, because it shaped everything after.

**A carried project does not choose where it goes, how long it stays, or when it leaves.**

Our window was determined by the voyage's primary task.

The window we got was:

```
arrival           11 January
withdrawal        26 February
days on ice       46
usable work days  after weather and logistics, projected 31
```

**Forty-six days.**

And in forty-six days we had to drill through two thousand one hundred metres of ice.

And drilling through two thousand one hundred metres of ice, on the technology of the time, **usually takes two to three seasons.**

---

The day I got that window I did one thing.

I telephoned Ilan.

I said, "Forty-six days."

He was quiet at the other end for a moment.

Then he said:

**"Enough."**

I said, "Have you costed it?"

He said:

"I've been costing it for seven years."

---

## Permits

Permits are harder than money.

Because Antarctica belongs to nobody, and precisely because it belongs to nobody, doing anything there requires a great many people to agree.

The permits we needed, listed here because it is the least dramatic and most truthful part of this section:

```
environmental impact evaluation (initial)          written by us, reviewed by the ministry
environmental impact evaluation (comprehensive)    mandatory, subglacial drilling
advance notification to Antarctic Treaty parties   twelve months ahead
review under Annex II of the Environmental Protocol  entry into an undisturbed area
drill-fluid permit                                  waived; we applied with a hot-water drill
biological contamination control plan               mandatory
waste handling and full return-shipment plan        mandatory
domestic project, foreign affairs, and border formalities   omitted
```

And the worst of them is the third: **twelve months' advance notification.**

**And we had ten.**

---

Han Yin solved that too.

And what she did this time I want to write down, because it is the one thing in those ten months that made me uncomfortable.

**She filed our drilling as a *variation within the scope of an already notified project.***

That is: the voyage we were carried on already had a subglacial drilling task of its own (a glaciology project taking ice cores).

And our drilling, on paper, was a *depth adjustment* to that task.

---

I asked her: is that compliant.

She said, "It is compliant."

I said, "Is it honest."

She was silent for about three seconds.

Then she said:

**"Dr Shen. Twenty-one years in this job."**

**"Compliance and honesty are handled by different departments."**

---

I write this down because I signed that document.

**I knew what it was when I signed it.**

**I signed it.**

---

## Equipment

Ilan made the list. Six weeks.

Four hundred and eleven items.

I will not list them. Three of them, because all three were used.

**One: the hot-water drill.**

Not a mechanical drill: a pipe spraying hot water down, melting the ice.

Its advantage: speed. It descends twenty metres an hour; a mechanical drill does two.

Its disadvantage: **the hole freezes shut again.**

A hole made by hot water begins re-freezing from its walls once the heat stops.

The rate depends on ice temperature. At that location the ice is minus twenty-eight.

Ilan had computed it:

```
bore 0.5 m · closure in about 41 hours
```

**Forty-one hours.**

**So once we were down, we had forty-one hours.**

---

I want to stop here.

**That figure is the entire time budget of what happened later.**

And it was not ours to choose.

**It is a property of ice.**

---

**Two: the suit.**

One each. Six layers. The outermost windproof, the innermost wicking, four insulating between.

It weighs seven kilograms.

Eleven minutes to put on, seven to take off.

**And it has a property I have never seen in any specification:**

**Wearing it, you cannot hear your own breathing.**

Those six layers keep sound out, and keep sound in.

I took the hood off, later, down there.

**I took it off because I needed to listen.**

---

**Three: that instrument.**

Marcus asked for this one and none of us understood.

He wanted a **sequencer.**

A portable one, able to work below freezing, able to read a few hundred base pairs.

I said: we are going to look at a structure under the ice. What do we want a sequencer for.

He said, "In case."

I said, "In case of what."

He said:

"In case there is something in there that can be sequenced."

I said, "What could be sequenced."

He said:

"Don't know."

He said, "Which is why it's *in case.*"

---

**That instrument was used.**

**And what it sequenced is in none of our plans.**

---

## The confidentiality undertaking

I write this because it is the ugliest stretch of those ten months.

Our project sits under long-term continuity risk.

And the results of projects under that category are, before formal release, **controlled information.**

And the rules for controlled information require everyone with access to sign an undertaking.

---

I read it. Fourteen pages.

Its substance, in one sentence:

**Until approval is given, you may tell nobody.**

And *nobody* includes: your family, your colleagues, the leadership of your own institution, and other researchers.

---

I signed.

Ilan signed.

Marcus signed.

Ye Qi signed.

**Lu Ting did not sign.**

---

## Who was excluded

The last part of this section.

---

Lu Ting did not sign, and it was not refusal.

**He was not on the list.**

The list was set above us, on three criteria:

```
one    those taking part in field operations directly
two    those with primary responsibility for data analysis
three  the project lead
```

And Lu Ting is seventy-five. He cannot go onto the ice — he cannot pass the medical, and no doctor would sign.

And he does not do the data analysis.

And he is not the lead.

**So he is not on the list.**

**So he cannot sign that undertaking.**

**So he cannot have access to controlled information.**

**So from that day, I could tell him nothing.**

---

I want to write the day I told him.

An afternoon in November.

In that eighteen-square-metre room. It took about forty seconds.

---

The first thing he did when I finished was nod.

Then he said:

"Don't put yourself in a difficult position over compliance."

Then he stood up and went over to the whiteboard.

By then the board had sixty-three items.

The heading was still there, still with the two-word gap in the middle.

---

He stood there a while.

Then he said a thing I have thought about many times since.

He said:

**"He. My whole life I have been the one on somebody else's list."**

**"In 1974, in that pit, I was last on it."**

**"Then I moved up, a little at a time. Fifty years of it."**

He knocked on the board.

**"And now I'm off it again."**

---

He put his coat on.

He said:

"You go. And when you come back, if that undertaking still won't let you say —"

He stopped.

He said:

**"Then don't say it."**

**"I'll wait."**

---

He left.

After that day he still came in every day.

He still sat in the one chair with a back. He still worked on the fossil database — that involves no controlled information.

**And we never discussed the other thing again.**

**Fourteen months.**

**Not once.**

---

And one thing at the end of this section, because it is the one thing from those ten months I still think I got right.

When we came back from Antarctica, the undertaking was lifted.

**And the first person I told was not anybody in our group, not Han Yin, not the ministry.**

**It was Lu Ting.**

Six hours, in that room.

From the ice to the forty-one hours.

And in those six hours he did not interrupt once.

---

**And he never asked why I had waited fourteen months.**

**Because he knew why.**
