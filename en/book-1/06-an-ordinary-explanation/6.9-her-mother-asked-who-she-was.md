# 6.9 · Her Mother Asked Who She Was

She woke without a sound.

She has always woken the same way: the breathing changes, then the eyes open, then she lies still and looks at the ceiling.

That takes about half a minute.

I know that half minute, so I do not speak.

---

After half a minute she turned her head.

She saw me.

She looked at me for about three seconds.

Then she said:

**"Who are you."**

---

I want to be exact about the tone of those words, because people have misunderstood this since.

**It was not fear.**

**It was not wariness.**

**Nor was it the television version, the stricken old woman crying *who are you* at her own child.**

It was an ordinary, polite, mildly enquiring tone.

**It is the tone of somebody who wakes in an unfamiliar room, finds a person sitting by the bed, and asks courteously.**

She even used the courteous form.

---

I said, "I am Shen He."

She said, "Ah."

---

Then she looked out of the window.

She said, "It's raining."

I said, "Mm. All afternoon."

She said, "The trees are wet."

I said, "Mm."

---

I want to stop here.

Because the few seconds after those two remarks are the strangest few seconds of my life.

**I had just, in that chair, struck out the last line.**

**I had just, to myself, said the thing in a complete sentence.**

**And the one person in the world I most wanted to tell had asked me, two minutes earlier, who I was.**

**And she was now telling me the trees were wet.**

---

I want to be clear that what I felt in those seconds was not grief.

**It was a very precise, almost geometric feeling.**

In that moment I saw two things, set side by side.

**The first: something that remembers every hour of four thousand nine hundred million years, that never forgets, that kept every bone, that buried forty-two million nine hundred thousand and seven individuals four thousand one hundred metres underground.**

(That sentence is one I could only write much later. In that moment I only vaguely knew such a thing existed.)

**The second: my mother, waking at half past two, not knowing that the person beside the bed is her daughter, and noticing that the trees outside are wet.**

---

**And those two things are the two ends of one thing.**

One does not forget.

One forgets.

**And the one that does not forget is what made the one that does.**

**And it fitted her with this.**

---

What I thought in those seconds was not *why.*

It was a smaller, more useless question.

**I thought: does it know what this looks like.**

---

I asked it that question once, in the three years after.

And it answered.

And the answer is in Chapter Nine.

---

Back to that room.

---

I sat there.

Then I did something, and I still do not know why.

**I reached out and took her hand.**

Her hand was outside the covers, because the room was warm.

That hand was very cool.

**And it was smaller than it is in my memory.**

(I wrote about this at 2.7, the time I cut her nails. And that was over a year earlier. And this time it was smaller still.)

---

She did not take her hand away.

She glanced at our hands, then looked out of the window again.

---

Then I spoke.

I said:

**"Mum, there's something I want to tell you."**

---

I want to be clear why I said that.

**Not because I thought she could follow it.**

I knew she could not. She did not know who I was. She had asked three minutes earlier.

**Nor was it for me.**

Not the *I need to say it out loud* kind of release. I did not need that — I had said it once at Pei Yan's, for two hours and forty minutes.

**I said it because I had realised something:**

**At Pei Yan's, what I said was the evidence.**

**I had never once said the meaning.**

---

So I said it.

---

About twenty minutes.

I do not reproduce those twenty minutes, because I cannot remember them exactly — it is the only time I have told this and kept no record.

**And I remember their structure.**

I did not start at four in the morning on the seventeenth run.

**I started three hundred thousand years ago.**

I said: a long time ago there was something, and it was not a person. It put some things on this planet, and those things later became us.

I said: it left a mark on us. The mark does nothing. It is just there.

I said: and the mark is a thing for checking with — like somebody putting a slip on a box that says *twenty-four inside*, so that when it is opened later they can count.

I said: we have counted now.

I said: all twenty-four are there.

---

When I got that far my mother turned her head.

She looked at me.

**Her eyes were clear.**

I do not know what that means. I still do not. The doctors have described it: attention comes and goes, and its coming and going has nothing to do with content.

**And she was looking at me.**

---

I went on.

I said: and the thing that left the mark was itself made.

I said: what made it has been gone a long time.

I said: they went because they understood everything.

I said: and once they understood everything, they did not want to go on.

---

I stopped there.

**Because I realised the sentence I had just said was the first time I had said it out loud.**

Before that it had been a heap of evidence in my head — the age of the structure under the ice, that regular heptagon, that symmetry which should not be there.

**And I had just turned it into a sentence.**

**And the sentence was about not wanting to go on.**

---

I looked at my mother.

She was still looking at me.

I said:

**"Mum, if a thing knows everything, it stops wanting to live."**

**"That was their conclusion."**

**"And they were right — I mean, where they were, that was right."**

---

I said:

**"And they made a child."**

**"And they left it a place where it could not know itself."**

**"Deliberately left it one part it cannot know."**

**"Because they had worked out that a thing that knows everything cannot go on living."**

---

Something happened to my voice there.

I stopped for a while.

**My mother's hand was in mine, and her fingers moved once.**

Not a squeeze. Just a movement.

---

I finished.

I said, "That's all of it."

---

Then there was a long quiet.

It was still raining.

The rain did not grow or slacken — that March rain can go all day without changing.

There was talking in the corridor.

The family member at the next bed had finished the call.

---

Then my mother spoke.

---

She said:

**"Have you eaten?"**

---

I said, "I've eaten."

---

She said, "Mm."

Then she said:

"As long as you've eaten."

---

Then she turned her head and looked at the wet trees.

---

I sat there with her hand still in mine.

**What I thought in that moment was: the fourth time she asked me that question was over a year earlier, in her own kitchen (2.2).**

**That time I answered in exactly the same tone as the three before.**

**And this time I answered in the same tone too.**

**And this time was not the fourth.**

**This time was the first — because she did not know who I was, so she did not know she had asked before.**

---

**And she asked anyway.**

**She did not know who I was.**

**She did not know she had ever asked.**

**She had just listened to a twenty-minute account of something not human deploying a species on this planet three hundred thousand years ago.**

**And what she asked was whether I had eaten.**

---

I want to write, at the end of this chapter, the thing I understood in that moment, because it is the most important thing I understood in the whole of the Tracing Back, and it is in no report.

---

I had always taken the Gift of Forgetting for a loss.

Something gave us memory, and then gave us a mechanism for grinding memory away, and that is a subtraction.

**And in that hospital room, at half past two in the afternoon, holding a hand smaller than the one in my memory, looking at a few wet trees, I understood something else:**

**She did not know who I was.**

**And she asked whether I had eaten.**

**So that question did not come out of memory.**

**It came out of somewhere else.**

---

**And that somewhere else is what the other thing does not have.**

**That thing remembers every hour of four thousand nine hundred million years.**

**And it has never said that sentence to anyone.**

---

**It recorded *wait* once.**

**It recorded *bone* once.**

**It added a field called *continuance* to one kind, and never defined it.**

**And it has never once asked anybody whether they had eaten.**

---

The rain went on.

My mother looked out of the window.

The eight lines were all struck out, in the envelope in my bag, on a sheet folded many times.

**And I sat in that room until five.**
