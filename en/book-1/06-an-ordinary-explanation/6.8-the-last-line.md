# 6.8 · The Last Line

Three remain.

Line four: statistical error.

Line seven: my definition is wrong.

Line eight: coincidence.

**And these three are the shortest of the eight — two months between them.**

They are also the most important, because they do not examine the sequence. They examine **me.**

---

## Line four · statistical error

This one I did not do.

Not because I did not want to. Because I should not.

**A person cannot check their own statistics.**

I computed a probability at 1.6. I wrote there: I computed it three times and then sat down.

And three times were mine.

**A person who computes three times uses, all three times, the same assumptions, the same intuitions, the same blind spot.**

---

So I gave it away.

To two people.

**The first was Marcus.**

Six days. His report ran to eleven pages.

His conclusion was that there is an error in my method.

I want to write it down, because it is the most important error anyone pointed out to me in the whole of the Tracing Back.

---

**He said: in testing, I had fallen into multiple comparisons.**

What I did was search the non-coding regions of the human genome for a checksum-bearing sequence.

And the human genome has three thousand million base pairs.

And my window was two hundred and forty-seven base pairs, sliding one at a time.

**So I performed about three thousand million tests.**

And my significance threshold was p < 0.001.

**Three thousand million tests at 0.001 gives an expectation of three million false positives.**

---

When I reached that page something went cold in my stomach.

**Because if that is right, then the whole thing is over.**

---

And Marcus's eleventh page says something else.

It says:

> **The above criticism applies to the operation *search the genome for a checksum.***
>
> **It does not apply to what Shen He did.**

His reasons, three lines:

> **One. She was not searching. She was testing a specific, pre-specified region.**
>
> **Two. She did not choose that region — the region was singled out while she was doing something else (a conservation analysis), because it was anomalously conserved. And that screen had nothing to do with checksums.**
>
> **Three. Therefore the number of tests is one, not three thousand million.**

---

At the foot of the eleventh page he wrote:

> **But her paper does not make this clear.**
>
> **And any reviewer will read it as three thousand million tests.**
>
> **Recommendation: rewrite the methods. Put the provenance of the region before the test.**

---

I rewrote it as he said.

Four days.

**And they are the most useful four days I have ever written.**

Because in the three years after, across fourteen external briefings, nobody once came at us with multiple comparisons.

**Not because they did not think of it.**

**Because the methods section blocked it in front.**

---

**The second person** is the one my table at 6.1 calls *somebody who does not know what I am looking for.*

I found a statistician, surname Wu, in her forties.

What I gave her was a set of sequences: one of them the real one, and ninety-nine others drawn at random from the genome at the same length.

I did not tell her which was real.

**The question I gave her was: of these hundred, is any one special in some sense.**

I did not say *checksum.* I did not say what I was looking for.

---

Nineteen days.

She sent back a table and a letter.

The table has one hundred sequences, each with a score.

**One of them scores eleven standard deviations above the other ninety-nine.**

**And that one is the real one.**

---

And her letter says:

> Dr Shen, I do not know what you are looking for. And there is a thing on sequence 41 which I can describe and cannot name.
>
> Its first 240 positions, under some linear combination, land on the value specified by its last 7.
>
> Statistically I do not know what that is called.
>
> **And I have seen it at my husband's. He does telecommunications.**

---

**Line four: does not hold.**

**Time: one month (of which four days were mine).**

---

## Line seven · my definition is wrong

This one was Marcus's; I had no part in it.

The question: when I imported *checksum* from information theory, did I import it wrongly.

Specifically: **is the probability of a random sequence carrying a checksum relation really as small as I take it to be.**

Because if that probability is not small, none of this is worth discussing.

---

Three weeks.

I cannot follow all of his report; I can only write his conclusions.

They have two parts.

---

**Part one: the exact value of that probability.**

He computed:

```
a random sequence of length 247
under a given encoding, given weights, given modulus
the probability that the weighted sum of its first 240 positions
exactly equals the value represented by its last 7

= 1 / 16 384
```

**About four in sixty thousand.**

He says: that is not small.

---

**Part two — and this is the point.**

He says:

> **The figure above is a conditional probability: given the encoding, the weights, and the modulus.**
>
> **And those three were not given.**
>
> **Shen He found them.**

He says:

> **When she obtained the sequence she did not know the encoding, the weights, or the modulus.**
>
> **She tried. She tried a number of combinations.**
>
> **And if she tried 16 384 combinations, then finding one that passes is certain.**
>
> **So the real question is: how many did she try.**

---

And he went and looked at my lab records.

They are there — I have kept them from the first day, because I knew somebody would come and check.

**The number he found is three.**

Combination one: A=0, C=1, G=2, T=3; weights the position index; modulus 16 384.

**Passes.**

He writes:

> **The first combination passed.**
>
> **She then tried two more (different encodings) to confirm it was not an artefact of the encoding.**
>
> **Those two do not pass — and that matters, because it shows the relation depends on one particular encoding rather than holding under any encoding.**

---

And the last paragraph of his report:

> **So the real computation is this.**
>
> **She tried 3 combinations. Each passes with probability 1/16 384.**
>
> **So the probability that at least one passes is about 3 / 16 384 ≈ 1.8 x 10^-4.**
>
> **And she verified it in 194 000 individuals; all pass.**
>
> **And an accidental relation will not hold in 194 000 independent individuals — unless that stretch is the same stretch in all 194 000.**
>
> **And it is.**
>
> **So the relation is a property of that stretch, not of that test.**
>
> **So 1.8 x 10^-4 is not the probability of the relation. It is the probability that Shen He found the relation within three attempts.**
>
> **The probability of the relation requires asking: what is the probability that a length-247 region, identical across 194 000 people, carries such a relation.**
>
> **And that figure is 1 / 16 384.**
>
> **And that figure needs a reference: how many regions of length 247, identical across 194 000 people, exist in the human genome.**

He computed it.

```
count = 41
```

**Forty-one.**

> **So: there are 41 such regions in the human genome.**
>
> **If any one of them carrying this relation were accidental, the expected count is 41 / 16 384 = 0.0025.**
>
> **And the observed count is 1.**
>
> **Ratio: 400.**

---

**Line seven: does not hold.**

**Time: three weeks (Marcus's).**

---

## Line eight · coincidence

One left.

And this one is mine.

**Because it cannot be given away.**

*Coincidence* does not mean *the probability is small.* Small-probability things happen every day.

**It means: could all of this be a heap of independent things that happen to have landed together.**

---

I listed the things.

```
one     a 247 bp non-coding sequence, anomalously conserved
two     it carries a checksum
three   it is identical across 194 000 people
four    it is present in 1 407 ancient samples, the oldest 45 000 years
five    its age is 300 000 years, and 300 000 is not enough to fix a neutral sequence
six     the fossil record has a gap at the same point in time
seven   there are no transitional forms on either side of the gap
eight   there is a structure under the Antarctic ice holding temperature for 300 000 years
nine    that structure's outline is a regular heptagon
ten     humans have 41 features not explicable by evolution
eleven  the selection coefficient needed to complete those 41 in the time available is 0.41
twelve  the information in that checksum exceeds what a signature requires
```

Twelve.

---

And what *coincidence* asks is: **are these twelve independent events.**

Because if they are independent, then multiplying their probabilities gives an astronomical figure, and such things do not happen.

**And if they are not independent — if they are twelve faces of one thing — then their joint probability is the probability of that one thing.**

---

Three weeks.

The method was pairwise tests of independence.

Twelve items, sixty-six pairs.

For each pair I asked: does knowing A change the probability of B.

---

I made a table of the sixty-six.

It is in the Tracing Back archive as 6-8-1.

**Of the sixty-six, sixty-three are correlated.**

The four strongest:

```
(two · twelve)   checksum & excess information     r = 1.00   (the same place in the same sequence)
(five · six)     300 000 years & the fossil gap    r = 0.99   (the same point in time)
(eight · nine)   the structure & the heptagon       r = 1.00   (the same object)
(ten · eleven)   the 41 features & 0.41             r = 1.00   (the latter computed from the former)
```

And the three uncorrelated:

```
(one · eight)    sequence conservation & the Antarctic structure
(three · nine)   identity across 194 000 & the heptagon
(four · ten)     ancient samples & the 41 features
```

**Those three are uncorrelated.**

And I want it clear why that is the crucial part.

**Because if all twelve were pairwise correlated, that would suggest they are twelve ways of saying one measurement — that is, that one error of mine is showing up in twelve places.**

**And those three uncorrelated pairs show that among the twelve there are at least two groups arising from entirely different chains of evidence.**

---

**One group is molecular (the sequence, the checksum, the age, the ancient samples).**

**One is physical (the Antarctic structure, the geometry, the constant temperature, the energy expenditure).**

**And they are uncorrelated — no shared method of measurement, no shared instrument, no shared database, no shared operator.**

**And they point at the same moment in time: three hundred thousand years ago.**

---

I finished on the seventeenth of March.

A Friday.

**And when I finished it was two in the afternoon, in a hospital room.**

---

Why I was there.

My mother was not well that season. She fell at the end of February — the hip — had surgery, and had to stay in for a while.

I went every afternoon. I took my laptop and worked while she slept.

On the afternoon of the seventeenth of March she was asleep.

And in the chair beside her bed I finished the last pair.

The sixty-sixth.

**(seven · twelve): no transitional forms on either side of the gap · and · the information in the checksum exceeding what a signature requires.**

**r = 0.31.**

Weakly correlated.

---

I finished.

I saved the table.

Then I took the envelope out of my bag.

Out of the envelope I took the sheet — the two-column table of eight lines, drawn on the seventeenth of April in that eighteen-square-metre room.

By then it had been folded many times. Lines one, two, three, five, six, four, seven were all struck out.

**Only line eight remained.**

---

I took a pen.

There was a pen on her bedside cabinet, from the nurses' station, blue ballpoint, the cap chewed.

I used that.

---

I drew a line through line eight.

---

I want to describe that stroke, because I remember it.

**It is not straight.**

Because I was sitting in a hospital chair with the sheet on my knee.

**And a knee is not a desk.**

The stroke starts to the left of the word *coincidence*, runs right, and has a wobble in the middle.

---

Then I put the sheet back in the envelope.

Then I sat.

It was raining outside.

**March rain, very fine, not forming drops on the glass, only making it translucent.**

That room was on the third floor, and outside the window was the hospital's back yard, with a few trees in it.

**Those trees are a darker colour in the rain than out of it.**

---

I sat there looking at those trees and thinking about something very small.

I was thinking that all eight lines on that sheet were struck out now.

**And eight lines struck out means one thing, and until that moment I had never said that thing to myself in a complete sentence.**

I was in that hospital room, beside my mother's bed, at two in the afternoon, with rain outside.

**And for the first time I said it to myself in a complete sentence.**

---

**I said (to myself):**

**Humanity was made.**

**And what made us is not human.**

**And it left us a checksum.**

**And a checksum means somebody intends to read it again.**

---

I sat there a long time.

I remember the rain kept on.

I remember a trolley going along the corridor, the sound of wheels on that flooring.

I remember the family member at the next bed on the phone, talking about buying vegetables.

**And I sat there with an envelope that had grown very thick.**

---

Then my mother woke up.
