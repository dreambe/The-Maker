# 6.3 · Ruling Out: Horizontal Transfer

The second line.

One thing first: **of the eight, this is the one that looks most true.**

Not the easiest. Not the one I most wanted (that was the first).

**The one that looks most true.**

Because it has precedent. Because it has a mechanism. Because it happens every day. Because in my line of work, *this stretch came from somewhere else* is a normal, respectable conclusion with dozens of papers a year discussing it.

---

What the line means.

**Horizontal gene transfer:** a stretch of genetic material passes from one organism into another without reproduction.

This is not a conjecture. It is an observation.

Bacteria do it constantly — antibiotic resistance travels that way. It happens between eukaryotes too, only far more slowly and far more rarely.

And for humans the largest channel is **viruses.**

---

That channel has to be set out, because the whole force of this line is in it.

There is a class of virus called retroviruses. When one infects a cell, it does not break in, copy itself, and leave.

What it does is: **write its own genetic material into the host's genome.**

Once written, the stretch is there. Every time the host's cell divides, it is copied along with everything else.

And if the cell it infected happens to be a germ cell — that stretch passes to the next generation.

**And it goes on passing.**

---

How many times has that happened to humans?

**About a hundred thousand.**

Sequences of retroviral origin make up **eight per cent** of the human genome.

Eight per cent.

Set a number beside it: protein-coding genes make up **one and a half per cent** of the human genome.

**The viral wreckage in us outweighs our genes five to one.**

---

So the question is:

**Could that checksum-bearing stretch have come from a virus?**

And if it did — where did the checksum come from?

Answer: **from that virus's history.**

And a virus's history is a history of thousands of millions of years about which we know almost nothing. Viruses evolve orders of magnitude faster than their hosts; their sequences are compressed, optimised, rearranged over very short intervals.

**Could a sequence compressed a few thousand million times happen to carry a checksum relation?**

**I do not know.**

**And that is the force of this line: it moves the miracle off humans and onto something we do not understand.**

---

I spent four months.

Four things in four months.

---

**One: search everything.**

If that stretch came from elsewhere, elsewhere should show traces.

I compared those two hundred and forty-seven base pairs against everything I could get.

Everything meaning:

```
all sequenced viral genomes                      ~40 000
all sequenced bacterial and archaeal genomes     ~300 000
all sequenced eukaryotic genomes                 ~8 000
all environmental metagenomic datasets           ~14 000 datasets
all annotated human endogenous retroviruses      ~200 000
```

Six weeks of it. Three different algorithms, because one algorithm can have a systematic blind spot.

**Result: nothing.**

Not *weak homology.* Not *insufficient significance.*

**Nothing resembles it above random expectation.**

---

Why that result cannot prove anything by itself.

Because *not found* has two possible meanings:

**One, it did not come from elsewhere.**

**Two, it came from elsewhere, and that elsewhere no longer exists, or has never been sequenced.**

And the second is far from negligible. The proportion of extant species that have been sequenced is very small. And extinct species almost entirely lack sequence.

**So this step does not rule the line out.**

It says only: if it came from elsewhere, that elsewhere is not in our databases.

---

**Two: look for the fingerprints of a virus.**

This is the prettiest step of the four months, and it was mine.

A sequence of viral origin, even if all its homologues are extinct, should still carry **the marks of a virus.**

What marks?

**One: long terminal repeats.** A retrovirus inserting into a genome leaves a repeat at each end. That is the scar of its insertion mechanism.

**Two: target site duplication.** On insertion, the host genome produces a short repeat flanking the insertion point.

**Three: codon usage bias.** A virus's codon bias differs from its host's, and the statistical trace of that bias survives long after the sequence stops coding for anything.

**Four: GC content.** A virus's GC content usually differs from the region it inserts into, and that difference decays very slowly.

**Five: insertion site preference.** Retroviral insertion is not random; it prefers particular chromatin states and particular sequence motifs.

I checked all five.

---

Result:

```
one · long terminal repeats     none
two · target site duplication   none
three · codon usage bias        no difference from the surrounding region
four · GC content               no difference from the surrounding region
                                (0.003, far below the detection threshold)
five · insertion site preference  the site belongs to no known preference class
```

**All five: none.**

---

And this step is much stronger than the first.

Because the first is *no relatives found*, and no relatives found may mean the relatives died.

**And this one is *no scars on the body.***

If a thing was inserted, the act of insertion leaves marks. And the disappearance of marks takes time — and the degree of conservation of that sequence (1.3) shows it has barely changed in a few hundred thousand years.

**A few hundred thousand years is not enough to wear those marks away.**

---

**Three: ask it backwards.**

Three weeks on this, and it is the nearest I came in the whole of the Tracing Back to deceiving myself.

What I asked was: **is there a class of transfer mechanism we do not yet know that leaves no marks?**

And I found a candidate.

The candidate is **non-viral vectors of horizontal transfer**: certain exchanges of genetic material between bacteria and eukaryotes via conjugation, transformation, or extracellular vesicles, which do not necessarily leave insertion scars.

I followed it for three weeks.

And the reason it failed I want to set down, because the reason is small, technical, and decisive:

**The length distribution of what those mechanisms transfer is known.**

What they move has a median length of a few hundred to a few thousand base pairs, and a **lower bound** around eight hundred.

**Because those mechanisms require a minimum length of homologous arm to complete integration.**

**And that sequence is two hundred and forty-seven base pairs.**

**It is an order of magnitude shorter than the shortest fragment those mechanisms can move.**

---

**Four: compute its age.**

The last two weeks of the four months, and the real conclusion of this section.

If a sequence arrived by external transfer, that transfer happened at some moment.

And that moment can be estimated — from its divergence from homologues, or from its distribution across populations.

And this stretch has no homologues (step one).

**So I used the second way: its distribution across people.**

---

I pulled every human genome dataset I could get. At the time that was one hundred and ninety-four thousand individuals.

I checked the state of that sequence in all one hundred and ninety-four thousand.

Result:

```
carriers                                   194 000 (all)
sequence exactly identical                 193 991
sequence differing                         9
differing sites per person                 1
positions of the differing sites           different in each of the 9
does the difference affect the checksum     in all 9, yes — checksum fails
```

Nine people.

Of one hundred and ninety-four thousand, nine people whose checksum does not pass.

---

I want to stop here and write something that has nothing to do with data.

Those nine people are anonymous. All I could see was an identifier, a sex, an approximate geographic origin, and an age band.

I wrote the nine identifiers on a sheet of paper, and I have kept it.

I do not know why I kept it.

**I know that in those nine people, something is broken.**

And its being broken affects nothing about them — that sequence does nothing (1.3). They will live as long as they live and have as many children as they have.

**Those nine are the only nine people on this planet who do not carry that signature.**

**And they do not know.**

**And I know.**

**And I cannot tell them, because the data are anonymous, and because what would I tell them.**

---

Back to the data.

**Nine differences among one hundred and ninety-four thousand people.**

And that proportion can be used to estimate an age.

Because mutations at a site occur at a known rate. And accumulating to a given frequency in a population takes time.

I computed it.

**Result: the mutational load on that sequence corresponds to a time of 3.0 x 10^5 years.**

Three hundred thousand years.

---

And that number is this section's conclusion.

Because:

**If that stretch entered humanity by some transfer, that transfer must be older than three hundred thousand years.**

**And three hundred thousand years ago is when humans had just appeared.**

---

I want the logic very clear, because it is the most important step of inference in this whole chapter.

A horizontal transfer enters a species and then **fixes** — spreads to every individual of that species.

And fixation takes time — Marcus's expression at 5.5.

**At the human effective population size, under a strong selection pressure, fixation takes fifty thousand years.**

**And if it carries no selective advantage — and that sequence does nothing, so it has none — fixation takes 4Nₑ generations: about one million years.**

**And it has only had three hundred thousand.**

---

**So it did not have time to fix.**

**And it is fixed — one hundred and ninety-four thousand people, all carrying it.**

---

So the conclusion of this line:

```
it comes from no known source                            (step one)
it bears no marks of insertion                           (step two)
it is shorter than the lower bound of any known mechanism (step three)
and its age in humans is three hundred thousand years    (step four)
and three hundred thousand years is not enough to fix a neutral sequence
```

**Therefore it is not horizontal transfer.**

**Therefore its presence in the human genome is not something transferred in.**

**Therefore it has been there since the first day of this species.**

---

I finished that step in January, a Thursday afternoon.

I remember it snowed that day — the first snow of that winter, very light, gone as soon as it touched the sill.

I wrote the conclusion out on paper and then sat there.

Ilan was in the room that day, sorting Antarctic material.

I said, "Ilan."

He said, "Mm."

I said, "It didn't come from somewhere else."

He looked up.

I said, "It's been there since the first day."

He put down what he was holding.

He asked a question, and I thought about that question for years afterwards, because it is more precise than my four months of work.

He said:

"Which first day."

---

I said, "Meaning."

He said:

"You say it's been there since humanity's first day."

"Which day is humanity's first day."

"A species doesn't have a first day. A species slowly becomes that species."

He said:

"Unless it does."

---

**Line two: does not hold.**

**Time: four months.**

**Six remain.**

---

And the sheet with the nine identifiers is still with me.

It is in the same envelope as Lu Ting's page and the sheet with the eight lines.

That envelope is thick now.
