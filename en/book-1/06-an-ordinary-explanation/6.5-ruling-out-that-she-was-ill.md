# 6.5 · Ruling Out: That She Was Ill

The fourth line.

It took forty minutes.

**And it was the hardest stretch of the eleven months.**

---

Why it is on the table.

Because it has to be.

If a person repeatedly sees, in a stretch of sequence nobody has studied, something nobody else can see, then before she rewrites the history of the universe she should check something less laborious:

**whether there is something wrong with the person looking.**

Let me be clear: this line is not self-deprecation and it is not modesty.

**It is a real exclusion. It has real methods of testing. And I can perform none of them.**

I am a geneticist. I cannot administer a cognitive assessment. I cannot administer one to myself.

**So this is the only one of the eight I had to have somebody else do.**

---

I did it at the end of April.

The first line (unknown natural mechanism) was still running; the second had not begun. I brought this one forward because I wanted to know early — if there was something wrong with me, none of the rest needed doing.

---

I want to write the booking, because it took longer than the consulting room did.

I booked through the online system of the largest hospital in the city.

The system asks for: name, identity number, telephone, department, principal complaint.

The first four I filled in truthfully.

**On the fifth I sat for about twenty minutes.**

*Principal complaint* has a thirty-character limit.

I typed six versions.

**Version one:** *Suspected abnormality in own cognitive function.*

Deleted. Because *suspected* would file me under anxiety, and I am not anxious.

**Version two:** *Unexplained repeated judgment arising in work; require exclusion of cognitive impairment.*

Deleted. Because *unexplained*, in a clinic's context, means *delusional.*

**Version three:** *Poor sleep for six months, reduced attention.*

I looked at that one for a long time.

**Because it is true.**

My sleep has been very poor for six months. My attention has genuinely dropped in many respects — I missed a water bill; I once rode two stops past my own.

And it is true, and it is not what I am asking.

**What I am asking is whether the thing I see is there.**

**And this version asks whether I am tired.**

Deleted.

**Versions four** and **five** I do not remember.

**Version six** is what I submitted.

It was two words:

> **Cognitive assessment.**

---

The appointment was for the afternoon of the eleventh of May.

I arrived forty minutes early.

I sat on a chair on the ground floor of the building. The kind bolted in rows, plastic, cold to sit on.

Beside me were an elderly couple. He was holding a plastic bag with films in it. She was talking to him — household things: whose child had an exam, the tree downstairs needing cutting.

**He did not answer.**

**Every so often he nodded once.**

I sat there looking at my appointment slip.

What I was thinking was: the first time my mother went, I sat like this too. A different hospital, but the same chairs, cold, bolted in rows.

That time I took her. She sat beside me. She talked to me, and it was household things too.

**And I was thinking about something at work that day. I remember what it was: a grant deadline.**

**I nodded a few times.**

---

Number called. Third floor. The room is at the end of the corridor.

The doctor is a woman in her fifties, short hair, a dark jumper under the white coat.

On her desk: a computer, a stack of forms, a pen, a cup with pens in it (four; three black and one red), and a small plastic anatomical model.

She looked at the two words in the system, then looked up at me.

She said, "Booked forty minutes ago. You're here now."

I said, "I came early."

She said, "You wrote cognitive assessment. Who sent you."

I said, "Myself."

She said, "Has anyone told you there is something wrong with you."

I said, "No."

She put the pen down.

She said, "Then tell me."

---

I want to write what I said, because it was the first time I said this to a stranger, and the version I told was closer to the truth than anything I have written in any document.

I said:

"I work in genetics."

"I have found a structure in a stretch of human DNA. That structure should not be there."

"I have run it seventeen times. It is there every time."

"I had a colleague recompute it independently. It is there."

"I am now systematically excluding every possible explanation."

"And one of them is that the thing I see is not there."

"I do not know how to test that one on myself, so I came."

---

When I had finished she said nothing for about five seconds.

Then she said something I still remember.

She said:

"I don't hear that reason once a year."

She said, "Most people come because somebody sent them."

Then she began.

---

I looked the battery up afterwards. It has a name.

I set it down in order, because I went through it once and my mother has been through it at least three times.

---

**One: orientation.**

She asked: what is the date. What day of the week. What season. What city is this. What floor is this.

I said: the eleventh of May. Thursday. Spring, nearly summer. (The name of the city.) Third floor.

She ticked the form.

---

**Two: registration.**

She said, "I'll say three words. Repeat them. Then remember them; I'll ask again later."

The three words were:

**Apple. Coin. Table.**

I repeated them.

---

**Three: attention and calculation.**

She said, "Start at a hundred and subtract seven each time."

I said: ninety-three, eighty-six, seventy-nine, seventy-two, sixty-five.

She said, "That's enough."

---

**Four: recall.**

She said, "The three words."

I said: apple, coin, table.

She ticked.

---

**Five: naming, repetition, comprehension, reading, writing.**

She held up her watch and asked what it was. I said a watch.

She pointed at her pen. I said a pen.

She said, "Repeat after me," and gave me a phrase full of consonants. I repeated it.

She gave me a sheet with a three-step instruction. I did it.

She gave me a sheet reading *close your eyes.* I closed them.

She said, "Write a sentence. Any sentence, with a subject and a verb."

---

I want to stop here.

**I still remember the sentence I wrote, and I did not think about it. I just wrote.**

I wrote:

> **There is a tree outside the window.**

And outside that room's window there is no tree.

**Outside the window is the wall of another building.**

---

She looked at it, ticked the form (the criterion for that item is *subject and verb, grammatically complete*; content is not scored), and turned to the last page.

---

**Six: visuospatial.**

She turned the sheet round, pushed it to me, and said:

"Draw a clock."

"Round, with all twelve numbers, and set the hands at ten past eleven."

---

I picked up the pen.

---

I want to be precise about the next thirty seconds, because they are the only important thing in this section.

**I could not draw it.**

Not because I do not know how.

**Because I had seen the one my mother drew.**

---

Two years earlier. A different hospital, a different doctor, the same form, the same item six.

I took her. I sat in the chair beside her.

The doctor said, "Draw a clock."

My mother drew a circle. She drew it very well — she taught secondary school geography; she drew contour maps her whole life; her hand is steady.

That circle was extremely round.

Then she began the numbers.

She started at 12, at the top. Then 1, 2, 3.

Then 4.

Then 5.

**Then she stopped.**

Then she wrote 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11.

**All of them in the right half.**

The left half of the circle was empty. The numbers ran clockwise from 12, crowding tighter as they went, and by 11 they were pressed up against the 12.

**Twelve numbers, all in the right half.**

**And her circle was extremely round.**

---

I sat beside her and watched her draw it.

I did not speak.

The doctor did not speak either.

When she had finished my mother looked up at the doctor and said, "Is that right?"

The doctor said, "Yes. Very good."

Then the doctor put the sheet into the file.

---

That sheet is in that hospital's records office now.

And I have never looked at it again.

---

**Back to the eleventh of May.**

I held the pen and looked at the blank sheet.

The doctor was waiting.

She did not hurry me. She put her hands on the desk and looked at me.

**What I was thinking, in those thirty seconds, was: I should draw a normal clock.**

**And I understood at once that the thought itself was a problem.**

Because a normal person given this task does not think *I should draw a normal clock.*

**They think *ten past eleven; short hand between eleven and twelve; long hand on the two.***

**And what I was thinking about was my performance.**

---

So I did the thing I think, in retrospect, was the most correct thing I did that day.

**I put the pen down.**

I said, "I need to tell you something."

She said, "Go on."

I said:

"My mother did this test two years ago. This item."

"I sat beside her and watched her draw."

"All twelve of her numbers were in the right half."

I said:

"I am holding this pen now, and what I am thinking about is not a clock. What I am thinking about is whether I will draw it the way she did."

"I think I should tell you that, because it will affect the result of this item."

---

She looked at me for about three seconds.

Then she did something I did not expect.

**She took the sheet away.**

She pulled a fresh one from the stack and pushed it to me.

She said:

"You're right. It will affect the result."

"So we'll use a different sheet."

She said, "Draw it now. Think about your mother drawing it; that's all right. Just draw a clock."

---

I drew it.

My circle is not as round as my mother's.

My twelve numbers are evenly spaced.

My short hand is between eleven and twelve, my long hand on the two.

---

She looked, and ticked.

Then she put both sheets in my file — the blank one and the drawn one.

---

**Total: nineteen minutes.**

**Result: thirty out of thirty.**

She said, "You do not have cognitive impairment."

She said, "This battery is a screen. It will not catch anything very slight. If you want more precision there is a full neuropsychological assessment; it takes three hours and has to be booked."

I said, "Is it necessary."

She said:

"On today's performance, no."

"And given your reason for coming, I will book it if you want it."

---

I said I would leave it for now.

She nodded and began entering things into the system.

Halfway through she stopped and asked a question.

**It is not in the battery.**

She said:

"How are you sleeping."

---

I said, "Badly."

She said, "How long."

I said, "Fourteen months."

She said, "How many hours a night."

I said, "Three to four."

She said, "What time do you go to bed."

I said, "Sometimes two. Sometimes four."

She said, "Can you get back to sleep when you wake."

I said, "No."

She said, "What are you doing when you wake."

---

I said, "Arithmetic."

---

She took her hands off the keyboard.

She said:

"Dr Shen, what I am going to say now has nothing to do with cognitive assessment."

"Your test today is a perfect score. There is nothing wrong with your brain."

"And for fourteen consecutive months you have slept three to four hours a night and done arithmetic when you woke."

She said:

"You will work that thing out sooner or later."

"And your body may not still be here for it."

---

She prescribed two weeks of something.

One for sleep, one a vitamin.

She said, "The sleep one — don't be frightened of it; two weeks won't make you dependent. Sleep for two weeks and then come back."

---

**I did not take them.**

I want that clear, because it is not creditable and it is not something I recommend.

**I put the two boxes in the kitchen cupboard, with the bowls.**

I never took them.

They expired. Their shelf life is two years.

**The month they expired was the month I was in Antarctica.**

---

I came out of that building a little after four.

There was sun outside. May, the kind of sun that has begun to be warm and is not yet unpleasant.

I stood downstairs for a while.

**What I was thinking was: there are two sheets with clocks on them in this city.**

**One in this hospital's record system. Mine. Twelve numbers evenly spaced.**

**One in another hospital's records office. My mother's. Twelve numbers in the right half.**

**And her circle is better than mine.**

---

**Line four: does not hold.**

**Time: nineteen minutes (booking: twenty).**

**Four remain.**

---

I told nobody I had gone.

Not out of shame.

**Because if I had told them, this line would not have counted.**

An exclusion performed while somebody is watching is performed by somebody who, without meaning to, wants it to pass.

**I needed those nineteen minutes to contain only me and one person who did not know me.**

---

And one last thing.

That blank sheet, the doctor put in my file.

**She could have thrown it away.**

**She put it in.**

I still think that was the second decent thing I saw in those eleven months, after Lu Ting standing up.
