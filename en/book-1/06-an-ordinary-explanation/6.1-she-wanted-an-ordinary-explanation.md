# 6.1 · She Wanted an Ordinary Explanation

I want to put one thing at the head of this chapter, and it is a thing I never said to anyone during the whole of the Tracing Back, not to Lu Ting, not to Ilan.

**From the first time the checksum came out, I was hoping it was false.**

Not *maintaining scepticism.* Not *prudence.* Not the thing scientific training requires, where you work hard at refuting your own hypothesis.

**Hoping.**

Those are different things. A person can hope a thing is true and still work honestly at demolishing it — that is good research. What I was doing was something else: while I worked at demolishing it, there was something in me hoping I would succeed.

**And that hope is a source of contamination.**

I only admitted it much later. And this chapter is about how I handled that contamination — and the way I handled it was to make it into a table, and then work through the table, one line at a time, for eleven months.

---

First, why I was hoping.

Not because I was afraid of the truth. I am not. I have done this work all my life in order to know something nobody else knows yet.

**What I was afraid of was what I would turn into.**

---

I want to tell you about a man.

His surname was Zhou. When I was doing my master's, he was an associate researcher in another group in our building, thirty-eight, working on plant genomes.

The first time I met him was at the microwave in the corridor. He was heating a lunch box. He spoke to me, asked what I was working on, and I told him. He made one very sound suggestion about a parameter in a piece of software I was using at the time. The suggestion was right, and I used it afterwards.

He was a normal person. He was very intelligent. His hands had that look people who do a lot of bench work get, the nails pale at the edges.

And three years later I saw his name on an internet forum.

It was an eighteen-thousand-word essay. I remember the title because it was so long: *On Non-Random Structures in the Rice Genome and Their Fundamental Challenge to Current Molecular Evolutionary Theory (Part Seven).*

Part seven.

There were six before it.

---

I read all seven.

I want to be clear about one thing, because it is the exact source of my fear:

**A substantial part of those seven was correct.**

His data handling was clean. The non-random structure he had found was real — other people found it later, and there is a proper paper explaining it as a consequence of transposon insertion bias.

Where he went wrong was not the data.

**Where he went wrong was in deciding that the thing could no longer be explained within the existing framework.**

And at the moment he made that decision, he was right — at that moment the structure genuinely had no explanation.

**His problem was that he did not wait another three years.**

---

I asked about him afterwards. He did not go mad. He did not lose his job. He is still at that institute, still supervising students.

And nobody cites him any more.

And here is the harder thing: he is still writing. Part fourteen came out four years ago.

I read it.

In part fourteen, the originally sound data handling is gone. The whole essay derives downward from the conclusions of part nine, and part nine derives from part seven.

**He built a tower. The first storey is correct.**

**And he has never gone back to look at the first storey.**

---

That is what I was afraid of.

I am not afraid of being wrong. People in my line are wrong every day; three years down a wrong problem is ordinary.

**I am afraid that from some moment onward I will stop going back to look at the first storey.**

---

So from the morning after the seventeenth run, I fixed one thing for myself.

I fixed this: **I would give every ordinary explanation of this thing a real chance.**

Not for form. Not one sentence in the discussion section saying *we excluded the possibility of contamination.*

**Actually do it. Actually spend the time. Actually hope it holds.**

Because if an ordinary explanation holds, then I can go back.

Back to my work. Back to my grant applications. Back to visiting my mother every Wednesday.

**I am thirty-nine. I could have a normal second half of my life.**

---

I want to write one entirely honest sentence here, because without it this chapter is a lie.

**If any of those ordinary explanations had held, I would have been relieved.**

**Not disappointed. Relieved.**

That is not a scientist's reaction.

And it was mine.

---

I made the table on the seventeenth of April, three days after Lu Ting said the word *deployment.*

I made it alone in that eighteen-square-metre room, in the evening, after the others had gone.

I made it crudely: I drew a two-column table on a sheet of A4.

The heading of the left column I wrote as:

> **If this thing is false, where is it false**

The right column:

> **How to check**

---

I copy that sheet here.

I still have it, together with that page of Lu Ting's manuscript. It has been folded many times and some of the writing along the folds is no longer legible.

Eight lines.

> **One · An unknown natural mechanism.**
> There is some molecular mechanism we do not yet know that can produce this relation in a sequence.
> **Check:** list every known mechanism that produces non-random sequence structure, and compute for each whether it can produce a summation relation modulo p. If none can, look for mechanisms that are theoretically possible and not yet observed.

> **Two · Horizontal gene transfer.**
> That stretch came from another organism — a virus, a bacterium, some vector. The checksum came with that source, and we do not know that source's own history.
> **Check:** search everything. Homology across every database. If it came from elsewhere, elsewhere should show traces.

> **Three · Modern human agency.**
> Somebody did this in a laboratory, and the sample got into a public database, or into my laboratory.
> **Check:** every relevant experimental record for seventy years. The provenance chain of every dataset I have used. Every operation in my own laboratory.

> **Four · Statistical error.**
> There is something wrong with my algorithm. My significance testing is contaminated by multiple comparisons. Searching a few thousand million bases for a pattern, finding a "significant" one is inevitable.
> **Check:** redo the whole of the statistics. Have somebody who does not know what I am looking for recompute it independently.

> **Five · I am ill.**
> I have a cognitive problem I am not aware of. What I see is not there.
> **Check:** see a doctor.

> **Six · A hoax.**
> Somebody has set this up for me.
> **Check:** compute what the setup would require. Who. How much money. What access.

> **Seven · My definition is wrong.**
> When I imported the notion of a checksum from information theory, I imported it wrongly. I take it to be a rare property, and it is not.
> **Check:** find a mathematician. (This one was eventually done by Marcus.)

> **Eight · Coincidence.**
> It is simply coincidence. The universe is large, the samples are many, everything happens.
> **Check:** compute the probability. Three times. (I had computed it at 1.6. And I would compute it again, because that time I did it alone, and that time I wanted it to be significant.)

---

Eight.

I looked at that sheet for a long time that evening.

I remember what the room looked like: the fluorescent tube, the whiteboard, the gap in the heading that was cleaner than everything around it, the join between the two desks.

I remember I was holding a paper cup of instant coffee that had gone cold, with a brown ring inside it.

At the very bottom of the sheet I wrote a line.

That line is not in the table. It is below it, at the very bottom edge, smaller than the rest.

It reads:

> **If all eight of these are ruled out, I have nowhere to go back to.**

---

Two things to end this section.

---

**First.**

Those eight lines took me eleven months.

Not full time — the Tracing Back had other business; Ilan needed people in Antarctica, Lu Ting's fossil database had to be built, Ye Qi's forgetting line had to be followed, Marcus needed somebody to find him sequencing data.

But I did not skip one of the eight.

The second took me five months.

The fifth took me forty minutes — nineteen of them in a consulting room, and the rest on the bus there and back.

**And those forty minutes were the hardest stretch of the eleven months.**

---

**Second.**

I want to say this in advance, because I do not want to repeat it in every section that follows.

**I did not succeed in ruling out any of the eight.**

All eight fail.

And what this chapter is about is not the result *I ruled out eight things.*

**What this chapter is about is the eleven months.**

Because how a person rules a thing out matters more than what they ruled out.

And because — I still think this — **if I had done any one of those eight less than honestly, none of what came after counts.**

What came after includes: the thing under the Antarctic ice, the forty-one hours, the second layer that Marcus solved, and the sentence I heard in a room when I was forty-one.

**All of that stands on these eleven months.**

---

And the meaning of the eleven months is not what they proved.

**It is that they let me go back and look at the first storey.**

**And I looked.**

**And the first storey was still there.**
