# 6.4 · Ruling Out: Modern Human Agency

The third line.

It is the simplest of the eight, and the second longest in time spent.

**Simple, because the answer was clear from the start.**

**Long, because I will not end a thing on the word *clear.***

---

What the line means.

If the checksum in that sequence is real, and not naturally produced, the least troublesome explanation is:

**somebody did it.**

And if *somebody* means a modern person, then it happened within the last few decades, in a laboratory, by one or several people whose names can be spoken.

And there are two paths by which it could reach my data:

**Path one:** somebody actually edited a human germ line; that person's descendants carry the sequence; and samples from those people entered public databases.

**Path two:** somebody synthesised the stretch in a laboratory, and it entered some sequencing pipeline as contamination, and thence a database.

---

Path one first, because it is easier to rule out, and the logic is hard.

---

**What it takes to insert a specified sequence of two hundred and forty-seven base pairs at a specified position in the human genome.**

I made a list of the required technologies, with the year each first became capable of it.

```
one · knowing where the position is
    requires: a human genome reference
    first available: 2001 (draft) / 2003 (finished)

two · being able to read the sequence at that position
    requires: directed sequencing
    first available: 1977

three · being able to synthesise the sequence
    requires: oligonucleotide synthesis, length >= 247
    first available: about 1990 (segmented synthesis and ligation)

four · being able to get it into a cell
    requires: transfection or viral vector
    first available: the 1970s

five · being able to insert it at a specified position rather than at random
    requires: a targeted nuclease
    first available: zinc-finger nucleases about 1996
                     TALENs 2010
                     CRISPR-Cas9 2012

six · being able to do it in the germ line rather than in somatic cells
    requires: operating on a zygote or early embryo
    first done in humans: 2018 (once, unsanctioned, two individuals)

seven · being able to spread it to all of humanity
    requires: —
```

---

For the seventh I wrote a dash.

Because it does not require technology. It requires time.

And the time it requires is what Marcus's expression at 5.5 gives: a neutral sequence fixing in a population of human size takes about a million years.

**And targeted editing has existed for under thirty.**

---

So path one dies at **item seven.**

Not on the technology — technologically, after 2012, doing that thing is feasible; a trained person in a well-equipped laboratory could produce it in a few months.

**It dies on the spreading.**

To have one hundred and ninety-four thousand people all carrying it, you cannot edit one hundred and ninety-four thousand embryos.

You have to let it spread by itself.

**And spreading takes a million years.**

---

And there is a smaller, harder point here that I want to set down, because it is the one step in this line I find beautiful.

**Of the one hundred and ninety-four thousand people, nine fail the checksum (6.3).**

And the differing site is a different one in each of the nine.

**If the stretch were recently inserted, where would those nine differences come from?**

A newly inserted sequence would show no differences among its carriers — they are all copies of one template, and mutation has not had time to accumulate.

**Nine differences at nine different sites are the trace of time.**

**And that time is three hundred thousand years (6.3).**

**And the upper bound on modern agency is thirty years.**

**Four orders of magnitude apart.**

---

Path one: does not hold.

Time: one week.

---

Now path two: contamination.

That took four weeks.

And for three of those four weeks I was not working on data.

---

The data week first.

Contamination has one property, and that property saved me a great deal of time:

**Contamination is local.**

One batch in one laboratory gets contaminated, and that batch's samples carry the stretch. And another laboratory, another batch, another country, another decade does not.

So what I did was direct:

**I grouped the one hundred and ninety-four thousand samples by provenance, and looked at the occurrence of that sequence in each group.**

```
by sequencing centre       41 centres
by year                    1998 to 2021
by platform                7 platforms
by sample type             blood, buccal, tissue, cell line
by geographic origin       26 population groups
```

If it were contamination, some groups should be at 100 per cent and the rest at zero.

**Result: every group, 100 per cent (apart from the nine).**

Forty-one centres. Twenty-three years. Seven platforms. Four sample types. Twenty-six populations.

**Not one grouping shows the signature of contamination.**

---

And that is still not enough.

Because of one possibility: **what if the contaminant is something everybody uses.**

A reagent. A vector. A standard. A control sample every laboratory runs.

Then the contamination would be global, and the analysis above would not see it.

---

So I did the thing that actually settles it.

**I went to ancient DNA.**

---

The logic is very clean.

**If that sequence is also present in somebody who died long ago, and that person died before any modern reagent existed, then contamination is closed.**

And ancient DNA can be sequenced. In thirty-odd years that technique has gone from impossible to routine.

And more importantly — this is the core of this section — **ancient DNA carries a signature that cannot be forged.**

---

That signature is **deamination damage.**

When an organism dies, its DNA begins to degrade. And the degradation is not random; it has a very specific chemistry:

**Cytosine (C) deaminates to uracil (U), which is read as thymine (T) in sequencing.**

And that reaction occurs most at the **ends** of DNA fragments, because bases at the ends are single-stranded and exposed.

Which produces something measurable:

**The frequency of C→T substitution decays exponentially from the fragment end inward.**

And the slope of that decay is related to the sample's age and its conditions of preservation.

---

Why it cannot be forged.

To forge it you would need:

**One, to produce C→T substitutions at the right positions in the right proportion.**

**Two, to make that proportion decay exponentially with distance along the fragment.**

**Three, to keep the frequency of every other substitution class at background.**

**Four, to have all of this hold on every fragment you synthesise, with every fragment a different length.**

And if you damage DNA chemically, the pattern you get is **different** — artificial deamination is uniformly distributed, not end-enriched.

**Because end-enrichment comes from single-stranded exposure, and that requires a real, slow, in-the-ground degradation.**

**You cannot hurry it.**

---

So what I did was:

**Take every ancient human genome dataset I could get; see whether the sequence is present; and check the deamination gradient on those reads.**

The ancient samples then available at sufficient coverage numbered a little over fourteen hundred. Ages from four hundred years to forty-five thousand.

---

Result:

```
samples covering that region                        1 407
sequence present                                    1 407
checksum passes                                     1 404
checksum fails                                      3
reads showing a deamination gradient                1 407 (all)
correlation of gradient slope with sample age       0.88
```

**One thousand four hundred and seven. All of them.**

The oldest is forty-five thousand years.

That is a man found beside a river in Siberia. In his femur, the sequence is present, the checksum passes, and the C→T substitutions on those reads decay exponentially from the fragment ends at a slope consistent with forty-five thousand years.

---

One more thing, because when I had computed that slope I did something I thought afterwards was silly.

**I computed it a second time.**

Not because I doubted the first.

**Because I wanted to look at it again.**

On the screen that curve is a clean exponential decay. It starts at the first base of the fragment, goes inward, decays, decays, and by about the tenth base it is at background.

**That is forty-five thousand years, drawn as a line.**

I looked at it for about five minutes.

---

**Path two: does not hold.**

---

And now the three weeks.

---

For those three weeks I was in archives.

What I was looking for was: **in the past seventy years, has anybody performed directed, intentional, systematic operations on human genetic material.**

I was looking because I wanted path one done properly. A technology list can only prove *could not.* I wanted to know whether anybody had **tried.**

So I looked. Ethics review records in a number of countries; retracted literature; misconduct investigation reports; declassified research archives from several states; and the transfer inventories of institutions that have since closed.

---

**In those three weeks I found nothing connected to that sequence.**

**And I found other things.**

---

I am going to write this passage with restraint, because I am not a historian, because what I read was secondary material, and because I do not intend, in a record about myself, to pass judgment on anybody else's life.

So I write only the **classes** of thing I read. No details, no names, no institutions.

What I read was:

**One: compulsory sterilisation programmes in a number of countries in the first half of the twentieth century. With statutory basis, with execution data, with identifiers for those on whom they were executed. Totals in the hundreds of thousands.**

**Two: a number of medical studies conducted on particular populations without informed consent. Durations measured in decades.**

**Three: a number of breeding programmes conducted in full knowledge, with the improvement of humanity as their stated purpose. With archives. With forms inside the archives. With columns on the forms.**

---

I read for three weeks.

And I want to set down the thought I had when I had finished, because it is the last sentence of this section, and because it has to do with my four months, and with my whole working life.

---

**I spent four weeks proving that humanity is not capable of doing that thing.**

**And humanity is indeed not capable of doing that thing.**

**And before humanity was capable of that, it was already doing the things it was capable of.**

---

**Line three: does not hold.**

**Time: five weeks.**

**Five remain.**

---

Going home that evening I was not thinking about the checksum.

I was thinking about the forms.

The forms were printed. Which means somebody designed the layout, and somebody printed it, and somebody bound the sheets into volumes, and somebody wrote in them.

**That is a process.**

**A process needs many people.**

**And each person fills in only their own column.**
