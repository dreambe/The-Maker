# 6.6 · The Ex-Husband

This section is not on the table.

I write it here because it happened between the fourth line and the fifth, and because it changed the direction of the whole thing — more than any of the eight.

---

My ex-husband is called Pei Yan.

We married in 2014 and divorced in 2019.

One sentence about that marriage, only one, because it bears on this section:

**We did not divorce because something happened.**

No betrayal, no quarrels, no moment you can point at and say *it started there.*

We divorced because one year the two of us worked out how many hours we had been at home, both awake, at the same time.

**About six hundred hours in a year.**

And when we worked that number out we were sitting on opposite sides of the same table, each looking at our own screen.

Neither of us said anything.

**About two months later he raised it.**

I remember the sentence he used, because it was very like him:

He said, "He, the loads on this structure are fine. It's the connections that are fatiguing."

I laughed.

Then I said all right.

---

He is a bridge engineer.

He does structural design for very large bridges — main spans of several hundred metres, across rivers, across sea.

He is away half the year. There are tens of thousands of photographs on his phone and ninety-nine per cent of them are close-ups of concrete and reinforcement.

One feature of that profession has to be stated, because the whole of this section depends on it:

**A bridge engineer's professional habit is to assume the worst case.**

Someone in basic research asks first: *is this true.*

**Someone who builds bridges asks first: *if this is true, what then.***

Because a bridge cannot wait until every case is settled. He has to assume the worst wind, the worst earthquake, the worst load, the worst batch of material, and then design something that will not fall down under any of them.

**He spends his life handling *if.***

---

I contacted him on the fourteenth of May.

Three days after I came out of that hospital.

Why him.

Not because I needed comfort. I did not, and he is not good at it.

**Because I needed one person to hear the whole of it, from the beginning.**

And that person had to satisfy three conditions:

**One, he had to be able to follow the technical content — at least the logical structure.**

**Two, he could not be in my field.** Because somebody in my field begins, on hearing it, to think about what it means for him, and then he stops listening.

**Three, he had to not be afraid of me.**

The third is hardest.

If a person wants something from me — a student, a colleague, a subordinate — then while listening he is thinking about something else.

**And a man who has divorced me wants nothing from me.**

---

I sent a message.

I wrote:

> Where are you. I have a work thing I want to tell you about. It'll take a long time, maybe two hours. You don't have to give me advice.

He answered fast.

He wrote:

> Back day after tomorrow. Home Sunday afternoon. Come over. Is two hours enough.

---

I want to write his flat, because it bears on this.

East side of the city, a nineties block, sixth floor, no lift.

It is the flat we used to live in. It went to him in the divorce — not because I gave it up, but because he bought it before we married and it was always his.

The last time I went through that door was four years earlier.

And the flat has hardly changed.

The furniture is the same. The photograph of the two of us in Qinghai that hung on the wall has been taken down; the nail is still there.

And one thing has changed.

**There is nothing on the dining table.**

When we lived there that table always had things on it — my papers, his drawings, two laptops, several cups.

And that afternoon the table was empty.

Wiped.

**He had cleared the table because I was coming.**

---

I talked for two hours and forty minutes.

I started at that four in the morning, the seventeenth run.

I told him about the sequence, the checksum, how I computed the probability, the second person and the third and the seventh, the conference, Lu Ting's drawer, the gap in the fossil record, the email, the point under the Antarctic ice still spending energy, the forty-one items on the whiteboard, Marcus's zero point four one, the word *deployment*, the table of eight lines, lines one and two and three, and that three days earlier I had come out of a hospital with a perfect score.

He did not interrupt once.

He got up once, went to the kitchen, brought back two glasses of water, put them on the table, sat down, and said, "Go on."

---

When I finished, the light outside had changed. A May afternoon: the light at four and the light at six are different colours, and the six o'clock one is yellower.

I finished and drank the water. It had gone cold.

He did not speak at once.

**The first thing he did was take a sheet of paper and a pencil out of the drawer of that table.**

It is a very old mechanical pencil, 0.5. He has always used those.

He drew something.

---

He drew a box, and above the box he wrote:

> **checksum**

Then he drew an arrow into it on the left, and an arrow out of it on the right.

Then he looked up and asked the question.

---

He said:

**"What is this thing checking."**

---

I said, "It checks the sequence itself. It shows the sequence was deliberately written."

He said, "No."

I said, "No what."

He said:

"What you just described is a signature."

"A signature and a checksum are two different things."

---

He turned the sheet round to face me.

Under the box he wrote two lines.

The first:

> **Signature: proves *I made this.***

The second:

> **Checksum: proves *this thing is not broken.***

---

He said:

"A signature is for other people. Its function is attribution."

"A checksum is not for other people."

"A checksum is for **whoever reads it.**"

He tapped the box with the pencil.

He said:

"He. I build bridges. There is a thing on a bridge exactly like this."

---

He said the following. I set it down as nearly as I can.

He said:

"A large bridge, once built, has sensors embedded at critical points. Strain gauges, accelerometers, displacement transducers, thermometers. On a bridge with an eight-hundred-metre main span there might be several hundred."

"Those sensors feed a system that reads them twenty-four hours a day."

"And every channel has a check on it."

"Why does it need a check?"

"Because sensors fail. Cables break. Signals drift. And a failed sensor does not stop producing data — it goes on producing data, and the data are wrong."

"So we have to be able to distinguish *this number is real* from *this number is broken.*"

"That is what the check is for."

---

He said:

"So the existence of a checksum tells you one thing."

**"It tells you somebody intends to read it again."**

---

I want to stop here.

Because sitting on the other side of that table, when I heard that sentence, something in my head came down.

Not refuted.

**Rearranged.**

---

For fourteen months I had been treating that checksum as a **mark.**

A mark is static. It is cut there, it proves something happened, and then it sits there.

The question I had been asking was: **who cut it, when, why.**

**And a checksum is not a mark.**

**A checksum is a functional component.**

The existence of a functional component means there is a process that needs it.

And the process a checksum serves is **reading.**

---

**So the sequence is not there in order to say *I made you.***

**It is there so that something, at some point, can check whether that sequence is broken.**

---

I said that. I said it badly, and it took about three minutes.

He listened and nodded.

Then he said:

"Right. And that raises three questions."

He wrote three lines on the sheet.

---

> **One. Who reads.**

He said:

"A checksum has to have something that reads it. That thing has to know the algorithm — which encoding, what weighting, what modulus."

"You worked it out, so you know. It took you fourteen months."

"And whatever wrote it **knew.**"

"So the question is: who was it expecting to read it."

"Was it expecting you? Or is it planning to come back and read it itself?"

---

> **Two. When does it get read.**

He said:

"Sensors on a bridge are read continuously, tens of times a second."

"Some checks are one-off — a factory test, once, and never read again."

"Some are periodic — once a year, or once a decade."

"And some are **event-triggered** — read only when something happens."

He looked up.

He said:

"Three hundred thousand years, and it has never once been read."

"So either it is one-off (and it has expired), or it is periodic (and the period is longer than three hundred thousand years), or it is event-triggered."

---

> **Three. What happens if the check fails.**

He said:

"This is the one that matters."

"The purpose of a check is not knowing. It is **disposition.**"

"When you find a bad channel you do one of three things: repair the sensor, discard that channel's data, or take the bridge out of service."

"A checksum with no disposition behind it is meaningless — you spent something on checking, the check failed, and then you do nothing. Why did you check."

He put the pencil down.

He said:

"So, He."

"That thing put a checksum in the human genome."

"And behind a checksum there has to be a disposition."

---

**"Your nine people."**

---

I said, "What."

He said:

"You said that of one hundred and ninety-four thousand people, nine fail the checksum."

"What happened to those nine."

I said, "Nothing happened. They are normal people. That sequence does nothing."

He said:

"And what does that tell you."

---

He answered it himself.

He said:

"It tells you the disposition **has not happened yet.**"

"Not that there is no disposition."

"That it is not yet time for it."

---

We did not go further that day.

Not because there was nowhere to go.

**Because both of us understood, at the same moment, that going further would take us somewhere neither of us had any standing to be.**

He pushed the sheet across.

On it: a box, two lines, three questions.

He said, "Keep it."

---

He came down to the entrance with me.

That stairwell is the nineties kind, no windows, a sound-activated light; you have to stamp for it to come on.

We stood at the door.

He said one thing, entirely unrelated to the rest of this section, and I set it down because it is the only sentence about us from that day.

He said:

"You talked for two hours and forty minutes."

He said:

"In five years you never once talked that long."

---

I said, "There wasn't anything like this then."

He said, "Mm."

Then he said:

"Or I didn't ask."

---

I went down.

At the gate of the block I looked back up at the sixth floor.

His light was on.

**And I knew the dining table was empty.**

---

That sheet is still with me.

It is in the same envelope as Lu Ting's page, the table of eight, and the sheet with the nine identifiers.

**And it is the only sheet in that envelope written by neither me nor anyone in our group.**

---

Those three questions became the Tracing Back's three directions.

**Who reads was Marcus's line. He solved the second layer.**

**When does it get read was Ilan's line. He went to find out whether that structure was waiting for an event.**

**What happens if the check fails —**

That one we never answered.

**And when I was forty-one, in a very small, very dry room under the Antarctic ice, I asked it out loud.**

**And it answered.**
