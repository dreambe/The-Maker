# 6.2 · Ruling Out: An Unknown Natural Mechanism

The first line.

This is the one I wanted most.

Let me say why: because it requires nobody to have done anything.

It requires only that nature be more complicated than we know — and nature has always been that.

---

I have to explain what I was looking for, because the whole of this section's work depends on the exact definition of that thing, and for the first two months I was in fact looking for the wrong thing.

---

At 1.4 I explained to a student what a checksum is, using a supermarket receipt. That example is enough to convey a concept. It is not enough to do the work in this section.

So here is the accurate version.

**A *pattern* in a sequence and a *relation* in a sequence are two entirely different things.**

**A pattern** is: some local structure in the sequence occurs at a frequency that departs from random expectation.

Example: the frequency with which the bases C and G occur adjacent is markedly below random expectation in vertebrate genomes. That is a pattern.

Its cause is known: a methylated C readily deaminates to T, so CG is slowly worn away.

**That is a pattern. It is a statistical departure.**

And the patterns nature can produce are beyond counting. Transposons insert with bias. Recombination has hot spots. Replication slips. Palindromes form hairpins. Segments repeat in tandem. Codons have usage bias. Nucleosome occupancy is periodic. GC content has large-scale block structure.

**All of those are patterns.**

**And what I had found is not a pattern.**

---

**A relation** is: the value of one part of the sequence equals some function of another part of the sequence.

The exact form of that checksum:

Those two hundred and forty-seven base pairs, read under a fixed encoding (A=0, C=1, G=2, T=3), give a run of numbers.

The first two hundred and forty numbers, weighted by position and summed, then reduced modulo a certain number.

**The residue equals the value of the last seven numbers read under the same encoding.**

---

I want the difference clear with a non-specialist example, because it is the key to this whole section, and because it is the thing I had to explain over and over in every external briefing the Tracing Back ever gave.

**A pattern is: *the letter e occurs unusually rarely in this book.***

That could have many causes: the author's habits, a property of the language, a printing error, or the author doing it deliberately (one novel actually managed it).

**A relation is: *the last sentence of this book equals the total number of words in every sentence before it.***

For the first, you can imagine many natural causes.

**For the second, you cannot.**

Because it requires the last part of the book to *know* the content of everything before it.

And between the last page of a book and the pages before it there is no physical channel by which the information *how many words there are so far* could travel.

**Unless somebody counted.**

---

So the mechanism I was looking for is not *a mechanism that produces non-random patterns.*

**What I was looking for is: a mechanism that establishes an arithmetic relation between two parts of a sequence.**

And that mechanism has to be able to **sum.**

---

It took me two months to get the question right.

Those two months were not wasted — I made a table of every known molecular mechanism that produces sequence structure, forty-seven entries. That table later became an internal Tracing Back document and other people use it.

And of those forty-seven, only one class can produce a **relation.**

---

That class is: **secondary structure constraint.**

And this is the one thing in those five months that genuinely excited me.

I want to set it out, because it is the body of this section, and because it very nearly worked.

---

Take a stretch of RNA.

It is single-stranded. And single-stranded RNA folds on itself — because bases along the chain can pair complementarily: A with U, G with C.

So a stretch of single-stranded RNA takes on a three-dimensional structure: stems, loops, hairpins, pseudoknots.

And that structure has function. The cloverleaf of transfer RNA, the active centre of a ribozyme, the scaffold of ribosomal RNA — all of it depends on that folding.

**And here is the crucial thing:**

**If a sequence's folded shape is maintained by natural selection, then correlations arise between bases far apart along that sequence.**

Because for position 3 to pair with position 214, those two positions must match. If position 3 changes, position 214 must change with it, or the structure comes apart.

**That is a long-range, non-local correlation arising from a constraint between two distant ends of a sequence.**

**And it is entirely natural.**

It has a name in biology: **covariation.** It is a mature, widely used phenomenon — people have been using it to infer RNA secondary structure for forty years.

---

What I did in those five months was ask one question:

**Can covariation produce a summation relation.**

---

I want to set down the arithmetic, because it is the most beautiful piece of work I have ever done, and its conclusion is no.

Let the sequence have N positions.

Let its folding produce M pairs.

Each pair is one constraint: position i must be complementary to position j.

**Can those M constraints express, numerically, *the weighted sum of the first 240 positions, modulo p, equals the last 7 positions*?**

---

The answer is no, and the reason is very clean.

**The form of a covariation constraint is one-to-one.**

One constraint involves **two** positions. It says *i and j must match.*

And the form of a summation relation is **many-to-one.**

It says *the weighted sum of two hundred and forty positions equals the value of seven positions.*

---

Those two forms belong to different classes mathematically.

The totality of one-to-one constraints constitutes a **matching** (each position participates in at most one pair).

And what a matching can express is bounded: M constraints, each removing one degree of freedom.

**And a summation relation is a linear form; it compresses two hundred and forty variables into one value.**

**To express it with one-to-one constraints, you would need all two hundred and forty positions paired onto those seven.**

**And those seven positions can participate in seven pairings.**

---

**Seven positions, at most seven constraints.**

**And constraining the sum of two hundred and forty positions requires at least two hundred and forty constraints landing on those seven.**

**Seven is not two hundred and forty.**

---

That is it.

That is the result of five months, in a sentence.

**RNA secondary structure constraint produces long-range correlation, and the correlation it produces is of pairing type, not summation type.**

**And the relation in that sequence is of summation type.**

**Therefore it is not secondary structure constraint.**

---

I want to write down the day I finished that step, because on that day I did something unlike me.

Early September, a Tuesday, afternoon.

In that room, with about fourteen sheets of paper spread on the desk.

I finished the last step and put the pen down.

Then I sat for about two minutes.

Then I stacked those fourteen sheets, one at a time, very neatly, edge to edge, corner to corner.

Then I tore them.

---

Not into pieces. I folded the stack in half and tore it along the fold, into two halves.

Then I stacked the two halves and tore again.

Four.

Then I stopped.

I looked at the four stacks on the desk.

**I realised what I was doing, and I stopped.**

I squared the four stacks up, put a staple through one corner (it would not go through; I did it twice), and put them in a manila folder.

That file is still in the Tracing Back archive. Its first page is torn, and the tear down the middle is mine.

On the folder I wrote a line:

> **Line one · unknown natural mechanism · does not hold**

> **Enclosed: the whole of those five months. The tear was made by the operator and has no experimental significance.**

---

On the bus home that evening I was thinking about something very small.

I was thinking: if the covariation arithmetic had gone through, what would I be doing tomorrow.

I had worked it out. The answer is: tomorrow I would write a paper, called something like *A long-range covariation structure in a human non-coding sequence, with functional conjectures.* I would submit it to a middling journal, it would be accepted, and I would become somebody with a small reputation in that area.

That paper would get about forty citations.

I would use it to apply for a grant, and the grant would come through.

I would take on two students.

**I would stay in that grey building until I retired, and I would never go to Antarctica.**

---

I thought about that on the bus, very clearly, in detail, all the way to my stop.

Then I got off.

That evening I cooked noodles. I stood by the pan waiting for the water.

**And I think I learned something about myself that day:**

**It is not that I was hoping those eight lines would hold.**

**It is that I was hoping they would hold and hoping they would not, at the same time.**

**And both of those existed in me at once, and the ratio was not fifty-fifty.**

---

I do not know what the ratio was.

**And I know I never once changed a number to make it come out.**

That is the one thing about my life I am certain of.

---

**Line one: does not hold.**

**Time: five months.**

**Seven remain.**
