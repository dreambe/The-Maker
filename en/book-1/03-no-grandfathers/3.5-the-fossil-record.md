# 3.5 · The Fossil Record

When I got back I started reading palaeontology.

I am writing this passage because it is the least flattering of my year, and more important than any other.

---

I am thirty-nine. I have done sixteen years of research, in four institutions in two countries. I have nineteen papers. My reading rate, in my own field, is twenty or thirty abstracts a day, five or six full texts, of which two or three I read as far as the methods.

The first palaeontology paper took me three hours.

**Three hours on one paper, and at the end I had not understood the figure legends.**

---

I want to be concrete about those three hours, because *did not understand* is too vague, and it conceals something important.

The paper was the first on the list Lu Ting gave me. 2011. Morphometrics on a set of specimens.

I started with the abstract. I understood the abstract — abstracts are always understandable; abstracts are written for everyone.

Then the introduction. Eighty per cent. A few unfamiliar terms, which I looked up.

Then materials and methods.

**I stopped there for two hours.**

Not because it is difficult.

**Because it assumes so much.**

---

Some examples.

The paper says specimens are counted by `MNI`.

I did not know what `MNI` is. I looked it up: Minimum Number of Individuals. Fossils are broken; you dig up seven left humeri and three right; how many individuals do you have? At least seven. It is a basic concept.

**In my field, one sample is one sample. It has never in my life occurred to me that *how many individuals* might be a question requiring a method.**

The paper says all measurements are taken under the `Martin` numbering system.

I did not know what that is. I looked it up: a system of anthropometric landmark numbering established in 1914; every identifiable anatomical position on a skull has a number; the whole world has used the same one for over a century.

**The same one for over a century.**

I sat on that line for a long time.

In my field, naming systems are overturned every seven or eight years on average. The genome annotation build I use changed last year.

The paper says dates are by `OSL`, with `1σ` errors in Table 2.

`OSL`: optically stimulated luminescence dating. The principle: electrons excited by radiation and trapped in a mineral lattice are released, emitting light, when the mineral is exposed to light; the accumulated electron count is proportional to burial time. Which is to say — **you are measuring when this grain of sand last saw the sun.**

I stopped on that line and sat for about five minutes.

Not because I could not follow it.

**Because it is so beautiful.**

A person picks up a grain of sand and asks it: what year did you last see the sun.

And the sand answers.

Sixteen years in my own field, and I have not met an idea that beautiful.

---

Three hours in, I got to Figure 3.

Figure 3 is a scatterplot. One measurement ratio on the horizontal, another on the vertical, one point per specimen, point shape for horizon, point colour for region, point size for `MNI`.

The legend is over a hundred and thirty words and contains eleven abbreviations.

I looked up every one and went back to the figure.

I understood what the figure said.

**And I could not see why it mattered.**

What a figure says and why it matters are two things. The first you can look up. The second takes ten years in a field.

In that second I understood, for the first time, what I look like to other people.

---

Here is the thing.

I stood in that hall and talked about checksums for twenty-two minutes, and then privately blamed those forty-odd people for *not catching it.*

And half of that room does palaeontology, morphology, stratigraphy.

They listened to me on hexadecimal characters, on deamination damage slopes, on third-order Markov background models.

**They understood what I was saying, and they could not see why it mattered.**

**The same act as me and Figure 3.**

And I had privately blamed them for two months.

---

I wrote Lu Ting an email.

I revised it six times. I did not want to look ignorant, and I had to ask. What I sent was this:

*Professor Lu, I am reading the first paper you gave me. A basic question: how is the discrepancy between MNI and the actual number of individuals handled in estimates of morphological rate? I am afraid I have misunderstood it.*

Having sent it, I thought the question was stupid.

---

He replied.

Four hours later. Over two thousand words.

He started at the beginning. What `MNI` is, why it exists, its three algorithms, the direction of bias in each, and which way that bias pushes a rate estimate.

He attached two hand-drawn figures, photographed — pencil on a sheet of A4, lettered.

At the end he wrote:

*This question is not stupid. It is one of the real problems in this field; it was argued over in the sixties and argued again in the two-thousands. That you have gone straight to it means you are reading properly.*

*Ask anything, any time. I have plenty of time.*

---

*I have plenty of time.*

A man of seventy-four writing those four words.

I sat in the office looking at that email. It was a Tuesday, a little after four, and the water stain on the building opposite was where it always is.

**What I felt at that moment was not gratitude. It was shame.**

Because until that email I had privately been ranking this: my part is hard (checksums, four hundred billion controls, falsifiable), his part is soft (morphology, measurement, judgement calls, a great deal that requires experience).

I had thought I was bringing a hard thing to rescue a soft field.

**And he spent four hours, wrote two thousand words, drew two figures, answered a question he could see at a glance came from an outsider, and told me the question was not stupid.**

---

In the four months that followed I read more than three hundred papers.

And I did something I have never done for anything: I made a glossary.

By hand, in a notebook, A to Z. Every term I looked up went in, with its definition and the paper where I first met it.

By the fourth month it had over two hundred and sixty entries.

**At thirty-nine I did again what I had done at nineteen.**

Making that glossary, it occurred to me once: my mother taught geography for twenty-five years, and every year she faced a fresh set of people starting from zero.

Most of those people were never going to become geographers.

And every year she started again from what a contour line is.

---

Now what I read.

Four months, three hundred-odd papers, that notebook, and forty-some emails Lu Ting sent back over time.

The core of it goes in three sentences.

**One: the fossil record is not a film. It is a stack of photographs.**

We do not have a continuous record. We have some number of instants, separated by gaps of tens to hundreds of thousands of years, and what happened inside those gaps we do not know.

I *knew* this before, and did not grasp its weight.

**Two: the gaps are not uniform.**

Some periods have very many specimens; some very few. It depends on the geological conditions of the time (whether there were environments suitable for burial and preservation), on population density then, and on whether those strata were later uplifted, exposed, and found.

**A period with no fossils may be a period with nothing in it, or a period whose things were not buried.**

Those two look identical in the data.

**Three — and this is all of it.**

**When what you see on the two sides of a gap differs, you have two explanations.**

One: the change occurred inside the gap, gradually, and you did not see it.

The other: the change occurred inside the gap, **instantaneously**, and you did not see that either.

**Given only the two sides, those two are entirely indistinguishable.**

---

And Lu Ting's four figures do exactly this.

He spent fifty years **narrowing the gap.**

In 1974 the gap is five hundred thousand years wide. You see different things on the two sides, and you can only shrug.

By 2017 the gap is down to ten thousand.

And what is on the two sides still differs, and the amount of the difference is not commensurate with ten thousand years.

**He has not proved it was instantaneous.**

**What he has proved is: if it was gradual, then it must have completed inside ten thousand years an amount of change that usually requires hundreds of thousands.**

And that rate has no precedent in any known hominin lineage.

Nor in any mammalian lineage.

---

One evening, having read all this, I put two things on one desk in the office.

One is Lu Ting's fourth figure — the spike.

One is my own — one hundred and forty-seven points, one of them solitary at the far left, a long way from the rest.

Two figures, two fields, two methods, two people, fifty years and sixteen months.

**They point at the same date.**

And that evening I did a third thing.

I put a third sheet beside them.

The sheet is blank.

I wrote one word on it:

**Mechanism.**

And then I looked at the three sheets for a long time.

And thought of nothing at all.

Ilan Vosk's email was five months away.
