# 3.7 · Two Lines

It took four months to align the two lines.

*We*, in this section, is a word I use for the first time.

Before this, the thing was *I*.

---

First, how we worked.

Lu Ting is in another city. We began with email.

In the third week I proposed video.

That first call took forty minutes to connect.

I want to write those forty minutes, because they are my favourite part of the whole collaboration.

He has a laptop, handed down from his granddaughter, about six years old. He knew it had a camera. He did not know where the camera was.

First ten minutes: we could hear each other; I could not see him and he could not see me.
Minute twelve: I could see his ceiling.
Minute eighteen: I could see his forehead.
Minute twenty-five: I could see his whole face, and the picture was upside down.

At minute thirty-one I said: *Professor Lu, turn the computer over.*

He said: *If I turn it over I can't see the screen.*

I said: *You don't need the screen. You only need to hear me.*

He turned it over.

The picture came right.

And then he said: *Ah. There you are.*

I kept those four words for a long time.

A man of seventy-four, for a working meeting, holding a computer upside down for six minutes, and then seeing me and saying *ah, there you are.*

We had about sixty calls after that. He never learned the software. Every time I telephoned ten minutes in advance and talked him through where to click.

Sixty times. Taught sixty times.

**He never once apologised for it, and I never once found it a nuisance.**

Much later I understood why:

**Because he was there every time.**

A man who sits down in front of the computer early every time and has failed to learn it in six years, against a fluent operator whose mind is elsewhere — I know which I want.

---

Now the four months.

We had to align two dates.

**Date one: the lower bound on the sequence's age.**

From my side. The derivation:

The sequence is present in modern humans, Neanderthals, and Denisovans, and identical.
Therefore it is present in their last common ancestor.
Therefore its age is greater than or equal to that of the last common ancestor.

And the age of the last common ancestor comes from a molecular clock.

And molecular clock estimates carry error, and not a small one: the mainstream range is six hundred to eight hundred thousand years, and different methods, mutation-rate assumptions, and calibration points give different figures.

**My line gives a lower bound, and the bound itself has an interval about two hundred thousand years wide.**

**Date two: the age of the gap.**

From Lu Ting's side. The derivation:

Five regional density curves fall to zero simultaneously; the last specimen before and the first specimen after each has a date.

And every specimen's date has error too — OSL, argon-argon, palaeomagnetism, each with its own error structure.

**His line gives an interval about a hundred and ten thousand years wide.**

---

Now the difficulty.

Two intervals. If they overlap, what does that establish?

**Looking only at overlap, nothing.**

I worked that out in the first month, and it is the point I held hardest.

Two intervals of two hundred thousand and a hundred and ten thousand years, overlapping on a two-million-year axis — that is not a remarkable coincidence. Take any two Pleistocene events and the probability their intervals overlap is considerable.

**Two overlapping intervals is extremely weak evidence.**

And in my field a great many people would stop there, would write *two independent lines of evidence point to the same period*, would put it in an abstract, and would get a decent citation out of it.

I did not intend to.

Not out of nobility.

**Because if I did, I would never be able to go any further.**

A thing published on weak evidence attracts correct criticism immediately, and the criticism will be correct enough that I cannot answer it, and after that nobody will listen to a second sentence about this line of enquiry.

---

So we did something else.

We did a **joint estimate.**

In the simplest terms, because it is the whole of those four months.

Suppose there is a true date. Call it T.

My method measures T and yields an estimate and an error distribution.
His method measures T and yields another estimate and another error distribution.

If the two methods measure the same T, and if the two methods' errors are **independent of one another**, then:

**Multiplying the two distributions gives a joint distribution narrower than either.**

This is among the most elementary operations in statistics. It has many names and one meaning: **two independent, individually imprecise measurements are together more precise than either.**

And it has a precondition.

**The precondition is everything.**

---

**The errors must be independent.**

If the two methods' errors share a source, multiplying them is self-deception — you are not using two pieces of evidence, you are counting one piece twice.

So we spent three of those four months on one thing:

**establishing that our two lines' errors are independent.**

---

We made a table.

On the left, every source of error in my line. On the right, every source in his.

Mine:
- error in the mutation-rate estimate
- the generation-time assumption
- the choice of calibration point (which fossil anchors the molecular clock)
- the choice of population-genetic model
- sequencing depth of the ancient samples
- damage correction for ancient DNA

His:
- dose-rate estimation in OSL
- standards in argon-argon
- uncertainty in stratigraphic correlation
- horizon assignment of specimens
- sampling density
- the subjective component in the preservation-potential assessment

Twelve items.

We went through them one at a time, asking the same question of each: **could this item also affect the other side?**

Eleven: no.

**One: yes.**

---

That one is **the choice of calibration point.**

A molecular clock needs an anchor: you must know the exact time at which two lineages separated in order to convert a mutation count into years.

And that anchor is usually a **fossil.**

**Which is to say: part of my line depends on the discipline his line lives in.**

We found this at the end of the second month.

I remember that call. He was silent on his side for about twenty seconds — the only time in sixty calls he was silent that long.

Then he said: *Then that can't be used.*

He said it flatly.

**A man who has waited fifty years, on finding a possible circular dependency, says first: then that cannot be used.**

---

And we solved it afterwards.

The solution was: **change the anchor.**

I redid the molecular clock estimate using a calibration point that is not any hominin fossil, but an entirely unrelated, much older divergence in a different mammalian lineage.

That anchor is further away, so my error is larger — my interval went from two hundred thousand years wide to two hundred and ninety thousand.

**I made my own precision worse.**

And what it bought was: **independence.**

---

In the fourth month we did the joint estimate.

Result:

**About seven hundred thousand years ago, standard deviation about forty thousand.**

From two hundred and ninety thousand wide and a hundred and ten thousand wide, to forty thousand.

Not because we did anything clever. That is the necessary result of multiplying two independent distributions.

**And it is testable, contestable, and redoable by other people with other data.**

---

Now that evening.

We finished a little after eleven at night. The call was still open.

I shared the joint distribution figure with him.

He looked at it a long time.

Then he said: *Forty thousand.*

I said: *Forty thousand.*

He said: *My 1979 paper says about seven hundred thousand, with large uncertainty.*

He said: *Forty-five years.*

And then he did not speak.

I watched, on my side, the face of a seventy-four-year-old man through the camera of a six-year-old laptop, in bad quality and low light — his lamps are the old incandescent kind.

He did not cry. He did not smile.

**He only sat there looking at the screen.**

After about a minute he said:

*Dr Shen.*

I said: *Yes.*

He said:

*We have a date now.*

He said:

*And we still do not know what happened on it.*

---

I said: *Professor Lu, at least we know something happened.*

He said:

*I know.*

He said:

*I knew fifty years ago that something happened.*

He said:

*That is not what I want.*

---

Neither of us said anything for perhaps half a minute after that.

Then he said: *It's late. Go to bed.*

I said: *All right.*

He said: *Tomorrow I'll go through the preservation assessment again. There's a place I want to ask that young woman about.*

I said: *All right.*

We hung up.

---

One last thing, because it is the real product of those four months, and it is not the number.

In four months, between us, we went through twelve error sources, ran three independence checks, changed a calibration anchor, redid the molecular clock twice, and split and recombined a three-thousand-one-hundred-row table by five regions four times.

We never disagreed once.

Not once.

**Because we never discussed conclusions. We only discussed methods.**

If one person says *I think this is real*, the other has nothing to answer.

If one person says *your error source and mine share an origin*, the other can only go and check.

And this way of working was not agreed between us.

It formed by itself, from the first email.

Much later I understood why:

**Because both of us wanted the conclusion far too much.**

A person who does not much care about a conclusion can argue about conclusions, winning and losing being immaterial.

And of the two of us, one had waited fifty years, and one had dragged a manuscript out of the submission system and back into drafts.

We both knew that if we began persuading each other about the conclusion, we would succeed.

**And that would have destroyed it.**

So we never touched it.

We only checked errors.

Four months, twelve items, three tests.

And the number came out by itself at the end.
