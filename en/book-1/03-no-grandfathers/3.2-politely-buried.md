# 3.2 · Politely Buried

*You still chose them.*

First: whether the question is right.

It is.

It is a hundred per cent correct, and it is a genuinely important methodological problem. I spent a whole section on it at 1.6. I am the person who raised it — it came to me in the shower, I stood in the water thinking about it for eight minutes, and when I came out the floor was flooded.

A person asked, in a conference room, a question he has already thought about and already resolved, ought to be pleased.

I was not pleased.

Here is why.

---

I answered.

I said:

*Yes, the candidate set is mine. I stated it in the talk: seventeen functions, all standard, fixed before testing, none constructed by me.*

*And selection effects can be tested independently, by whole-genome negative control. I ran it: those seventeen functions over the entire human genome, window lengths from one thousand to ten thousand, about four hundred billion tests.*

*The false-positive count is zero.*

I paused.

I said: *Not very few. Zero.*

---

He said:

*Ah.*

---

That *ah* deserves a paragraph.

It was not a rude *ah*. His tone was ordinary, with even a touch of a nod in it. Having said it, he lowered his hand and leaned back a little in his chair.

That movement means: **I have finished asking.**

Not *I am persuaded.* *This subject is now closed.*

A person actually struck by that answer asks a second question. He asks: how were the four hundred billion done? How did you handle window overlap? What threshold? And isn't zero false positives itself strange — shouldn't pure statistics have produced some?

**That is what I needed.**

I needed somebody to be uneasy about the zero. Because I am uneasy about it. Because I rechecked it twice. Because I spiked artificial positives into the genome to confirm the pipeline was not broken.

And he said *ah* and leaned back.

In that second I knew the talk was finished.

---

There were two more questions.

**The second** was about Imhoff.

A young man, a PhD student by the look of him, in the middle rows. He asked: that 1987 paper — could there have been a problem with her samples? Twenty East Germans, the technology of the period.

It is a good question. Sincere, technical, answerable.

I said: her sample size and technique were of course limited. And my conclusion does not depend on her data — I used seventy-one modern genomes and four ancient samples. The value of her paper is that it specifies this sequence independently of me, which is what excludes my having selected it out of the genome.

He nodded. He said: *Understood. Thank you.*

He genuinely had understood.

And he had no second question.

A person who genuinely understands, and who cares, has a second question.

**He did not care. He was doing what a young PhD student does: asking one respectable question in the question period.**

I do not blame him. I did the same at his age.

**The third question** came from the chair.

A chair asks a question when no hand goes up. It is his job, it is courtesy, it prevents dead air.

He asked: *Have you considered that this might result from some epigenetic constraint we do not yet understand?*

---

Here I record my failure.

The correct answer is:

*A constraint mechanism can explain conservation. It cannot explain an arithmetic relation. A constraint can hold a sequence unchanged; it cannot make its last quarter equal a function of its first three quarters. Between those two things there is a chasm of an order of magnitude, and no known biological mechanism crosses it.*

I had prepared that answer. It is in my backup slides, number thirty-seven.

And what I said was:

*That is a possibility I cannot fully exclude at present. I think more work is needed.*

---

Why I said that.

Because at that moment I was in a conference room, facing a chair with whom I need to remain on good terms, in a question period with ninety seconds left.

And the correct answer sounds like correcting him.

*A constraint cannot explain an arithmetic relation* is, on paper, a technical point. In a room, out of the mouth of an associate researcher, addressed to the chair, it is a posture.

I chose not to strike the posture.

**With one sentence — *more work is needed* — I buried six months of elimination.**

---

*More work is needed* has a meaning in this field.

It means: **this result is not solid.**

It is a self-protective sentence. It makes the speaker look careful, measured, unlikely to over-read her own data — and it simultaneously tells everyone present that the thing has not landed.

A thing that has not landed does not need to be remembered.

I said those words, the chair nodded, and said: *Right, we'll stop there. Thank you all.*

Applause.

End.

---

Now the senior figure.

He did not speak during questions.

He said it afterwards, in the corridor, as the room emptied.

His name is Ben. Sixty-nine. A genuine authority in this field — not the kind that comes from a position, the kind where reading the literature up to the two thousands you cannot go round him. I have read at least thirty of his papers.

He passed me and stopped.

He said: *Dr Shen.*

I said: *Professor Ben.*

And then he said the following. I wrote it down that night in the hotel room, so I believe it is accurate to the word.

---

He said:

*A suggestion. You need not take it.*

*Your data is solid. The elimination is clean — cleaner than most people manage now. Four hundred billion controls is real work.*

*And you have presented it as a mystery.*

*A mystery is good at a conference. It is not good in a paper.*

*Reviewers do not want a mystery. Reviewers want a mechanism. You give them a phenomenon, they will ask for the mechanism; you say there is no mechanism, they will say you have not finished.*

He paused.

He said:

*Go home and find the mechanism.*

*If you cannot find the mechanism, don't publish yet.*

---

He patted my arm, said *it isn't easy*, and walked off.

---

I want to be plain about this: he was right.

**Every word of it is right.**

A paper reporting a phenomenon with no mechanism is very difficult to publish. Reviewers do want a mechanism. That is not reviewer prejudice; it is a reasonable norm with several centuries behind it and a generally excellent record: a phenomenon with no mechanism, and how do you know it is not some artefact you have not thought of.

And *if you cannot find the mechanism, don't publish yet* is, in ninety-nine per cent of cases, **the correct advice.**

It protects young people from publishing things that will ruin their reputations. It protects the literature from being drowned. It protects the signal-to-noise ratio of a whole field.

I have met many senior people who give no advice at all. Professor Ben stopped in a corridor after a session and said six sentences to a forty-year-old associate researcher with no connection to him, scheduled last.

That is a good thing.

**And it killed the work.**

---

That night in the hotel room, having written his words down, I sat for a long time.

What I was thinking about was technical:

**If the mechanism is *somebody made it*, then *find the mechanism* amounts to requiring me to prove first that the somebody exists.**

And the only evidence I have for that is the sequence itself.

So the requirement becomes a loop:

To publish the sequence, first supply the mechanism.
The only possible mechanism requires the sequence as evidence.
And the sequence is not published.

**It has locked itself outside.**

---

In that room I understood my position completely for the first time.

Not *the field is conservative.* The field is not conservative. The field has a set of filtering rules, and in the overwhelming majority of cases those rules are correct.

My position is:

**What I have happens to fall in that ruleset's blind spot.**

The ruleset says: phenomenon plus mechanism equals publishable.
The ruleset has no column reading: phenomenon, where *the existence of a mechanism is itself the conclusion.*

It was not rejected.

It **did not enter.**

At 10.8 I will write about something else — a record format with eleven categories, none of which can hold a set of clear standing bodies, so they were entered as *environmental*, so their interiors did not need to be read, so they were not read.

That is a thing I learned much later.

And in that hotel room, in the autumn of 2022, I tasted it for the first time.

**A thing that falls outside your classification table will not be rejected.**

**It will be entered in the nearest column, and handled as that column is handled.**

---

That night I did two things.

First: I wrote down Professor Ben's words, exactly.

Second: I opened the submission system and dragged my prepared manuscript from *ready to submit* back to *drafts.*

It sat in drafts for eleven months.

After eleven months I deleted it.

Not because I had given up.

Because by then the manuscript was hopelessly insufficient — by then what I had was no longer a sequence. It was a thermal map from Antarctica, a set of contour lines in nineteen volumes of lesson plans, a mathematician's question about information content, and a manuscript unpublished for thirty years shown to me by a man of seventy-four.

---

And that night I knew none of it.

I sat on the edge of the bed in a hotel room, looking at a sheet of paper with six sentences on it.

I was thinking: mechanism.

Where am I going to find a mechanism.

I had no idea whatever.

And at that hour, in the back row of that hall, there had been a white-haired old man who looked at my last slide for eight full seconds after I finished.

He was in that hotel too.

The next morning he would sit three tables away from me at breakfast, looking at me, and not come over.

He would wait until the conference was entirely over, until most people had left, until there was nobody else in the corridor.

And then he would call after me.
