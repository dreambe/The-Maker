# 3.3 · One Exception

At breakfast the next morning he was sitting three tables away.

I noticed him because he was looking at me.

Not rudely. The way a man eating breakfast and thinking about something has his eyes come to rest on you without his knowing it.

When I looked back he moved his gaze to the window.

Outside the window was the hotel car park.

He ate very slowly. In front of him were a bowl of congee, an egg, two slices of cucumber. He was a long time peeling the egg — old people are slow with eggs; it is not hesitation, it is the hands.

I finished. I passed his table on my way out.

He did not look up.

---

That was the third day. The fourth morning was the closing session, and everyone would leave that afternoon.

I sat at the back for the closing.

In those two and a half hours I did not once look at the platform. I looked at my notebook and redrew the figure with the one hundred and forty-seven points, over and over, about eleven times.

That is what I do when I am anxious. I do not bite my nails or jiggle my leg. I redraw a figure I have already drawn.

The session ended, applause, the room emptied.

People moved toward the doors. The hall was full of the sound of wheeled suitcases.

I shuffled along at the back of the crowd.

---

The corridor had thinned out.

I got to the lifts and pressed the button.

Behind me somebody said:

*Dr Shen.*

---

I turned round.

Him.

White hair, the old jacket, older-looking than in the hall — conference lighting is kind to everyone.

He was carrying a cloth bag, the sort given out at conferences with the logo on it, and it was full.

He said: *My name is Lu. Lu Ting.*

I said: *Professor Lu.*

In that second I had no idea who he was.

I want to admit that here, because it bothered me for a long time afterwards: **I did not know who Lu Ting was.**

I do molecular evolution. He does palaeontology. Two fields with a wall between them — we cite each other's conclusions and do not read each other's papers. He has a name in his own field, and I had read none of it.

My reaction in that second was: an old man who wants to chat.

I prepared myself to hear a courtesy or two, say thank you, and get into the lift.

---

He said the following.

Slowly, and with no preamble. No *interesting talk*, no *I work in palaeontology*, nothing to lead in.

He said:

*Those four ancient samples of yours.*

I said: *Yes.*

He said:

*You said that stretch is in the Denisovan too.*

I said: *Yes. Thirty-one per cent coverage, and every covered position identical.*

He said:

*Then it is older than seven hundred thousand years.*

---

I stopped where I stood.

I should explain why.

That sequence is in the Denisovan, in the Neanderthals, and in modern humans.

The last common ancestor of those three, on current molecular-clock estimates, is between six hundred thousand and eight hundred thousand years ago.

**So the sequence must predate that split.**

It is an elementary inference. Anyone who has done one semester of phylogenetics can make it.

And I did not state it in my talk.

I presented four ancient samples, I gave coverage, I discussed deamination damage, I discussed contamination. I said all three species' names aloud.

**And I did not do the arithmetic for them.**

I do not know why I did not. I thought about it for a long time afterwards. I think my reasoning was: it is obvious, everyone present can derive it, saying it aloud would look like condescension.

And in fact:

**Of the forty-odd people in that room that afternoon, exactly one derived it.**

And he was in the back row.

---

I said: *Yes. At least seven hundred thousand. Possibly older.*

He nodded.

And then he said it.

He said:

*I have waited fifty years for that number.*

---

I want to write the next forty seconds, because they are the only time that year that a stranger went straight through me in a corridor.

I said: *Professor Lu, what is your area?*

He said: *Pleistocene hominin fossils.*

He said: *I started in 1974.*

He said:

*In 1979 I wrote something for the first time saying that the rate of morphological change in that period is wrong. Too fast. Too fast to be explained by the mechanisms we have.*

He said:

*It was not published. The reviewer said my sample size was insufficient. He was right; my sample size was insufficient — in those years, every relevant specimen in the world put together was insufficient.*

He said:

*Later there were more specimens. The sample size became sufficient.*

He said:

*The rate is still wrong.*

---

The lift arrived. The doors opened.

Two people inside looked at us.

I did not get in. I pressed the button beside it and let the doors close.

That is the lowest-cost, highest-return action I have ever taken.

---

He said:

*Fifty years, I have been waiting for one thing.*

*Not for somebody to prove me right. For somebody to come at it from the other end.*

He said:

*If a thing is real, then from the fossil end you see a shape; and from somewhere else you should be able to see another face of the same shape.*

*If only my end can see it, then most likely I am seeing it wrong.*

He said:

*Yesterday afternoon you put four lines on a screen.*

He said:

*You said you offer no explanation.*

He said:

*And your date and my date are the same date.*

---

I said: *Professor Lu, I did not give a date.*

He said:

*You did.*

He said:

*You said Denisovan.*

---

In that second I understood something that has stayed with me:

**A person who has genuinely been waiting for a thing will hear it in what you did not say.**

Forty-odd people. Twenty-two minutes. I gave them all the data. I said all three species' names aloud.

And one person took the word *Denisovan*, derived seven hundred thousand years from it, and knew what that number meant.

Because he had waited fifty years.

**A prepared person needs one word.**

And an unprepared person, given a whole talk, will say *you still chose them* and lean back in his chair.

I do not blame that man. He had not waited fifty years. He had no reason to.

---

He hitched the cloth bag up and said:

*I'm on the third floor. If you aren't rushing off, I'll show you something.*

---

I said: *My flight is at three.*

He said: *Ah.*

He said: *Never mind, then.*

He said it in an entirely ordinary voice. No disappointment, no reproach. Just *never mind, then.*

A man of seventy-four who has waited fifty years, on the last morning of a conference, in a corridor, who has spoken to a woman of forty for six minutes, hears *my flight is at three*, and says *never mind, then.*

He turned to go.

---

I said: *Professor Lu.*

He stopped.

I said: *I'll change the flight.*

---

He looked at me.

I remember his expression exactly.

It was not pleasure.

It was the expression of confirmation — of a man who has done a thing he was not sure was worth doing, and has received a signal telling him: it was.

He said: *Come up, then. Three oh seven.*

And then he said something I did not understand at the time.

He said:

*There's rather a lot of it. Don't mind the mess.*

---

In the lift I phoned the airline to change the ticket.

The change fee was eleven hundred and something. My monthly performance pay then was a little over three thousand.

My hand shook when I pressed confirm — not over the money.

Because I knew I was doing something I would afterwards be responsible for.

Until then, that sequence was mine alone. I could stop at any time. I could do as Professor Ben said — not publish without a mechanism — leave the manuscript in drafts until I no longer wanted to look at it, and in three years say *oh, that; I didn't take it further.*

And the moment I went up to the third floor, the moment I looked at what a seventy-four-year-old man had accumulated over fifty years —

**there would be a second person inside it.**

A thing only I know, I can abandon.

A thing two people know, abandoning is betrayal.

That is what I thought about in the lift.

And I pressed confirm.

---

The door of 307 was ajar.

I knocked.

He said: *Come in.*

I pushed it open.

And stopped in the doorway.

It did not look like a hotel room.

The bed, the desk, the floor, the windowsill, the space outside the bathroom door — all of it paper.

Not scattered paper. Sorted paper: stacks, each stack under a bulldog clip, each clip holding down a handwritten label.

I counted roughly. More than forty stacks.

The cloth bag was open on the floor by the bed, and there was more in it.

He was standing by the window, lifting a stack off a chair and putting it on the bed to make somewhere for me to sit.

He said:

*Fifty years. This is all of it.*
