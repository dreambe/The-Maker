# 3.9 · The Empty Case

Between those two skeletons there is a third case.

The same size as the two either side, the same height, the same glass, the same plinth, the same spotlight.

**It is empty.**

---

Why I did not notice it for forty minutes.

Because it is not conspicuous.

An empty case in a museum is not conspicuous. There is nothing to look at, so the eye slides past. Human vision skips empty regions automatically — an extremely effective mechanism, and how we find the one thing we are looking for in a room full of things.

And more importantly, a second reason:

**I assumed it was being installed.**

Museums often have empty cases. An object is out on loan for a special exhibition, or away for conservation, or in rotation. The case is empty for a period, usually with a card explaining.

Across those forty minutes my eye passed over it three or four times, and each time my mind supplied an explanation: that case is temporarily empty.

**I gave it a default.**

---

At two minutes to five the announcement came.

I looked at the two cases one last time.

I turned from the right-hand skeleton to the left-hand one, and my line of sight crossed the middle.

And that time it stopped in the middle.

I do not know why that time. It had not stopped the previous three or four.

---

I went over.

The spotlight on that case is on.

I will repeat that, because it is everything:

**The light is on.**

A case being installed has its light off. Nobody lights an empty case; it wastes electricity and looks bad.

**This case's light is on.**

It falls on the plinth. The plinth is that off-white slightly napped board, identical to the ones the skeletons stand on either side.

The light falls on it, and there is nothing.

---

There is a label in front of the case.

Same position as the two beside it, same angle, same material, same typeface.

I bent down to read it.

**There is text on the label.**

I will set down exactly what I saw, having photographed it, and being now able to write from the photograph.

The label has three panels, like the two beside it.

**Panel one, the title position.**

The left-hand label carries that skeleton's binomial and number.
The right-hand label carries that skeleton's binomial and number.

**This one carries four words.**

The four words are:

```
THIS INTERVAL IS VACANT
```

**Panel two, date.**

Left: a date with error.
Right: a date with error.

**This one: an interval.**

The interval is: approximately seven hundred and twenty thousand to approximately six hundred and eighty thousand years ago.

**Forty thousand years.**

I crouched there looking at those two figures.

Seven hundred and twenty. Six hundred and eighty.

And the joint estimate Lu Ting and I had finished nine days earlier came out at: about seven hundred thousand years ago, standard deviation about forty thousand.

**The same interval.**

**Panel three, text.**

On the two beside it, panel three gives the discovery history, morphology, and significance of the specimen. About two hundred words.

On this one, panel three is a passage.

I copied it. It reads:

> No reliable hominin fossils have been recovered from this interval, in this region or anywhere in the world.
>
> The cause of this vacancy is not at present understood.
>
> Possible explanations include preservation conditions, excavation intensity, and contingencies in the history of research.
>
> This museum retains this case in order to remind visitors: **what we display is what we have found; and what we have found is not everything.**

---

I crouched in front of that label a long time.

Long enough that a member of staff came over.

She said: *Excuse me, we're closing.*

I said: *Yes. I'm going.*

She glanced at the empty case, and then at me.

And then she said something.

She said:

*A lot of people ask about that case.*

I said: *Ask what?*

She said:

*Whether the thing hasn't arrived yet.*

---

Going downstairs I asked at the desk.

I asked: when was that empty case installed.

The person checked and said: 2009, in that redisplay.

I asked: who wrote that text.

She said she did not know; I would have to ask curatorial, and curatorial had gone home.

I said: all right. Thank you.

---

On the train I messaged Lu Ting.

I sent three photographs: the whole case, the whole label, and a close-up of panel two.

I wrote nothing. Just the three photographs.

He replied about ten minutes later.

He wrote:

*2009.*

I said: *You know that case?*

He said:

*I proposed it.*

---

Now what he told me afterwards, sentence by sentence, because it is the end of this chapter.

He said:

*In 2009 they redisplayed and asked me to consult.*

*In their scheme those two stood side by side, with a label between them reading: over time, hominin morphology changed gradually.*

He said:

*I said that sentence cannot be written.*

*They asked why. I said because we do not know that it was gradual.*

*They said, then what do we write.*

He said:

*I said: leave an empty case in the middle.*

---

*They refused.*

He said:

*They said an empty case makes visitors think something has been stolen, or sent for repair, and they will complain.*

*And a missing object breaks the rhythm of the whole gallery.*

He said:

*I said: then break the rhythm there.*

---

He said:

*We argued for two months.*

*In the end the director made a decision I am grateful for to this day.*

*He said: keep the case, keep the light on, and write the label clearly.*

He said:

*Keeping the light on was his.*

*I did not understand at the time. I said, why light an empty case.*

He said:

*He said: with the light off, visitors think it is being installed. With the light on, they know it is an exhibit.*

---

On the train I looked at the three photographs.

The third: that interval. Seven hundred and twenty to six hundred and eighty.

I wrote: *Professor Lu, who set that interval?*

He said:

*I did.*

He said:

*The data I had in 2009 only let me set it that wide.*

He said:

*I wrote an interval on the label because I did not know which year.*

Then he paused.

Then he sent one last message.

It reads:

*Fifteen years.*

*You have it down to forty thousand.*

*And that case is still empty.*

---

I got home a little after ten.

I did not put the light on. I stood in the entryway for a while.

I was thinking about that case.

I was thinking: that case has stood there for fifteen years.

Every day, from nine in the morning until five in the afternoon, that spotlight is on, falling on an off-white slightly napped board.

Every day several hundred people walk past it.

The overwhelming majority do not notice it. A few notice and assume the thing has not arrived. A very few read the label, say *oh*, and move on.

**Fifteen years, that light on eight hours a day, on an empty place.**

And it is not an oversight, not an unfinished installation, not a position waiting to be filled.

**It was left there deliberately.**

It is the outcome of two months of argument between a man of seventy-four — fifty-nine at the time — and a museum director.

And its function is to say one thing to several hundred people a day:

**There is nothing here.**

**And that fact is itself a thing that needs exhibiting.**

---

That night I did something.

I printed the photograph of the label and put it up on my office wall.

In the same place — behind the door, where four years earlier I had taped *the author is evidently a scientist of literary sensibility.*

That sheet stayed there.

And beside it I later put two more.

One is Lu Ting's fourth figure. The spike.

One is my own. One hundred and forty-seven points, one solitary at the far left.

Three sheets.

And between them I left a patch of bare wall.

There is nothing on that wall.

**And I know what belongs there.**

What belongs there is a sheet with the mechanism written on it.

That patch stayed bare for a long time.

**It was filled before the glass case was.**

And what filled it was not me.

It was a forty-six-year-old sidelined glacial archaeologist, seven years dry, whom I did not then know, in an email with six words in the subject line, with a thermal map attached.
