# 3.8 · The Museum

Nine days after the joint estimate I went to a museum.

Not for work, not for a meeting. I took half a day's leave and rode two hours on a train to look at two skeletons.

I told nobody. I did not tell Lu Ting.

I arrived at twenty past four. The museum closes at five.

**I had given myself forty minutes.**

---

Why I went, because the reason itself establishes something.

I had seen those two skeletons in data.

They occupy one row each in my three-thousand-one-hundred-row table. They have numbers, localities, dates, dating methods, errors, preserved elements, publication years, current repositories.

The *current repository* field reads: this museum.

And in the seventh week of making that table, filling in those two rows, I stopped.

**It occurred to me that I had never seen a real hominin skeleton.**

Thirty-nine. A doctorate in biology. Sixteen years of research. Four months and three hundred-odd palaeontology papers, a two-hundred-and-sixty-entry glossary, a three-thousand-one-hundred-row table.

**And I had never seen a real bone.**

---

I did not go in that seventh week. I had other things to do, and I told myself I would go when they were done.

And when they were done I went at once.

On the train I thought: if Lu Ting knew I was going, what would he say.

I think he would say: *You should have gone long ago.*

And he would not explain why.

---

The gallery is on the third floor.

There were seven or eight people in it when I came in: a couple with a child, three who looked like students, one old man alone.

The lighting is museum lighting — dim overall, with the cases lit.

I followed the route.

Stone tools first, environmental reconstructions, a whole wall of timeline. I barely stopped.

And then I was in front of the two skeletons.

---

They stand in a small separate room. Two glass cases, about six metres apart.

The left one — I remember its number and will not write it here. Its date falls **before** the gap.

The right one, **after.**

The six metres between them is forty thousand years.

---

I stood in front of the left one first.

What I saw, as accurately as I can put it, without metaphor.

It is incomplete. There is a cranium (the mandible is a cast, slightly lighter in colour), a stretch of vertebrae, several ribs on one side, one femur, part of a tibia. The rest is armature and space.

The bone is dark brown, unevenly, nearly black in places. There are small holes and cracks in the surface.

It is **smaller** than I expected.

For that I was entirely unprepared.

I had seen all its measurements: estimated stature, estimated mass, cranial capacity, limb proportions. I knew the numbers.

**And numbers do not tell you *small*.**

Standing in front of that case, the cranium was at about the height of my chest — plinth height plus skeleton height.

If it stood up, it would be shorter than me.

What I thought in that second was extremely stupid: I thought about whether I might bump into it.

---

I stood there about ten minutes.

For ten minutes I was doing one thing: **trying to match what was in front of me to the row in my table.**

The row reads:

```
number · locality · date 782±31 ka · method Ar-Ar · elements cranium + partial
postcrania · published 19xx · repository this museum
```

I recited the row.

Then I looked at the thing.

**They do not match.**

Not because the data is wrong. The data is entirely right. The date is right, the elements are right, the repository is right.

**It is that there is no *small* in that row.**

There are no holes in the surface of that cranium in the row. No cracks. No uneven dark brown. No slightly lighter cast mandible — which means the real mandible was never found, which means somebody made a false one to fill the space, which means that somebody had to judge what shape it ought to be.

**There is nothing in that row.**

And I made that row. And I made three thousand one hundred of them.

---

Standing in front of that case, two things came to me.

The first is 2.6. The instrument.

*What year is it.* *A hundred minus seven.* *Draw a clock.*

Twenty-one.

And the relation of that twenty-one to my mother.

The second I would not learn for a very long while, and I write it here in advance, this section and it being the same event:

On the surface of a dead planet there stand fourteen thousand and seventy-five clear, transparent things.

Somebody measured the axial ratios of four hundred and eleven of them.

And wrote: **morphology variant · no regularity.**

---

**I did the same thing.**

I made three thousand one hundred rows. Eight fields each.

I compressed three thousand one hundred things that had lived into twenty-four thousand eight hundred fields.

And I had never looked at one of them.

Not that I could not. Most are in museums; some can be viewed on application; some even have high-resolution scans available to download.

**I did not look because my table has no field called *look*.**

---

I moved to the right-hand case.

Its date falls after the gap.

It is more complete. A nearly complete cranium, a mandible (real), a pelvis, long bones on both sides.

It is **larger** than the left one.

I had known that from the data long before — those are the two ends of the curve, the two sides of the spike, the thing Lu Ting has spent fifty years measuring.

And standing here looking, it is another matter.

The left one, standing up, would be shorter than me.

The right one, standing up, would be about my height.

**Six metres between them, and forty thousand years.**

---

I stood in front of the right one about fifteen minutes.

What came to me in those fifteen minutes I have never since shaken off.

I was thinking: **there is no relation between them.**

Let me be plain.

In evolutionary biology we draw phylogenies. We put one species below, another above, and connect them with a line. The line means: the second is descended from the first.

**We draw the line.**

Fossils do not tell you who descends from whom. Fossils tell you only: at this time, in this place, there was a thing like this; at that time, in that place, a thing like that.

***A evolved into B* is a connection we add.**

In the overwhelming majority of cases the connection is right, or nearly right, because morphology is continuous, because geography is continuous, because time is continuous, because between two things there is usually a string of intermediates.

**And between these two there is not.**

That line, between these two, is drawn on the strength of *there ought to be one.*

I stood there looking at the short one on the left and the tall one on the right, with six metres of floor between them — the lighting falls on the floor and you can see the joints between the tiles.

**And in those six metres there is nothing at all.**

Not *not on display.* Nothing.

---

One last thing.

While I was standing in front of the right-hand case, the couple with the child came up.

The child was five or six. He stood at the case, looked for a while, and turned to ask his mother:

*Why is he here?*

She said: *Because he's very old.*

The child said: *How old.*

She glanced at the label and said: *More than five hundred thousand years.*

The child said: *Then why did he die.*

She said: *Because he got too old.*

The child said: *Oh.*

And they moved on.

---

I stood there.

The child asked what a five-year-old asks. His mother gave an answer a five-year-old can take.

The answer is wrong — that thing was not old when it died; it may have been thirty; that was the average lifespan then. It did not die of age.

And there is one thing in that answer that is right, and right in a way that mother could not possibly know:

***Because he got too old* is the default explanation we apply to every death whose cause we do not know and which happened a long time ago.**

And a default explanation looks exactly like an argued one (3.6).

---

At two minutes to five an announcement said the museum was closing.

I looked at the two cases one last time.

The left one, short, before the gap.

The right one, tall, after the gap.

And only then did I notice the thing between them.

**I had stood in that room for forty minutes, and noticed it in the last minute.**
