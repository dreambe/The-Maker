# 3.6 · The Shape of the Gap

*Insufficient data* is an extremely useful sentence.

It is almost always true. In palaeontology the data is always insufficient; in any field that studies the past the data is always insufficient. A person who says *insufficient data* is rarely wrong.

And precisely because it is almost always true, it can be used to end any discussion.

Having read those three hundred-odd papers, I spent two months on one thing:

**I wanted to see whether that gap is *insufficient data*.**

---

First how I did it, because the rest of this section rests on it.

I did something extremely stupid: I made a table of every dated hominin specimen I could find.

One row per specimen. Columns: number, locality, date, dating method, dating error, preserved element, year published, current repository.

Seven weeks.

The table came to just over three thousand one hundred rows.

I sent it to Lu Ting.

He replied: *I made this table. In 1996. Mine had eight hundred-odd rows. Yours has two thousand more.*

And then: *You are faster than I was.*

I said: because you had to write and request, and I only have to download.

He said: *Yes. Which is why you have to do more.*

---

Now what the table shows.

**One: density.**

Plot the three thousand one hundred specimens by date as a histogram — time on the horizontal, number of specimens in that interval on the vertical.

You get a very uneven curve. Some intervals dense, some sparse.

That is normal. That is what *insufficient data* looks like in its ordinary state: the record of the past is patchy.

And at one place that curve **falls to zero.**

That is the gap.

---

**Two: the edges of the gap.**

This came to me one day in the second month, and having thought it I got up and walked twice round the office.

What should the edge of a sampling gap look like?

**Gradual.**

Because sampling density depends on geological conditions — whether that period had environments suitable for burial, whether there are volcanic ash layers to date against, whether those strata were later uplifted and exposed.

And geological conditions vary **continuously.** A basin does not stop depositing in a particular year; a sequence does not have everything above one horizon suddenly eroded away.

So the specimen density either side of a sampling gap should fall to zero **gradually**, and rise again **gradually.**

The curve should have shoulders.

**I looked at the edges of that gap.**

There are no shoulders.

Specimen density holds at a mid-to-high level on either side and then falls to zero across an extremely narrow interval.

I measured the width of that fall.

**The width of the fall is smaller than the dating error of the specimens themselves.**

Which is to say: **the sharpness of that edge already exceeds the limit of what we can measure. It may be sharper.**

---

The weight of this must be made plain.

A sampling gap has soft edges.
A real gap has sharp edges.

**And the difference is measurable, and requires no new data — only laying out the data we have by date and looking at the shape of the density curve.**

Anyone could do that.

What it requires is: **treating the gap as an object of study rather than an obstacle to be gone around.**

And before this, nobody had.

I asked Lu Ting about it much later. I asked: why has nobody done such an obvious thing in fifty years.

He thought for a moment.

He said: *Because a gap is not data.*

He said: *In this field we are trained: study what you have. What you do not have is not your object of study. It is your difficulty.*

He said: *Go and study a thing you do not have, and people will say you are not doing proper work.*

He said: *And there is no funding. You cannot write a grant application saying you intend to study a stretch of strata with no fossils in it.*

---

**Three: position.**

This came to me over about an afternoon once the first two were done, and it is the heaviest of the three.

If that gap were caused by sampling, it should fall in a **period of poor preservation conditions.**

Preservation conditions can be assessed independently — without fossils, from geology: whether that period had stable depositional environments, lacustrine sequences, cave deposits, volcanic activity (ash is the best dating horizon there is, and among the best burial media).

I had that assessment made. I cannot do it; I hired someone — a postdoc in stratigraphy, whom I paid two weeks' consultancy out of my own money, because there is no project under which I could claim *assessment of fossil preservation potential for a given interval.*

Her report is fourteen pages.

Conclusion: **preservation conditions in that interval rank in the top quartile for the entire Pleistocene.**

**Not poor. Good.**

That interval has stable lacustrine deposition, multiple ash horizons, cave accumulation. It is a period that **should** have left a great many fossils.

**And it is empty.**

---

My reaction on reading that report.

I did nothing immediately.

I read the fourteen pages through again, the second time looking for an error — I was looking for where she had got it wrong.

She had not.

Then I transferred her fee, somewhat more than agreed; she returned part of it; we went back and forth three times; in the end she took it.

Then I sat in the office and put the three things side by side:

The density curve (the gap).
The edge sharpness (no shoulders).
The preservation potential assessment (top quartile).

And what came into my head in that second was a very unprofessional sentence, which I wrote in the notebook:

**Not that nothing was left. That there was nothing.**

---

**Four.**

I thought the first three were all of it.

The fourth came from Lu Ting, in an email, in one sentence.

He said:

*Have you checked other regions.*

---

Why that sentence is lethal.

Sampling bias is **local.**

A basin's strata are eroded; that is that basin's business. A missing interval in East Africa does not affect the record in Asia. Geological histories differ everywhere, preservation conditions differ everywhere, excavation intensities differ everywhere.

**So if a gap is caused by sampling, it should fall at different times in different regions.**

And if it appears everywhere at once —

then it is not sampling.

---

Three weeks to split the three-thousand-one-hundred-row table by region.

East Africa.
Southern Africa.
Western Asia.
Eastern Asia.
Europe.

Five regions, five density curves.

Every curve is a different shape — different geology, climate, and excavation history. Some regions have very many specimens, some very few. Some regions are empty in certain intervals, and those empties do not coincide, which is what ordinary sampling gaps look like.

**And at one place all five curves fall to zero.**

The same window.

Five continental-scale regions, five entirely independent geological histories, five different research communities, a hundred years of excavation.

**Empty simultaneously.**

---

I plotted the five curves on one figure.

I showed that figure later at the first internal meeting of the Tracing Back. It is the only figure at that meeting that drew no questions.

Not because nobody had one.

Because after it went up the room was silent for something like fifteen seconds.

---

To end this section, back to the phrase.

*Insufficient data.*

I can now give its exact content in this case:

If a gap is caused by insufficient data, then:
its edges should be gradual — and these are sharp.
its position should fall in a period of poor preservation — and this falls in a good one.
it should be out of phase between regions — and this is globally synchronous.

Three properties, all reversed.

**The *insufficient data* explanation would have to violate all three of its own signatures at once.**

And for fifty years before this, that gap was filed under *insufficient data.*

Not because anyone had argued that it was.

**Because nobody had argued that it was not.**

It was a default.

A thing not argued about stays at its default. And a thing sitting at its default looks exactly like a thing that has been argued for.

---

The evening I finished that analysis I did something.

I telephoned Lu Ting — the first time; everything before had been email.

I said: Professor Lu. Five regions. Synchronous.

The line was quiet for a while.

Then he said: *I did three.*

He said: *In '98. East Africa, southern Africa, western Asia.*

He said: *Three, synchronous.*

I said: *Why did you not publish it?*

He said:

*I did.*

*I submitted to four journals.*

*All four said: this only shows that the sampling biases of those three regions have a common cause — for instance a global climatic event affecting preservation conditions everywhere.*

He said: *That is a reasonable position.*

He said: *I could not refute it at the time.*

I said: *And now?*

He said:

*Now you have five. And you have the preservation assessment.*

*A climatic event affecting preservation conditions everywhere at once — that is correct.*

*And a climatic event makes preservation conditions **worse**.*

He paused.

He said:

*Your report says that interval is in the top quartile.*

*Go and ask them: what kind of global climatic event makes preservation conditions everywhere **better** at the same time as the fossils **disappear**.*
