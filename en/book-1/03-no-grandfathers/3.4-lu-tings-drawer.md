# 3.4 · Lu Ting's Drawer

Forty-odd stacks of paper.

The labels first, because the labels matter more than the paper.

Each stack is held under a bulldog clip over a handwritten label: stiff card, pencil, very small writing.

I copied some of them down:

```
East Africa · cranial capacity · original measurements · 1974–1989
East Africa · cranial capacity · recheck · 1991
East Africa · cranial capacity · recheck · 2003
East Africa · cranial capacity · recheck · 2017
```

Four stacks. One quantity. Four years.

```
facial projection angle · all specimens · by horizon
facial projection angle · questionable specimens removed
facial projection angle · absolute dates only
```

Three stacks.

```
enamel thickness · I am not sure about this one · see 1998 notes
```

One stack, with a sentence on the label.

```
objections · received
objections · thought of myself
objections · thought of myself · unresolved
```

Three stacks.

The last is the thickest.

---

I stood in the doorway a long time.

He said: *Sit.*

I sat. He poured me water in one of the free disposable cups.

He sat on the edge of the bed.

He said: *Have you the time?*

I said: *I have.*

He said: *Then I'll start at the beginning.*

---

He talked for four hours.

I have to admit: I cannot reproduce all four hours. I did not record it — I thought about it and did not ask, because I felt it would look like collecting material.

I took notes. I used the eleven pages left in my notebook and then the backs of a stack of printouts he gave me.

What follows is the skeleton of what I retained.

---

**His problem states in one sentence.**

From about two million years ago to about a hundred thousand years ago, hominin morphology was changing.

The rate of that change can be measured.

The method: take a morphological index — cranial capacity, facial projection angle, brow-ridge thickness, tooth size, anything measurable — lay out every dated specimen in time, and see how the index moves.

**You get a curve.**

The slope of that curve is the rate of change.

---

He said:

*When I came into the field in 1974, the curve looked like this.*

He pulled a sheet out of one stack. A photocopy, very old.

On it was a curve. Fairly gentle, rising slowly left to right, with some undulation.

He said: *That was the state of it. One division on the horizontal is five hundred thousand years.*

He said: *On that figure the change is gradual. The slope varies, and there is nothing particular anywhere.*

He said: *In 1974 nobody thought there was a problem here.*

---

He pulled out another.

He said: *This is 1991. More specimens, better dating precision. One division is two hundred thousand years.*

The curve is different.

One stretch of it is steeper than what lies either side.

Not very steep. The kind of steep you have to stare at.

He said: *At that point people began saying there might be an acceleration here. And most held it to be a sampling problem — there are more specimens from that period, so the change looks fast.*

He said: *That is a reasonable position. I held it reasonable at the time.*

---

The third.

*2003. One division, fifty thousand years.*

The steep stretch is steeper.

And it is narrower.

He said: *By then the sampling-bias explanation no longer really held, because we had controlled for sampling density. Controlled, the stretch was still there.*

He said: *And the explanation became: a real acceleration, possibly climatic, possibly a bottleneck.*

He said: *Also reasonable. I accepted it at the time.*

---

The fourth.

*2017. One division, ten thousand years.*

I looked at it.

It is no longer a steep stretch.

**It is a spike.**

A largely gentle line that at one place goes very nearly vertically up, and comes down again.

He said: *That is the whole of my fifty years.*

---

Now what he said next, being the core of this section, and a genuine scientific argument — not an intuition, not a lament.

He said:

*Look at the order of those four figures.*

*1974: gentle.*
*1991: slightly steep in the middle.*
*2003: steeper, and narrower.*
*2017: a spike.*

He said:

*Every time our temporal resolution improves, that thing gets **sharper**.*

He said:

*That is the point. You are a quantitative person. You should see it immediately.*

---

I saw it.

I want to set it out, because it is the only thing I heard that day that made me sit up in my chair.

**A genuine gradual process, at improved resolution, reveals internal structure.**

Change the time base from five hundred thousand years per division to ten thousand, and if the change across that stretch is composed of many small, continuous, mutually independent steps, then you will see the steps — you will see jitter, pauses, reversals; you will see that the steep line is actually a few dozen small stairs.

**Improve the resolution, and a gradual process spreads out.**

An instantaneous event does not spread out.

Improve the resolution tenfold and it merely becomes ten times narrower, because it was a point all along and you previously lacked the ability to draw it as one.

**Improve the resolution, and an instantaneous event sharpens.**

Those are two entirely different behaviours, and they are distinguishable.

And Lu Ting spent fifty years improving the resolution fiftyfold.

**That thing sharpened every time.**

Not once did it spread.

---

He said:

*When I wrote that piece in 1979 I was looking at the 1974 figure. All I could say then was that the rate was wrong. I could not say where it was wrong.*

*The reviewer said my sample size was insufficient. He was right.*

He said:

*And the thing I wanted to say is not about sample size.*

He said:

*At insufficient sample size, a gradual process and an instantaneous event look identical — both are a steep stretch.*

*It is only at sufficient sample size that they come apart.*

He said:

*I waited forty-three years for them to come apart.*

---

He laid the four figures side by side on the bed.

1974, 1991, 2003, 2017.

Four figures. One thing. Sharper and sharper.

He said:

*I have never published those four together.*

I said: *Why not.*

He said:

*Because putting them together says something.*

I said: *Says what.*

He said:

*Says that it is not a process.*

---

He was quiet for a long while.

Then he said:

*And if it is not a process, what is it.*

He said:

*I cannot say. Fifty years, and I cannot say.*

He said:

*You cannot publish a thing you cannot say.*

---

In that second I thought of Professor Ben in the corridor.

*If you cannot find the mechanism, don't publish yet.*

**The same sentence.**

A sixty-nine-year-old authority in molecular evolution said it to me in a corridor yesterday afternoon.
A seventy-four-year-old palaeontologist has been saying it to himself for fifty years.

And both of them are right.

**And it silenced two people for decades apiece, and they do not know each other, and what they are silent about is two faces of the same thing.**

---

The 1979 manuscript is at the bottom of the last stack.

He pulled it out and handed it to me.

Eleven pages, handwritten, blue-black ink, small neat characters — and for an instant I thought of my mother's contour lines.

In the top right corner of the first page, in an editor's red: `rejected 1979.11`.

I turned to the last page.

The final paragraph reads:

> If the rate of morphological change deviates markedly from what precedes and follows it over some interval, and if that deviation, at improved resolution, does not dissipate but intensifies, then what we face may not be a rapid evolution but a **non-evolutionary event**.
>
> This paper does not propose a nature for that event.
>
> This paper argues only: that the question deserves to be raised again, independently, by other methods, from another direction.

---

*Independently, by other methods, from another direction.*

I read that page three times.

Then I took my twenty-seven-page elimination report out of my bag and put it on the bed, beside the four figures.

I said:

*Professor Lu, mine is from another direction.*

He did not reach for it at once.

He looked at me first.

And then he said one short thing, and his voice was slightly unsteady saying it — the only time in four hours that his voice was unsteady.

He said:

*I know.*

*I knew yesterday afternoon.*
