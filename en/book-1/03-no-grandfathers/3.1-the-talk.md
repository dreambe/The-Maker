# 3.1 · The Talk

I prepared for six weeks.

In six weeks I wrote three versions of the script, made seven versions of the slides, and rehearsed eleven times in an empty meeting room to a row of chairs, recording three of them and listening back.

Listening to yourself speak is an extremely unpleasant thing. I recommend everybody do it once and then never again.

Twenty-two minutes.

That was my allocation: twenty for the talk, five for questions. I compressed the talk to eighteen and left two in reserve, knowing that I speed up when nervous, and that a person speaking quickly does not sound credible.

In the event I took twenty-two.

I went slower.

---

First, what kind of meeting it was.

A mid-sized annual conference in molecular evolution. Four days, three plenaries, a number of parallel sessions. Mine was in the session on comparative genomics of non-coding regions. Forty-odd people, a small hall.

I was scheduled last on the afternoon of the third day.

I should explain what that slot means, because people outside the field do not know.

The afternoon of the third day is the worst slot in a conference. The first two days people still have energy; the fourth is the close and everybody is already calculating their journey home. The third afternoon, people are tired, there is a dinner that evening, and the last talk — the position of the last talk is that **every person in the room is waiting for you to finish.**

Not rudeness. Physiology. After three days of consecutive talks, attention is simply like that.

When I got the programme I looked at that slot and my heart went down a little.

And I did not ask to be moved.

There is a great deal in this section about what I did not do, and the reasons are all much the same, and I have already written the most honest version of that reason at 2.8.

---

Now the six weeks.

What I want to write is not what I prepared, but what I **decided not to say.**

My material was:

One: the sequence and its anomalous conservation (Imhoff, 1987).
Two: the checksum relation.
Three: five weeks of contamination elimination, nineteen pages, four ancient samples.
Four: two months of coincidence elimination, twenty-seven pages, four hundred billion negative controls.
Five: the conclusion.

The first four can be presented. They are data, method, elimination — the language of the field.

The fifth is not.

The fifth, stated, is this sentence: **the existence of this sequence is incompatible with natural occurrence.**

Over six weeks I wrote three versions for that sentence.

**Version one (aggressive)**: say it. Put it on the last slide in bold.

**Version two (conservative)**: do not say it at all. Present one through four; final slide reads *this study suggests the region may be subject to a constraint mechanism not currently understood*; end.

**Version three (middle)**: do not say it, but in the discussion list four possible explanations, of which the fourth is *non-natural origin*, in one clause, undeveloped.

I rehearsed version three. Nine of the eleven rehearsals were version three.

---

Three minutes before I went up, I changed my mind.

What happened in those three minutes was this.

I was sitting at the side of the front row while the previous talk ran. My laptop was connected; the projector was fine. I was looking at the room.

Forty-odd people.

I counted the ones on their phones: nineteen.

Under half, and close to it.

The previous speaker was presenting something on transposable elements — a decent talk, solid material — and there were no questions. The chair asked twice whether there were questions, nobody raised a hand, and he said *let us thank the speaker*, applause, done.

At that moment I went to the last slide and deleted version three's clause: *fourth possibility: non-natural origin.*

About ten seconds.

Deleted, my talk became version two.

---

I should be clear about why I deleted it.

Not cowardice. Or not only.

I deleted it on a tactical judgement, and the judgement looked correct at the time:

**I cannot, in a session where nobody asks questions, in the last talk, in front of forty-odd people who have been tired for three days, present a conclusion that requires them to follow me three steps.**

For a person to accept *this sequence did not occur naturally*, they must first accept: the checksum is real; the contamination elimination is clean; the coincidence elimination is clean. Every one of those requires them to verify it themselves, and nobody verifies anything at five in the afternoon.

If I said that sentence, what would happen?

What would happen is: they would remember the sentence and not the data.

**A sensational conclusion eats its own evidence.**

And what I needed was for them to remember the data.

What I needed was for one or two of them to go home and compute that checksum themselves.

So I deleted it.

---

I went up.

I want to record those twenty-two minutes as accurately as I can.

**Minutes one to three: Imhoff.**

I opened with that 1987 German paper.

I described the journal, that it folded in 1990, and that the volume had not been borrowed since March 1988.

I opened with that because it is a good opening. It has a story in it; it makes people look up.

It worked. I saw several people put their phones down.

**Minutes four to eight: the sequence.**

Number, position, length, annotation history. I covered the thirteen years of *possible pseudogene*, the honest reannotation in 2006, and the zero-polymorphism list where it stands first — and where the gap between first and second is larger than the gap between second and one hundred and forty-seventh.

Here I put up the figure. It is the best figure I have ever made: one hundred and forty-seven points, polymorphism on the horizontal, nothing on the vertical (just spread), and at the far left one solitary point, a long way from all the others.

There is not one word of my interpretation on that figure. It is only data.

I stood on that slide for about twenty seconds without speaking.

Those twenty seconds are the best twenty seconds of the talk.

More people looked up.

**Minutes nine to fourteen: the checksum relation.**

The hardest part to present, requiring the audience to take on a concept from outside biology.

I used the supermarket receipt. I said: when you buy something, that row of digits under the barcode — the last one is not part of the code, it is computed.

Three minutes on checksums.

The room was quiet through those three minutes.

And I know that quiet has two kinds: *I am listening*, and *I am waiting for this to be over.*

I could not tell which. The person on the platform cannot tell, which is the hardest thing about being on the platform.

Then I gave the result: the last one thousand one hundred and eighty-three bases equal a definite operation over the preceding three thousand five hundred and forty-nine.

I gave the sixteen characters.

I said: this is the result of my seventeenth run. (I still thought it was seventeen then.)

**Minutes fifteen to twenty: elimination.**

Contamination: five weeks, nineteen pages, four ancient samples, deamination-damaged reads only.

Coincidence: two months, twenty-seven pages, four hundred billion negative controls, zero false positives.

I went fast, because I was short of time, and because this is the part I am most confident in — this part is entirely standard, unimpeachable work the field recognises.

**Minutes twenty-one to twenty-two: the close.**

My final slide read:

> This study reports a non-trivial arithmetic relation within a highly conserved non-coding sequence of the human genome.
>
> The contamination hypothesis and the coincidence hypothesis are both eliminated.
>
> This study offers no explanation of the phenomenon.
>
> This study holds that the phenomenon merits independent replication.

Four lines.

The last one I added in those three minutes before going up, at the same time as deleting the fourth possibility.

*Merits independent replication.*

It is the nearest thing to a distress call that I could say in that room.

---

I finished.

I said *thank you.*

Applause.

I want to write about the applause, because applause carries information.

Forty-odd people clapping: its duration, its density, its rise and fall are all distinguishable. A talk that has caught interest holds applause until the chair speaks; a mediocre talk's applause disperses after four seconds.

That day it went about five seconds.

Not bad. Not good.

The chair said: *Thank you, Dr Shen. We have five minutes for questions.*

---

Silence.

I stood there.

I want to be precise about that silence, because it is the passage I have gone back to most in the three years since.

It lasted about eight seconds.

Eight seconds is a long time in a question period. Normally a hand goes up within two or three — usually the chair's, or a friend of the speaker's, or an elderly man who always asks something.

That day, eight seconds, nobody.

In those eight seconds I looked at the room.

I saw forty-odd faces.

Most of them wore the polite, vacated look of people waiting for a session to end. It is the commonest expression at a conference. There is no ill will in it. It is fatigue.

Several were packing — zipping bags, closing laptops. No ill will there either; they simply knew this was the last talk, and dinner was at half past six.

Two or three were looking at me with the expression that means *I did not follow that and I do not intend to ask.*

And in the back row, at the side, there was one person.

**He was not packing.**

He sat upright, both hands on his knees, eyes on the screen — which still had my last slide on it, four lines.

He looked at those four lines for the full eight seconds.

I did not know him.

My only impression at the time: an old man, white hair, an old jacket, back row, and not packing up like everybody else.

---

After eight seconds a hand went up.

Second row, a man in his forties, whose name I knew and whom I did not.

He said:

*Thank you for the talk. I have a question; possibly I missed something.*

*That operation you used — that is a function you defined yourself, isn't it?*

---

I said: I did not define it. It is a standard check function; I used a fixed candidate set of seventeen, fixed before testing.

He said: *Ah.*

He said: *And why those seventeen.*

---

I said: because they are standard. They are widely used in engineering practice. I constructed none of them.

He said: *Mm.*

And then he said one thing, briefly, and quite mildly, and it was the most important sentence of that afternoon.

He said:

*You still chose them.*
