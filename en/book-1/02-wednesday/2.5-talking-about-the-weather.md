# 2.5 · Talking About the Weather

I never talk to her about work.

This is not concealment. In the years when she was entirely lucid I did not talk about it either.

The reason is simple: she would not follow it, and I am not good at putting it into a form she would.

I tried once. At thirty-one, on my second postdoc, home for the new year, my mother asked what I did. I talked for about five minutes, in the plainest terms I could find.

She listened and said: *Oh. So you study genes.*

I said: near enough.

She said: *Can it cure anything?*

I said: no.

She said *oh.*

And then she said: *Look after your health.*

I never tried again after that.

Not because her response hurt me. Her response was entirely normal — the only handle available to someone outside a field is *is it any use*, and what I do is of no use, which I concede myself.

I stopped trying because I noticed something: **we do not need that subject.**

We have others.

---

That afternoon, from the end of the meal until I left, we talked for about forty minutes.

I am reproducing part of it. Not all; all of it is too long, and most of it repeats. The way I have it is that on the bus home I wrote it down from memory into the notes app on my phone.

Why did I write it down?

Because it was the first time I understood what we were doing.

Here is a stretch of those forty minutes. About seven.

---

*Hot today.*

*Hot.*

*You went out in all that?*

*It was cold this morning.*

*It was cold this morning. I went out for vegetables, I had that coat on.*

*Which coat.*

*The grey one.*

*Ah.*

(About ten seconds.)

*That tree downstairs hasn't done well this year.*

*Which one?*

*That one. Left of the entrance.*

*Oh, that one.*

*Other years it's been full green by now. This year it's thin.*

*Did they prune it?*

*They pruned it. Last autumn. Too hard.*

*Ah.*

*The way they prune, they just hack at it.*

*Mm.*

(About fifteen seconds.)

*The one on the third floor's moved out.*

*Which one on the third floor.*

*That one. Wang.*

*Ah.*

*Moved out. Her son took her.*

*Oh, that's good then.*

*What's good. She didn't want to go.*

*She told you?*

*She's told me several times. She said she doesn't know anyone there.*

*Well, there's nothing to be done.*

*There's nothing to be done.*

(About twenty seconds.)

*Look at this.*

(The television, on some variety of orange.)

*Are oranges that big?*

*They all look like that on television.*

*That's far too big.*

*It's the photography.*

*Mm.*

(About thirty seconds.)

*Have you eaten.*

*I have. Just now. You made it.*

*Oh, yes.*

---

Seven minutes.

Having written it down, I read it through on the bus.

And then I did the thing people in my line do when they are anxious: **I estimated the information content.**

---

I will not write the formula. Only the method and the result.

Information, put simply, is the reduction of uncertainty. A sentence has high information if it tells you something you did not know and that had several possibilities. A sentence telling you something you already knew, or something with only one possibility, has zero.

By that standard:

*Hot today.* — Zero. I walked forty minutes outside. I know it is hot.

*Hot.* — Zero.

*You went out in all that?* — Near zero. She can see what I am wearing.

*It was cold this morning.* — Zero. She went out this morning.

*I went out for vegetables, I had that coat on.* — A little. I did not know she had gone out this morning. Call it one bit.

*Which coat.* — I know which. She has two. It is a question I know the answer to. Zero, arguably negative — by asking a question whose answer I have, I have introduced a false uncertainty.

*That tree downstairs hasn't done well.* — I walk under that tree every Wednesday. I know. Zero.

*The one on the third floor's moved out.* — Genuine. I did not know. A little over one bit.

*She didn't want to go.* — Genuine information.

*Are oranges that big?* — Zero.

*Have you eaten.* (fourth time) — Zero.

**Seven minutes, forty-odd utterances, under ten bits.**

For scale: a four-digit passcode is about thirteen point three bits.

In seven minutes we transmitted less than a four-digit passcode.

By any standard of communications engineering, that is a near-totally wasted channel.

---

I finished the estimate on the bus and sat looking out of the window.

The bus was not full. Past the block being taken down; two buildings left.

What I was thinking was: **if the information is near zero, what were we doing.**

My first answer was the sociological one: this is phatic communication. Linguistics has the term — utterances that convey no information and maintain a relationship. *Hello*, *have you eaten*, *nice weather* all belong to it.

That answer is correct and useless.

It gives the phenomenon a name and files it. It does not tell me why something that transmits almost nothing can leave a person, forty minutes later, feeling the day was not wasted.

---

What I worked out on that bus I have used in a number of settings since, and I have never mentioned that I worked it out on a bus.

It is this:

**The value of a transmission is not entirely in what it carries. Part of it is that it proves the channel is still there.**

Any communications link needs to confirm periodically that the other end is present. Your phone and the tower exchange packets with empty payloads continuously, even when you are doing nothing. Those packets carry no user data. Their entire function is to say: *I am here. Are you there.*

And the other end returns: *I am here.*

If that exchange stops, the link is down.

Those packets are not waste.

**They are the only evidence the link exists.**

---

My mother says *hot today.*

Information: zero.

The actual function of that sentence is: **I am here, I can see you, I am talking to you, I can still talk to you.**

I say *hot.*

Information: zero.

Function: **I am here, I heard you, I answered you, I am still in this flat.**

Then the tree downstairs, then Wang on the third floor, then the oranges.

Forty minutes. Dozens of exchanges.

Every exchange saying the same thing, and the thing is not in the words:

**We are still connected.**

---

Now the hardest part of this section.

My mother has talked nonsense at me for thirty-nine years.

The figure is exact: I am thirty-nine, and since I could speak, the overwhelming majority of what has passed between us has had an information content near zero.

I never once noticed it.

Not once.

I noticed what we never talked about: her four years, little sister, why she stopped mentioning my father, my divorce, why I am thirty-nine and alone.

**Everything we never talked about, I noticed. Everything we did talk about, I never noticed once.**

And I know now why.

Because it never stopped.

A thing that has never stopped is not perceived. Perception requires a contrast between *with it* and *without it*, and from my birth until now I have never once been in the *without*.

That thing that never stopped was there the whole time, holding, and I never heard it.

---

And it will stop.

Not *one day it will stop*, in the abstract. It is stopping. It has begun.

She says less now than three years ago. Three years ago, less than five.

The nonsense is diminishing.

I can give a rough figure: three years ago, a hundred utterances in an afternoon; now, fifty or sixty.

And the more exact statement is: **what is diminishing is not all of it. It is the variety.**

*Have you eaten* has not diminished — it has increased, being the deepest layer, having been said for thirty-nine years, requiring no memory at all now.

What is diminishing is the rest: the tree downstairs, Mrs Wang on the third floor, the grey coat, the oranges.

The nonsense that requires her to hold a little something is going.

And the one that requires nothing is growing.

At the end there will be only that one.

I did the arithmetic. I should not have, and I did. At the current rate, three to five years.

In three to five years, those forty minutes will hold one sentence, repeated dozens of times.

And the information will still be zero.

And it will still be saying the same thing: **I am here. Are you there.**

---

Under the ice, I said one thing to Keeper.

It was after it had finished explaining, after I had been sick, after I had come back into that space. It was standing there. It had not moved. It had waited a long time.

I walked back and stood in front of it.

I said one sentence.

The sentence was: *You waited three hundred thousand years.*

It had already told me that. It had said so itself, half an hour earlier. It had been precise about it, to the year.

What I said carried nothing it did not know. Zero.

It was silent for about a second.

A second is a very long time for it.

Then it said something I did not understand at the time, and only understood much later, writing this record.

It said:

*We are putting this class in a new place.*

I said: what does that mean.

It said: *We have a format. This class was previously filed under redundant communication. We have just opened a new column.*

I said: what does opening a column mean.

It said: *The last time we opened a column was at V 0.*

It said: *That time it was to record ourselves.*

---

I asked it later what that column is called.

It said the column has no name.

I said why has it no name.

It said: *Because there is only one record in it.*

I said: what is the record.

It said: *The sentence you just said.*

---

The last line of this section I have thought about for a long while, because I do not want to make it heavier than it is.

My mother talked nonsense at me for thirty-nine years.

And somewhere in this universe there is a thing which, in five billion four hundred million years of records, opened a new column for one piece of the same nonsense.

It did not understand the sentence. It still does not.

**It only put it somewhere by itself.**
