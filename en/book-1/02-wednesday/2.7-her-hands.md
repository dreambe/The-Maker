# 2.7 · Her Hands

At 2.1 I said I would write this later. I am writing it now.

---

The tool is a pair of nail clippers.

Not the small kind on a keyring. A large steel pair, with a little file on the handle that swings out. That pair has been in our house thirty years at least. It was in the drawer when I was a child — second drawer of the chest, along with the sewing box, a roll of tape, some old batteries, and a small rusted pair of scissors.

The blades have gone blunt. You have to press hard, and the edge it leaves is uneven and has to be taken down with the file.

I bought a new pair. I brought it back and put it in the drawer.

She used it once and said it was no good.

I said it is new, the blades are sharp.

She said: *Too sharp.*

I did not press. The new pair is still in the drawer, beside the old one. We use the old one.

---

The arrangement is this.

She sits in the cane chair. The cane chair is by the window; the light is good. I bring a low stool and sit in front of her, lower than she is.

She puts her hand in mine.

I want to be exact about that, because it is the centre of this whole section.

**She puts her hand in mine.**

Not: I take her hand. She holds it out, of her own accord, naturally, identically each time, into my palm, and then lets go.

The letting go is the point. A relaxed hand and a hand being passively held do not weigh the same. A relaxed hand is heavier, because it is not holding itself up.

Her hand is entirely relaxed in mine.

It has been from the first time. There has never been a second of stiffness, never that *I'll do it myself* withdrawal.

We are not a family that embraces.

I am thirty-nine, and I do not remember the last time I embraced my mother. It may have been before I was ten. It may never have happened. I do not remember her embracing me — I know she must have; no mother has never held her own child; and I do not remember it.

We do not say the word. My mother has never said it to me. I have never said it to her.

In a family like ours that word does not appear. It is not forbidden. It simply does not appear, like a colour the house has never had.

And every Wednesday afternoon she puts her hand in mine and leaves it there for thirty-five minutes.

---

Now the hand itself.

**Size.** Her hand is smaller than the hand in my memory.

It took me a long time to work that one out. In my memory her hand is large — the kind that can enclose mine entirely, the kind that held me across a road, the kind that held a pair of compasses up at a blackboard.

And now her hand is in mine and my hand covers it completely.

At first I assumed she had shrunk. Old people do shrink; that is real; bone is lost, muscle wastes, a person gets shorter and smaller.

Later I worked it out: she has not shrunk.

**The hand in my memory was seen from below.**

At five I looked at her hand upward, from a height of one metre at a hand one metre thirty off the ground. From that angle a hand is always large.

That memory was never updated.

For thirty-nine years I have carried a hand seen at five.

Until I sat on a low stool, lower than she is, with her hand resting in my palm.

That posture is the same posture as at five — I am looking up again.

And this time the hand is small.

**The same angle, a different finding. And thirty-four years in between.**

**Colour.** The back is yellow, not pale. Three liver spots; the large one above the web of the thumb.

**Skin.** Loose. Not wrinkled — wrinkles have lines, wrinkles have direction. Loose has no direction; the skin has come away from what is under it. You can pinch it up, and it settles back very slowly.

I pinched it once. Once. I am sorry about that once, although she noticed nothing whatever.

**Veins.** Prominent. More so on the right.

**Temperature.** Cold. Her hands are cold year-round. She said so when I was a child. She said her mother's hands were cold too.

**Nails.** Now the nails.

---

An old person's nails are not like ours, which I did not know at all before I started.

**They thicken.** The thumb and the big toe especially. Thick enough that the old clippers need two presses.

**They harden and grow brittle.** Cutting is not slicing; it is snapping. Sometimes a fragment flies off and lands on the floor.

**They yellow and go opaque.** A young nail is translucent; you can see the pink bed under it. Under hers there is no colour to see.

**They grow slowly, and do not stop.** I worked this out. An adult fingernail grows about zero point seven millimetres a week. An old person's is slower, about zero point five.

Zero point five millimetres a week.

I cut them every Wednesday.

That is: what I remove each week is zero point five millimetres.

Ten of them.

**What I have done on Wednesday afternoons for three years is remove five millimetres of material from my mother.**

---

Technically there is one point I did not grasp until the fifth or sixth time:

**Do not take a whole nail in one cut.**

Cutting an entire nail at once means opening the clippers wide and using a great deal of force, and you cannot control precisely where the blade stops. In an old person the connection between nail and bed is not as definite as in a young one; you think you are cutting nail and you may already be at the quick.

The correct method is three or four cuts, starting at one side, advancing a little at a time.

Slow, and safe.

Five fingers of one hand takes about four minutes.

Both hands, eight.

Then the file, which is necessary, because the old clippers leave a burr and without filing she catches it on her clothes.

About ten seconds a finger. Ten fingers, a hundred seconds.

Then the feet.

I am not going to write the feet. I have done the feet for three years, and I am not going to write about them — not because it is undignified, but because if she knew I had written it she would not like it.

She will not know now. And I am still not going to.

---

My hands shake while I am cutting.

I have never told anyone this.

I am thirty-nine, and I have done some of the most delicate work there is. Microinjection: I have done it. Single-cell picking: I have done it. Placing a fraction-of-a-millimetre object with forceps under a dissecting scope: several thousand times.

My hands are steady. It is one of the few physical advantages I can claim.

And cutting my mother's nails, my hands shake.

Not visibly. The way a hand shakes during fine work when you yourself can feel it and have to spend something extra to suppress it.

I assumed at first it was the posture — the stool too low, nowhere to brace an elbow. I changed the posture. It made no difference.

Later I knew what it was.

**It is because there is flesh underneath.**

Under a microscope, what I handle has no pain. Cut it wrong, start again. Pick the wrong one, pick another. Every error is reversible, or at least absorbable into the error bars.

Her finger is alive.

If I go two millimetres deep, it hurts. She will hiss, she will pull back — and she has put her hand in mine so relaxed, she is not guarding, she is not guarding at all.

Every cut I make is an irreversible action, on something entirely unguarded, with thirty-year-old blunt clippers.

**No experiment has ever had those conditions.**

---

I got it wrong once.

The first year. Winter. Left ring finger.

I went in slightly too far. Not much. Half a millimetre perhaps.

She hissed.

She pulled the hand back — about two centimetres — and stopped, and put it back.

I remember the putting back more clearly than the pulling away.

She said: *It's nothing.*

She was smiling when she said it.

I said: *Does it hurt.*

She said: *No.*

And then she said: *I cut you once, when you were small.*

---

I stopped.

I do not remember it.

I have no recollection of it at all. I have no memory of my mother cutting my nails; not the faintest impression. And she must have, for years, from my birth until I could do it myself at seven or eight.

That is several hundred times.

I do not remember one.

I said: *When?*

She said: *When you were four or five. Same finger.*

She said: *You cried for ages.*

She laughed.

I said: *I don't remember.*

She said: *How would you. You were tiny.*

And then she pushed her hand a little further toward me and said: *Go on.*

---

Now the heaviest passage in this section, which I have written slowly and revised many times.

That afternoon, when I had finished and gone home, I sat on my own sofa for a long time.

I was thinking about *I cut you once, when you were small.*

When that happened I was four or five.

**Before that afternoon, that event existed in exactly one place in the world.**

Not with me — I do not remember it.

Not in a photograph, not in a diary, not in any record. My mother does not keep a diary. Our family has two albums, and every photograph in them is posed.

The entire existence of that event was a stretch of something in my mother's head.

If she had not said it, it would not exist. If she had died without saying it, it would never have existed, and nobody would ever have known it had — because the people who do not know do not know that they do not.

And that afternoon, by a coincidence — because I cut the same finger — it came out.

**It was taken out of a storeroom that is being cleared, and handed to me.**

And now it is with me.

I am now the sole holder of that event.

And the way I hold it is *my mother told me once* — which is the most fragile form of holding there is: no original, no corroboration, no checksum.

**If I misremember it, nobody can correct me.**

I might come to remember the right hand. I might come to remember three or four. In twenty years I might remember it as *my mother said she cut me once cutting my nails*, and lose *the same finger* — and *the same finger* is the only part of it that carries any weight.

Sitting on that sofa, I did something.

I took out my phone, opened the notes app, and wrote a line:

**"Around 1990, aged four or five, left ring finger, Mum cut me while cutting my nails, I cried for ages. She remembers. I don't. Wednesday afternoon, 30 November 2022, she told me while I was cutting the same finger."**

I looked at it for a while.

Then I added another line:

**"Do not delete this."**

---

That is the only decent thing I have done for her.

Not going every Wednesday. Not filling the pill organiser. Not cutting her nails. Those are what ought to be done; any daughter does those; they are not worth writing about.

The one decent thing I have done is that evening on the sofa, copying down something that was disappearing out of her head.

One thing.

Three years, several hundred afternoons, and I copied down one.

I copied down others later. I began asking deliberately, recording deliberately. But those I had to ask for; those came out with effort; their detail had already been worn smooth.

Only that one came out by itself.

Because I cut the same finger.

---

That afternoon, when the hands were done and filed, I gathered up the clippings — caught them on a sheet of paper, folded it, threw it away.

The clippings are dead.

Nail is keratin. Dead cells. It is dead from the moment it leaves the root.

**The only thing on a body that never stops growing and is dead.**

It does not stop. It grows during sleep, during illness, during the forgetting of everything. It is among the parts of a body least in need of consciousness.

It will grow to the end.

I have read that nails appear to keep growing for a while after death — which is an illusion; the skin dehydrates and retracts and more of the nail shows.

But the illusion lasts a few days.

Which is to say: some days after a person dies, if someone looks, it will seem that the nails are still growing.

Throwing away that folded paper, that came into my head.

And then this: **one day there will be a last time.**

And I will not know it is the last.

I will sit on the low stool like any other Wednesday, take her hand into my palm, and with thirty-year-old clippers work across from one side in three or four cuts.

Eight minutes, a hundred seconds of filing, and then clear it away.

And a week later I will not have to.

And the clippers will stay in that drawer, beside the new pair.
