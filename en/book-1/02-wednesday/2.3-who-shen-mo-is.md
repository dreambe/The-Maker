# 2.3 · Who Shen Mo Is

My mother is called Shen Mo.

*Mo* as in jasmine. Her father chose it, the reason being — so the story goes — that the jasmine in the courtyard flowered particularly well the year she was born. I have heard that account many times, from her.

I was thirty-seven before I learned it was not quite right.

My aunt told me later — not *little sister*; the other one, the one still living. She said there was never any jasmine in that courtyard. Her father took the character out of a book. He could not read much, and copying it he got one stroke wrong. It was originally a different character.

When I told my mother, she was already ill.

She listened and said: *Nonsense. We had jasmine.*

She said it with total certainty.

I said: all right. Then you did.

---

I want to set down in this section who my mother is.

And I have to say first something I only worked out in the course of writing it: **I do not know my mother very well.**

That sounds like self-reproach, like one of those lines about children realising too late. It is not. It is a technical statement.

What I know is the stories she told.

And the stories she told are the ones she chose to tell. What she told, I heard a hundred times; what she did not tell, I never heard once. And it never occurred to me to ask what relation a thing told a hundred times bears to a life.

What I hold is not my mother.

**What I hold is a copy my mother edited herself.**

And now the original is being cleared.

And the copy is with me, and I cannot verify it, because the person who could verify it is the original.

---

Here is the copy.

**Shen Mo, born 1951.** An older brother, two younger sisters. The brother went away in the sixties and afterwards they were not much in touch. Of the sisters: one is the aunt still living, and one is *little sister.*

*Little sister* belongs to the next section.

**She finished school in 1969.** A great deal was happening that year; I will not write it. She did not go straight to university, because at that time one did not. She went to a farm. Four years.

Those four years she almost never spoke about.

I asked as a child. Her answer was *it was what it was.* If I asked again she would say *what is there to tell*, and change the subject.

A woman who told her life a hundred times had four years she never mentioned.

I noticed that blank only much later. And by the time I noticed, there was no asking.

**In 1973 she came back to the city.** Two years in a neighbourhood workshop; I do not know at what. She said *making parts.*

**In 1977 she got into university.**

This is the passage she told most — not fewer than two hundred times, the details identical every time, word for word. I know now that a memory told two hundred times is no longer a memory. It is a text repeatedly transcribed, and every transcription draws nearer to the last one, until it becomes extremely stable, extremely smooth, and extremely suspect.

Her version: she heard the news at the workshop, went home and told her mother she was going to sit the exam, and her mother said nothing; she worked days and read at night, with no lamp, using a torch; she sat it twice, and missed the first time; the second time she got in, teacher training, geography.

Her voice always went up a little on *teacher training, geography.*

I heard that rise for thirty-odd years.

**Graduated in 1981, posted to that secondary school.** She taught there until she retired in 2006. Twenty-five years.

She worked in one place her whole life. Not unusual in her generation, and nearly impossible in mine. I have moved through three countries in five years, and my mother sat in the same office for twenty-five, third desk from the window.

I have been in that office. I went as a first-year, when I was thirteen. On her desk there was a tin chalk box, an enamel mug, a stack of papers, and a pair of compasses — the big teaching kind, black, wooden-handled, for drawing circles on a blackboard.

---

Now the contour lines.

I should explain what they are, because most people met them once in a school geography lesson, and met them as a fact to be memorised.

A mountain is three-dimensional. A sheet of paper is two. To put the mountain on the paper, and have someone see its height and its steepness.

One method is shading. That is drawing.

The other method is contours.

Contours work like this: you cut the mountain with a series of horizontal planes, into layers, and project each cut vertically onto the paper as a closed curve.

You get rings inside rings.

Where the lines are close, the slope is steep. Where they are far apart, it is gentle. The lines will not tell you by themselves whether this is a hill or a hollow — for that you read the labelled figures. The height difference between adjacent lines is fixed, and that fixed value is called the contour interval.

There is something here I never once thought about at school, and thought about only after my mother became ill:

**Not one line on a contour map exists.**

There are no lines on a mountain. A mountain is a continuous slope. Those lines are cuts at chosen heights, and which heights are chosen is a convention.

Every line you see on the paper is a thing that is not there.

And the whole sheet is made of things that are not there, and it is exact — a trained person looking at it can stand the mountain up again in their head, can see which face gets the sun, where you can walk, where water will pool, which gully a flood will come down.

**You take a set of lines that do not exist and hand someone a shape they cannot see.**

My mother drew them better than anyone in the school.

---

*Better than anyone in the school* is not my phrase. A teacher called Wu said it to me when I was thirteen.

His exact words: *Your mother draws the best contour lines in this school.*

I was thirteen. My reaction was: mm.

At thirteen I did not know that was a heavy sentence. I took it for the kind of thing adults say to children.

At thirty-seven I went through all nineteen volumes of her lesson plans on the third shelf, one at a time. It took an afternoon.

Nineteen volumes, 1976 to 2001. The paper in the early ones is brittle; you have to turn it slowly.

There are several hundred hand-drawn figures inside.

I should be exact about what I saw.

The lines are blue-black ink, fountain pen, drawn in one movement. No trace of pencil underneath — I held them up to the light; there is none.

Rings inside rings, outer to inner, the spacing as even as print. And it is not even — contours should not be even; where the slope is steep the lines should crowd. The crowding is accurate, and what is uniform is the weight of each line, the pressure, the smoothness of each turn.

One stroke down. No stopping, no going over, no correction.

There are sometimes forty or fifty closed curves on one sheet, and every one has to be right, because contours may not cross, may not break, may not touch themselves (except at a cliff, and a cliff has its own convention).

Forty or fifty lines, one after another, all correct.

Somewhere around the seventh volume that afternoon I did something I have never done. I took a pen and a sheet of paper and tried to draw one.

I could not.

My line shook. I stopped halfway and had to pick it up again, and you can see the join. My closed curve did not meet itself at the start.

I tried for about twenty minutes and produced seven or eight sheets, none of them any good.

I was thirty-seven. I had spent more than a decade doing precise laboratory work. My hands are not clumsy.

And I could not draw one line my mother drew without looking.

---

Now the volume that lies permanently open at the same page.

It is at the top of the third shelf, spread open, and has been since she became ill. I closed it once; the next Wednesday it was open again at the same page. I have not closed it since.

There is a figure on that page.

It is unlike the others. The others are teaching figures, redrawn from a textbook or an atlas. This one is surveyed.

How I know it is surveyed: there is data beside it. Pencil, small dense figures — station numbers, bearings, distances, height differences. That is field recording. That is a person standing on a hill with an instrument, taking it point by point, and drawing it afterwards.

And in the bottom right corner, in small characters:

*1977.8 · fieldwork · day three*

August 1977.

Not drawn as a teacher. Drawn while she was still a student, on fieldwork, on the third day.

She was twenty-six.

---

I know which hill it is.

Because she told me a hundred times.

She told it as: on that fieldwork she slid down a slope, opened her eyebrow on a stone, three stitches, and that is where the scar above her eyebrow came from. And she always told it with enormous satisfaction, because —

*The map in my hand did not get wet.*

That is the last line of the story. A hundred times. Word for word.

Going down, she instinctively lifted the map. She fell, her face opened, the map stayed dry.

She laughed telling it. She would do the gesture, the lifting. She would say *your grandmother was furious.*

That figure is the map from that day.

It is the best thing she ever drew.

And she keeps that volume open at that page, spread on the shelf.

I do not know when she started leaving it that way. Possibly after she retired. Possibly earlier. Possibly she turned to that page one day and never closed it.

---

One Wednesday last winter I pointed at the figure and asked: *Mum, where is this?*

She came over and looked.

She looked for about ten seconds.

Then she said: *This one.*

Then she said: *This is —*

And stopped.

She looked a while longer, and then she said: *That's drawn well.*

I said: it is drawn well.

She said: *Who drew it?*

---

I did not tell her.

I do not know why I did not tell her. I could perfectly well have said: *Mum, you drew this. 1977. On fieldwork. The time you came down that slope.* She might have remembered a little. She might not. She might have been pleased.

What I said was: *No idea. It was in there.*

She said *oh*, and went off to the kitchen.

I stood in front of that volume for a while.

I was thinking about something very concrete: **that hill is now only here, with me.**

The story, the date, the scar above the eyebrow, the lifting gesture, *your grandmother was furious*, *the map in my hand did not get wet* —

the original can no longer be read out.

And the copy is with me. And the copy is the version she transcribed a hundred times: smooth, stable, possibly at variance with the facts, possibly with no jasmine in it at all, possibly with her losing her footing rather than a stone coming loose.

I cannot verify it.

The person who could was in the kitchen, taking eggs out of a plastic tray one at a time and putting them into a different bowl.

---

One restrained sentence to end this section, because I do not intend to write the rest of it as a metaphor.

My mother drew contour lines all her life.

The whole of what that means is: **you cannot see a shape directly. You can only infer it from a set of lines, and the lines themselves do not exist.**

And walking back to the hut under the ice, after all the explaining was over, that is what I thought of.

I did not say it to anyone. I do not want a single person supposing that my mother's life is an illustration I use for a philosophical problem.

She is not an illustration.

She was born in 1951, surname Shen, with a miscopied stroke in her given name, four years she never spoke of, two attempts at the exam, twenty-five years of teaching, third desk from the window, the best contour lines in the school, one fall at twenty-six, and the map stayed dry.

That is what there is.
