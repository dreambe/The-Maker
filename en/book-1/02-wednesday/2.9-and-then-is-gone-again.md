# 2.9 · And Then Is Gone Again

I finished the dishes, dried my hands, and went back to the front room.

She was still sitting there.

The television was still on; the advertisement had finished and the programme was back — the same fruit programme, done with oranges and on to some melon I had never heard of.

I sat down.

---

We said a few more things.

I am going to set them all down, because they are the last part of that minute, and I did not know it at the time.

I said: *Mum, is the water coming tomorrow?*

(Our building gets bottled water delivered, irregularly.)

She said: *Don't know. Last time was last week.*

I said: *I'll ask tomorrow.*

She said: *You could ask.*

A pause.

And then she said:

*That thing you brought last time — I ate it.*

I said: *Which thing.*

She said: *That … the soft one.*

I said: *The bread?*

She said: *That's it. Bread. It was good.*

---

That was the last one.

*It was good.*

I felt nothing at the time. I said *I'll get the same next time*, she said *no need, no need*, I said *it isn't expensive*, she said *don't buy so much.*

Normal. All of it normal.

We watched about ten more minutes of television.

She did not speak in those ten minutes.

---

At about ten to five she said:

*Have you eaten.*

---

I said: *I have. At midday. You made it.*

She said: *Oh, yes.*

---

In that second I knew.

Not because of the sentence — she says it five times an afternoon; it is part of the thirty-nine-year apparatus.

I knew because **her voice had gone back.**

I know that voice too well. It is the voice of a person asking for the first time. The voice of a person who genuinely wants to know whether I have eaten, and genuinely does not remember having asked, and is genuinely concerned about me.

That voice is a good voice. That voice is the voice I hear five times every Wednesday.

And for the preceding half-hour or so, her voice had not been it.

---

In that second I did something.

I am writing it down, because it is the thing in this whole affair I can least forgive myself for.

I said:

*Mum, did I come last Wednesday?*

---

I asked a question I knew the answer to.

I was testing her.

At ten to five, in my mother's front room, I used a question whose answer I had in order to measure whether a thing was still there.

---

She said:

*You did.*

Entirely naturally. No hesitation, no searching, none of the *let me think* pause.

She said *you did*, with a faint puzzlement in it — as though I had asked something obvious.

And then she said:

*Don't you come every week.*

---

That is correct.

I do come every week.

That is a statistically correct sentence.

**She had replaced a memory with a regularity.**

It is one of the commonest mechanisms of this disease; I have read a great deal about it. Having lost a specific memory, a person fills the gap with a general rule, and fills it seamlessly, and has no idea whatever that it is a filling.

She was not lying. She was not covering. In her world, in that second, she said what is true: her daughter comes every week, so she came last week.

The inference is sound.

Only the fact of that one week is wrong.

---

And half an hour earlier, what she had said was:

*You didn't come last Wednesday.*
*I know.*
*I waited until five.*

---

Two versions.

The same person. The same afternoon. About thirty-five minutes apart.

The first is true, and is a fact that could not be produced by any regularity — it requires memory, and it is precise to *five o'clock.*

The second is false, and is perfectly produced by a regularity, seamless, delivered in a natural voice.

**And she does not know that they are two versions.**

To her they are not two answers. They are one — I asked her a question at ten to five and she answered it.

The preceding half-hour is not in her record.

---

I have to be plain about what I did, because being plain is the only thing available to me.

I administered an MMSE.

Not formally. No instrument, no thirty points, no doctor.

And methodologically it is exactly the same act:

**I used a question to which I knew the correct answer, in order to measure whether a function was still present.**

At 2.6 I spent a long passage saying that the instrument cannot reach who my mother is. I said those doctors measure only the measurable part. I said it is the same act as machines measuring the axial ratios of standing bodies.

And that afternoon, at ten to five, I did the same thing.

And I did it worse.

Because when a doctor does it, it is in order to prescribe, to stage, to make a correct judgement at the scale of tens of thousands. When a doctor does it, it is useful.

**When I did it, it was to find out whether that minute was still there.**

It was already gone. And after it was gone, I confirmed with a test that it was gone.

I got nothing.

I only made her answer one more question.

---

Now the hardest part of this section.

**I do not know when it ended.**

I can give the start: 4:22, she turned her head and said *He.*

And the end?

*It was good.* Was that voice the good voice or the ordinary one? Looking back now I think ordinary. And at the time I did not notice.

*You could ask*?

*Don't know, last time was last week*? — that one contains a specific temporal fact (*last week*), which suggests something was still there.

And *last time was last week* may also be a regularity filling in — the water does come about weekly.

I cannot decide.

I have run those few sentences through my head several hundred times, from every angle, with all my professional training, trying to locate the turn.

**I cannot find it.**

There is no line between those sentences.

It is not *this one is still there and the next one is not.* It is: some are, some are uncertain, some are not, and their order is unclear, and the transition between them cannot be seen.

---

Much later I worked out what it is like.

It is like daylight.

I wrote in the first section: you sit in a lit room with a black window, and then for some length of time you do not look at the window, and when you look again the window is grey. Nobody has ever seen the part in between.

I sat through three weeks of sunrises in that laboratory and never once caught the moment.

And that afternoon I sat in my mother's front room, present throughout, my whole attention on her, not one second elsewhere —

**and I did not see it end.**

Daylight has no boundary.

Neither did that minute.

---

There was no act of *going.*

I have used *gone again* in the title of this section, and it is inaccurate, and I concede it here.

*Gone* implies a thing that was here and then departed, and departure is an event that can be witnessed.

What actually happened is: for some stretch of time she was that way, and for some stretch of time she was not, and between the two there is no identifiable point.

Let me use a more accurate formulation, though it is uglier:

**That is not a person leaving. That is one distribution of states becoming another, across some width.**

That is an instrument's sentence, a clinical sentence, without feeling in it.

And it is accurate.

And I am writing it because in this matter accuracy matters more than being moving.

---

At twenty past five I left.

I put my shoes on at the door and she stood beside me.

She said: *Don't buy so much next Wednesday.*

I said: *All right.*

She said: *You have to eat too.*

I said: *All right.*

I opened the door, stepped out, turned round. She stood inside it with a hand on the frame. The landing light came on as I moved and made her face a bad white.

She said: *Go on, then.*

---

I went down ninety-six stairs.

I sat down on the concrete step at the bottom.

There was sun that day.

I sat a long time. Much longer than usual.

I was thinking about *I waited until five.*

I was thinking: where is that sentence now.

It is with me.

**Only with me.**

By ten to five she no longer held it. She does not remember saying it. She does not remember waiting. She does not remember that on that afternoon she sat in the cane chair from two o'clock until five.

That afternoon, those three hours, that five o'clock —

their entire existence now is a stretch of something in my head, and a line I would write in my phone a few hours later.

And the stretch in my head is my memory.

And my memory carries no checksum.

---

I sat on that step for about forty minutes.

When the light began to go I got up and took the bus.

Past the block being taken down. Two buildings left.

I took out my phone, opened the notes app, and wrote:

**"Wednesday 12 April 2023, 16:22 to approx. 16:50. She said: you didn't come last Wednesday. I know. I waited until five. She said: you're on your own. She said: have you got enough money. She said: the weather's bad today, go home early (the weather was fine). Ended 16:5x; the moment of ending cannot be determined."**

I read it through.

And added a line:

**"Do not delete this."**

That is the second entry in that notes file.

The first is about 1990, aged four or five, left ring finger.

I have two now.
