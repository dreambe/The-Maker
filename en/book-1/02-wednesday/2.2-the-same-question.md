# 2.2 · The Same Question

*Have you eaten.*

She asked me four times that afternoon.

The first was as I came in, in the entryway, before I had changed my shoes.
The second was about ten minutes after we came out of the kitchen and sat down.
The third was with the television on, the programme about fruit from somewhere, in the middle of a kind of orange I had never heard of.
The fourth was as I was leaving, putting my shoes on at the door.

Four times.

I answered four times.

What I want to write down is how I answered, because it is a craft, and it took me a long while to learn it, and in the course of learning it I did something I do not intend to forgive myself for.

---

First, the question itself.

*Have you eaten* is not, where I come from, a question about food.

It is a greeting. It can mean *hello*. It can mean *I see you*. It can mean *I am concerned about you*. It can mean *I do not know what to say but I want to say something*. A Chinese person says those three words in a lifetime far more often than they actually wish to know the state of anyone's digestion.

This matters, because it means: **the sentence is repetitive under normal conditions too.**

Before she was ill, my mother might ask it twice in an afternoon. That was nagging. That was a mildly irritating, entirely normal, entirely healthy nagging. I would say *you've asked*, and she would say *oh — well, have you*, and we would both laugh a little.

So between ill and not ill there is, on this sentence, no clear line.

That is one of the hardest parts. Some symptoms have a line: she forgot how to work the television — that has a line. She went out and could not find her way home — that has a line. *Have you eaten* four times has no line. It only goes from twice to three times, from three times to four, from four to —

I do not know what it is now, because I stopped counting.

I counted for a while. I kept a tally in the notes app on my phone. About three months, and then I deleted it.

The day I deleted it I was on the bus, and afterwards I looked out at the block being taken down, and thought: what is counting for.

Will counting slow it?

No.

Counting can only tell me more precisely that it is speeding up.

---

Now the answering.

There is one requirement: **the tone must be identical every time.**

This is not politeness, or breeding, or filial piety. It is a technical specification.

Because for me it is the fourth time and for her it is the first.

I need to write that sentence again, because the whole of Chapter Two stands on it:

**For me the fourth. For her the first.**

She is not repeating. She has no such experience. When she asks, she genuinely wants to know whether I have eaten; she is genuinely concerned; she is genuinely opening the subject for the first time. There is no trace of *I asked a moment ago* in her voice, because in her world she has not asked.

So if there is a trace of the fourth time in mine — slightly faster, slightly lighter, with that faint closing-down cadence of *yes, yes, all right* — she will hear it.

Her ears are not ill. Her hearing is better than mine; she can tell when the television has changed presenters.

She will hear that my tone is wrong.

And she will not know why it is wrong, because in her world she has just asked her daughter a perfectly ordinary question, and there is something in her daughter's voice that she does not recognise.

That something will unsettle her.

A person who is unsettled and does not know why is in one of the worst positions available to anyone.

So:

**I have to make every time the first time.**

I have to act. Every Wednesday afternoon, four times, five times, six times.

---

The technical detail, honestly, because it is all true.

**Speed must match.** If the first answer was slow, the later ones must be slow. You cannot answer faster because you have answered already.

**Look up.** I look at her the first time. I must look at her the fourth time. Answering with your head down does not work however right the tone is, because she can see.

**The content must vary, and the amount of variation must be constant.** This is the hard one. Four identical answers are a recitation, and a recitation does not sound real. So they vary: *I have, I bought buns on the way*; *I have, I'm not hungry*; *I've eaten, Mum*; *I have, don't fuss.*

But the amplitude of the variation has to be controlled. Vary too much and it sounds like fobbing her off; vary too little and it sounds like going through the motions.

That calibration took me about a year.

**The hardest part is after the fifth.** The first four are manageable. By the sixth or seventh my body starts wanting the subject closed. The urge is physiological, like needing the lavatory, like wanting to lower an arm you have held up too long.

What I learned is: after the fifth, stop merely answering. Add a question back.

*I have. Have you?*

That works, because it moves the exchange forward and gives her something to do — she has to consider whether she has eaten. And it pleases her, because somebody is asking her.

The cost is that she generally does not remember whether she has, either.

So we go to the kitchen together and look in the pot.

We have looked in the pot many times. Walk to the kitchen, lift the lid, see whether anything has been touched. It is a ritual in our house, a shared, tacit, unnamed ritual: two people going to check a thing that one of them cannot hold.

Sometimes the pot is empty, which means she ate.
Sometimes it is full, which means she did not.
Sometimes it is half, which means she ate a little.

And in all three cases she says: *Oh yes, I ate.*

Three cases. One sentence.

---

Now the thing.

It was about a year ago, last spring.

I was in a bad way that day. I do not remember exactly why — that was before the checksum; the largest trouble in my life then was funding and review, things that look now like nothing at all.

What I remember is that I was tired, and that she asked especially often.

At the seventh, I said it.

I said: *Mum, you've asked already.*

I did not say it harshly. I was not impatient; I did not raise my voice. I was almost gentle.

And the two seconds after I said it, I will have for the rest of my life.

She checked.

Not visibly. It was the pause of a person walking forward who finds the ground one centimetre lower than expected. Extremely brief.

Then she said: *Oh.*

Then she laughed a little. That embarrassed laugh. The *I've forgotten again* laugh.

Then she said: *My memory.*

Then she looked down at her own hands.

And then she did not speak for the rest of the afternoon.

---

I want to be precise about the remainder of that afternoon, because I think I owe her a precise record of it.

From that sentence until I left there were about two hours and forty minutes.

In those two hours and forty minutes she said six things.

I counted. I sat there and counted.

The six were:

*Do you want water.* (I said no.)
*Shall I turn the television off.* (I said no need.)
*That thing —* (she did not finish it.)
*What's the date.* (I told her; she said *oh*.)
*What time are you going.* (I said five.)
*Take care on the way.* (At the door.)

Six.

Where, on a normal afternoon, she says — conservatively — over a hundred things. Most of them repetitions. Most of them *have you eaten* and its near relations.

With one sentence I turned a hundred into six.

---

Going down the stairs that day I stopped on the third flight.

I was not crying. I did not cry.

I stopped because I was working out something very concrete, which was: **what did I save.**

I said it because I was tired and wanted to save myself answering an eighth and a ninth time.

I saved perhaps twenty answers at three seconds each. One minute.

The price was two hours and forty minutes.

That is a very bad piece of accounting.

If there had been anyone beside me to run those numbers aloud, I would have seen instantly that I had done something stupid. There was nobody beside me, so I used that minute on the stairs and ran them myself.

The first thing I did when I finished was go back up.

I climbed the ninety-six again.

I rang the bell.

She opened it the second way — slow, slippers, the chain. Her face in the gap: not knowing me, and then knowing me.

She said: *What are you doing back.*

I said: *I forgot my phone.*

My phone was in my pocket.

I went in, made a show of looking around, and then said I had found it, down the side of the sofa.

And then I said: *Mum, I've eaten. I bought buns on the way.*

She said: *Oh.*

And then she said: *Well, don't go hungry.*

And then I left.

---

I have never said *you've asked already* since.

Not once.

This is not breeding, and I have not become a better person. It is a technical lesson I paid tuition for: **that sentence costs far more than it saves.**

I do not say this to appear composed. I say it because it is genuinely how I think about it, and I hold that honestly writing down an unflattering motive is nearer the truth than inventing a flattering one.

On many Wednesday afternoons, answering for the seventh time, I am irritated.

I am irritated, and I answer exactly as I did the first time.

Both are true at once, and there is no contradiction between them.

A person can be irritated and get the thing right. That is close to the sum of what thirty-nine years has actually taught me that is any use.

---

One last thing in this section, because it is what the section is really about, and I did not think of it at the time at all.

I thought of it under the ice.

Keeper was explaining to me why they had designed forgetting into us. It explained very clearly, very gently. It believed it was delivering good news.

The substance of it was: only a being that must continually know itself and can never complete the knowing will stop at the threshold. So we made you forget. Forgetting is protection. Forgetting keeps you from seeing yourselves whole all at once. Forgetting was our gift to you.

When it finished, I asked it a question.

I asked: *Did you ever work out what it is like to watch a person forget.*

It went silent.

Its silence was not awkwardness and not evasion. Its silence was a **search.** It was going through its records — five billion four hundred million years of records — for that item.

It searched for about three seconds. Three seconds is a very long time for it.

Then it said: *No.*

And then it said the thing that is the most frightening sentence I heard in the whole of this:

*We cannot conceive of that position. We have no one beside us.*

They share memory. They have never had a position of *standing beside and watching.* What any one of them knows, all of them know in the same instant. They have no observers, because they have no separation.

So when they designed forgetting —

they computed the benefit to the one who forgets with enormous precision. They considered lifespan, and the rate of clearance, and priority, and what should be kept and what discarded. They made it into a piece of fine craftsmanship.

And *the person beside them* is not a column in their table.

Because their world has no such column.

**They designed a gift, and they could not conceive of who might be standing next to the person receiving it.**

Crouched on the ice after being sick, what came back to me was not humanity, or civilisation, or four billion years.

What came back to me was a Wednesday afternoon. Two hours and forty minutes. Six sentences.
