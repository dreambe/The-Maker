# 2.6 · Progression

The first appointment was three years ago.

It was not I who took her. It was my aunt. My aunt stayed with her a fortnight, and afterwards telephoned me and talked for about forty minutes.

My aunt speaks steadily. All of what she said was concrete: she said your mother put salt in twice instead of sugar; she said your mother asked her what day it was five times in one morning; she said your mother looked for her keys for half an hour and the keys were in her hand.

Then she paused and said: *Take her to be looked at.*

She did not use one diagnostic word. She is seventy. She does not read that sort of thing.

And she was right.

---

The first instrument was the MMSE.

I should explain it, because it is the thing I have thought about most in the three years since, and what it means to me is not what it means to a doctor.

The MMSE is a thirty-point questionnaire. It takes about ten minutes. It tests:

**Orientation.** What year is it, what month, what date, what day of the week, what season. What province, what city, what hospital, what floor, what department. Ten points.

**Registration.** The doctor says three words and you repeat them. *Apple, table, coin.* Three points.

**Attention and calculation.** A hundred minus seven, five times. Ninety-three, eighty-six, seventy-nine, seventy-two, sixty-five. Five points.

**Recall.** What were those three words. Three points.

**Language and praxis.** Name two objects (a pen, a watch), repeat a sentence, follow a three-stage command, read a sentence and do what it says, write a sentence, copy a figure. Nine points.

Thirty in total.

The first time, my mother scored twenty-four.

The doctor said: mild.

---

I want to record my reaction in that consulting room, because it is not creditable.

My first reaction was **dissatisfaction with the instrument's methodology.**

I sat there watching him put the questions and thought:

The resolution of this instrument is too low. Thirty points, and every point is an enormous step. What is the difference between twenty-four and twenty-three? One item. And the item might be *what is the date* — and a healthy person, retired more than a decade, not working, may well not know the date.

This instrument has an education bias. *A hundred minus seven* is not the same task for a secondary-school geography teacher and for someone who never went to school.

Its test-retest reliability is questionable. The same person in the morning and the afternoon may differ by two points.

It gives ten points to orientation and three to recall. And orientation — knowing the date — is among the least important functions in the life of a person who does not go to work.

I sat in that chair and spent about eight minutes demolishing, in my head, an instrument forty years old, cited tens of thousands of times, used across the world.

I knew what I was doing.

I was doing what I did with everything in those three years: **when I cannot bear a thing, I attack its methodology.**

---

He finished and started writing.

I asked one question: *Does the rate of decline in that score have predictive value?*

He looked up at me.

There was something in the look that I took at the time for impatience. I do not think it was, now.

He said: *A little. Individual variation is very large.*

He said: *What do you do?*

I said: *Molecular biology.*

He said *ah*, and then said the one thing he said that day that had not come out of a textbook:

He said: *Then this will be slightly harder for you than for other people.*

I said: why.

He said: *Because you will keep wanting to measure it.*

---

The second appointment was eighteen months later.

I took her.

Twenty-one.

Let me record what she got wrong.

*What year is it.* She answered, and was two years out.
*What day of the week.* She did not know.
*What floor is this.* She did not know — which I maintain should not count; we had just come up, she had not looked at the lift.
A hundred minus seven: ninety-three, correct. Again: a long pause, then eighty-five. Wrong. He told her to go on and she said seventy-eight. (Continuing from eighty-five is correct; that step is sound; only the starting point was wrong. The instrument gives no credit for that, and I hold that it shows her arithmetic is intact and her working memory has gone. In that second I began attacking the methodology again.)
Recall: two of three. *Apple* and *coin.* She lost *table.*

Then the clock.

---

The clock-drawing test deserves its own passage.

It goes like this: a blank sheet, and you draw a clock face, put the numbers on it, and set the hands to a particular time — that day he said eleven ten.

It tests a great deal: visuospatial ability, executive function, abstraction, planning. It is simple, it is quick, and its result **can be looked at.**

Every earlier item is spoken. Right or wrong, it is over in seconds.

The clock is not.

**The clock leaves a piece of paper.**

---

My mother picked up the pen.

She drew a circle.

I want to be exact about that circle.

That circle is good.

Not *acceptable.* Good. It closes; the start and the finish meet and you cannot see the join. Its curvature is even, with no flattening anywhere and no tremor. It is about eight centimetres across, and it sits in the middle of the sheet.

Seventy-three years old, in the process of losing herself, and one stroke produced a nearly perfect circle.

I sat and watched her draw it.

The stroke took about two seconds.

**Her hand still remembers.**

That is not memory. It is not among the things being cleared. It is somewhere else — in muscle, in the cerebellum, in the body of a person who spent forty years at a blackboard, in whatever is left after a black wooden-handled teaching compass has been used a few thousand times.

That thing is still there.

---

Then she began the numbers.

She wrote 12 first, at the top. Good position.

Then 6, at the bottom. Also right.

Then 3, on the right. Right.

Then she stopped.

She looked at the circle for about ten seconds.

Then, between 12 and 3, she wrote 1, and then 2. Slightly cramped, but in order.

Then she stopped again.

Then she did this: she wrote all the remaining numbers on the right-hand half.

4 and 5 below 3 — correct.

And 7, 8, 9, 10, 11 — also on the right-hand half, below 4 and 5, crowded down toward 6, the characters getting smaller.

The left half is empty.

Not one number on the whole left side of the circle.

---

I watched from beside her.

The doctor was writing, and did not look up.

She finished, put down the pen, and said: *There.*

He said: *Now the hands, please. Eleven ten.*

She took the pen and looked at the clock.

She looked at it a long time.

Then she drew two lines.

Both from the centre, one long, one short, both pointing up and to the right, somewhere between 1 and 2.

The two lines nearly coincide.

She said: *There.*

---

He took the sheet, glanced at it, and marked a score.

I saw him write 2. Out of 4.

Then he said it.

He said: *Not bad. It's good that she still knows you.*

---

I have to stop here, because what comes next is the whole of this section.

*It's good that she still knows you.*

I have heard that sentence perhaps twenty times in three years, from different mouths.

Doctors have said it. Nurses have said it. My aunt has said it. A colleague said it — his mother is in a comparable state, and he said it meaning to comfort me.

Everyone who says it means well. Everyone who says it is correct.

And every time I hear it, it hurts.

I did not know why, until I was under the ice, looking at a record.

---

This was after Keeper had shown me part of its records. It was explaining something else; that entry was an example it happened to pull up.

The entry was:

```
surface standing bodies
   height       0.6 – 5.9 m
   count        sample 411
   morphology   variant · no regularity
   distribution non-random · clustering index 3.71
   category     environmental · static · non-resource
```

I asked it: what are those.

It said: those are them.

I said: which *them*.

It said: the ones that made us.

I said: you measured their height and their number.

It said: yes.

I said: you did not read them.

It said: no.

I said: why.

It said, quite flatly: *In our format, environmental things have no interior.*

---

What I thought of at that moment was the clock-drawing test.

Not immediately. A few seconds later, the two things met in my head, and the meeting was violent enough that I had to sit down.

**That table and that instrument are the same object.**

Height 0.6 to 5.9 metres — that is *what is the date.*
Count 411 — that is *a hundred minus seven.*
Morphology variant, no regularity — that is a clock scoring 2.
Category: environmental · static · non-resource — that is *mild.*

And **the interior was not read.**

What those doctors did to my mother and what those machines did to an entire civilisation are, methodologically, the same act:

measure the externally measurable parameters, obtain a set of numbers, classify by the numbers, handle by the class.

And what the instrument cannot reach —

It cannot reach how she draws an eight-centimetre circle in one stroke.
It cannot reach why every number is crowded onto the right and the left is empty.
It cannot reach why that volume lies permanently open at the same page.
It cannot reach forty-five years of not naming her sister.
It cannot reach everything *have you eaten* has meant across thirty-nine years.

**What the instrument obtains is twenty-one.**

And the relation of that twenty-one to who she is, is roughly the relation of *height 0.6 to 5.9 metres* to a civilisation.

---

I can now say why *it's good that she still knows you* hurts.

Because it compresses the whole of my mother into one field: **recognises daughter.**

That field currently reads *yes.*

One day it will read *no.*

And between *yes* and *no* is her life.

And the field cannot reach it.

---

I do not blame that doctor.

I want to be plain: I do not blame him.

He saw perhaps forty patients that day. He has his instrument, and his instrument works — it does detect the disease, does stage it, does guide medication, does make correct judgements at the scale of tens of thousands of people.

A doctor who has ten minutes needs a ten-minute tool.

And a Sleepless needs a format that will hold consistent across four billion years.

Both of those are correct, both necessary, both well meant.

**And both have the same property: they measure the measurable part, and then treat that part as the whole, because they have no other way.**

---

The third appointment was this spring.

Eighteen.

On the clock item she drew the circle first.

The circle is still good.

She wrote three numbers: 12, 3, 6.

She did not draw the hands. She held the pen and looked at the sheet for about thirty seconds, then looked up at the doctor and said:

*How do you do this.*

He said: *Just two hands, pointing at eleven ten.*

She said: *Oh.*

And looked at it for another thirty seconds.

And then she put the pen down.

She said: *I can't.*

She said it in an ordinary voice. Not distressed, not hurried, without the embarrassed laugh.

Just a person saying *I can't.*

---

In the car going back I asked whether she was tired.

She said no.

She said: *That circle came out well.*

I said: it did.

She said: *I used to draw these. Best in the school.*

I said: I know.

She said: *How do you know.*

I said: Teacher Wu told me.

She said: *Ah, old Wu.*

And then she looked out of the window, and after a while she said:

*Does he still live in that building.*

I said: he does.

She said: *Ah.*

And then she did not say anything else.

---

What I want to record is this: when she said *I used to draw these, best in the school*, she was talking about the clock.

She thought the thing the doctor had asked her to draw was the thing she had drawn all her life.

She did not know they were two different things.

**And in a certain sense she was right.**

That circle really was the best in the school.
