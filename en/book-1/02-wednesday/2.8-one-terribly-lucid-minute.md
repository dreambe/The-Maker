# 2.8 · One Terribly Lucid Minute

Last Wednesday I did not go.

In three years I have missed four.

The first, I had a fever of thirty-nine.
The second, a flight was cancelled.
The third, my aunt was there and we had agreed she would take that week.
The fourth is this one.

The first three had reasons. A reason means: if somebody asks, I can say it, and they will nod.

The fourth had a reason too.

I went to see somebody that day.

---

His name is Marcus Deng, twenty-nine, mathematics.

He was recommended by somebody at the Tracing Back, and casually — a line at the end of an email: *On that checksum business, you might talk to Marcus. He works on information theory. He's a bit odd.*

I wrote to him. He replied quickly: next Wednesday, two in the afternoon, meeting room on the third floor of such-and-such building.

I looked at *Wednesday, two in the afternoon* and sat there for about a minute.

Then I wrote back: fine.

---

I remember exactly what I was thinking in that minute.

I was thinking: I could ask him whether we could move it.

I entirely could. One email. One sentence. He is twenty-nine, he was put in touch in order to listen to me; if I said *I have a standing commitment on Wednesdays, could we do Thursday*, he would say yes with ninety-nine per cent probability.

I did not ask.

Why I did not ask — I have gone over this many times since, and I have had three versions of the answer.

**Version one** (at the time): I did not want to be a nuisance, or to make a demand before a first meeting.

I held that for about two days.

**Version two** (a week later): I did not want a twenty-nine-year-old who might help me to know that I have an immovable private commitment on Wednesday afternoons. Because that makes a person look encumbered, and in this line of work being encumbered is a disadvantage.

That version is more honest than the first, and it is still not it.

**Version three** (much later):

I did not ask because I was afraid he would say *Thursday, then.*

And if he said Thursday, I would have to concede: the thing can be moved.

And if the thing can be moved, then the ninety-six stairs I have gone down every Wednesday for three years are not an immovable fact but a thing I have been choosing all along.

**An immovable thing is fate. A thing you keep choosing is your responsibility.**

I did not want the second.

So I would rather miss one than admit it could be rescheduled.

---

I telephoned her.

I said: Mum, I can't come this Wednesday, I have to work late.

She said: *Oh, well, you get on.*

She said: *Working late is good, it means they need you.*

I said: mm.

She said: *Eat something.*

I said: I will.

We hung up.

Forty-two seconds.

I know the figure because it is in the call log.

---

That day I saw Marcus.

That meeting is one of the turning points of the whole thing, and I will write it elsewhere. Here only this: he listened for ten minutes and then said *you have got one thing wrong.*

We talked from two until twenty to six.

Three hours and forty minutes.

At twenty to six I looked at my phone.

In that glance I saw the time, and I thought nothing else at all.

---

The following Wednesday I went.

Everything was normal.

The first way of opening the door — she was behind it. She said *you've come*, I said *I've come*, I lifted the bag, she said *why do you buy so much*, I said *it isn't much.*

Inside, the smell, she went to the kitchen to reorder the shopping, I went to look at the organiser.

That week: Wednesday's compartment empty, the other six full — meaning that in the seven days I was not there she took them by the day for six days, and the seventh, that Wednesday's, she took on the day it was due.

Which means she had a good week. That is good news.

I put the organiser away.

We ate. She cooked. Two dishes. Afterwards we sat down, the television on.

From two until twenty past four everything was normal.

*Have you eaten* — five times.
The weather — covered.
The tree downstairs — covered.
She called me little sister — twice.
Oranges — the programme was on oranges again.

Everything normal.

---

Four twenty-two.

I know the time because I looked up the schedule afterwards.

At that moment the television was in an advertisement. She stopped watching it.

She turned her head and looked at me.

---

I want to write down that look, because it is the only one in three years.

Her eyes had not changed — I wrote at 2.4 that her eyes are always clear, that she always sees me.

What changed is **the depth of the focus.**

Ordinarily when she looks at me she is looking at my face. That is the normal look of a person looking at a face.

In that moment she was looking at me as though from somewhere further back.

I do not know how to describe it. The nearest I can get: ordinarily what I see is a person looking at me; in that moment what I saw was **a person looking out from inside herself, who had not been out before.**

Then she spoke.

---

She said:

*He.*

---

She said *He*.

In the preceding three weeks she had not once said *He*. It had all been *little sister*.

And she said it in an entirely ordinary voice, as she would have three years ago, with no emphasis whatever.

I said: mm.

And then she said the following.

---

*You didn't come last Wednesday.*

---

I sat there.

I said: I know. I had to work late.

She said: *I know.*

She said: *I waited until five.*

---

I have to stop here, because what comes next is me taking those three sentences apart one at a time, and I know that is ugly, and I have to do it.

**You didn't come last Wednesday.**

She remembers.

She does not remember whether I came last Wednesday — that is wrong; let me be exact: she generally does not remember any particular Wednesday. She does not remember the one before, or the one before that. Ask her *what did I bring you last time* and she cannot answer.

And she remembers **the one I missed.**

**I know.**

She remembers the telephone call. She remembers what was in it.

Forty-two seconds, a week earlier.

And she does not remember what was on television that afternoon, or what she ate at midday, or what I brought the week before that.

**I waited until five.**

That one is the whole of it.

I ordinarily leave at twenty past five.

Which is to say: that afternoon she was waiting.

Not *remembered it was Wednesday* waiting. **Sitting there, waiting.**

And she waited until five, and then stopped.

How did she know to wait until five? Because I ordinarily leave at twenty past. A person who is waiting waits past the hour the other usually goes. And she stopped at five.

**Five is a figure she worked out: if she were coming, five would already be too late.**

She performed a calculation. She used a sense of time that is no longer reliable, performed a calculation, and accepted the result.

---

I said one thing.

I said: *Mum, I'll come next Wednesday.*

The sentence means nothing. Of course she knows I come next Wednesday; I come every Wednesday. And it was the only sentence I could produce in that second.

She said: *Don't work late all the time.*

I said: mm.

She said: *You're on your own.*

---

*You're on your own.*

After those four words she stopped.

She did not go on. She did not say *you're on your own out there, look after yourself*, did not say *you're on your own and I worry*, did not say anything that could follow.

Just *you're on your own*, and then she stopped.

I was divorced five years ago.

Since the divorce she has not mentioned it once. She has not asked why, has not said one word about young people these days, has not urged me to find somebody. Not once.

I had always assumed she did not mind, or minded and did not know how to say it.

And in that moment I knew: she had always known, and had always had it there, and had chosen not to raise it for five years.

**And in that minute she took it out, said four words, and put it back.**

---

She said two more things in that minute.

The second was:

*Don't buy so much.*

She says that every week, at the door, while I am putting my shoes on.

And this time she said it in the middle, not at the door, and in a different voice — ordinarily that sentence is procedure, part of our three-hundred-times exchange. This time it was real.

She was genuinely saying: do not buy so much.

I said: it isn't much.

She said: *Have you got enough money.*

---

*Have you got enough money.*

I am thirty-nine. I have a stable job, a mortgage I can service, savings. She is seventy-three, and her pension is less than a third of what I earn.

And she asked whether I had enough money.

I said: I have.

She said: *Then that's all right.*

---

Then the third, and the last.

She glanced out of the window and said:

*The weather's bad today. Go home early.*

---

The weather was excellent.

I remember it precisely, because I sat on the concrete step downstairs for twenty minutes afterwards in the sun.

There was no cloud that afternoon. The four-o'clock light came in at an angle and fell exactly on the third shelf, on the nineteen volumes of lesson plans.

The weather was fine.

And she said the weather was bad, go home early.

---

I want to record what I did, because it is one of the few things in my life I do not regret.

I did not say *the weather's lovely, actually.*

I said: all right.

Then I stood up and started clearing.

---

I should explain why, because it looks like compliance and is not.

In that minute, every sentence she said was accurate:

I did not come last Wednesday — accurate.
She knew I had said I was working — accurate.
She waited until five — accurate (I believe her).
I am on my own — accurate.
I buy too much — accurate.
She asked whether I had enough money — that is the question a mother asks, when she is still being a mother.

Five accurate.

The sixth: the weather is bad.

Not accurate.

**And I was not going to use the sixth to negate the first five.**

If I had said *the weather's lovely*, what I would have been doing is taking her one error and correcting her with it.

And in that minute, for the first time in three years, she was whole.

I do not correct a person who is whole for the first time in three years.

---

While I was clearing she sat and watched me.

She said: *Take your time.*

I said: mm.

I washed the dishes, bagged the rubbish, filled the pill organiser. I did all of it more slowly than usual, because I did not want that minute to end.

I knew it would end.

I did not know how it would end, or how much of it was left.

I only knew I must not let it stop in my hands — that I must not ask her anything, must not try to confirm anything, must not say *Mum, you're very clear today.*

Because that would make her notice that something was different, and the instant she noticed, the minute would be over.

**I had to use that minute up as though nothing were happening.**

It is the hardest thing I have ever done.

I washed dishes for about three minutes.

With my back to her.

In those three minutes I thought about nothing.

I was only washing dishes, and knowing that the person behind me was, at that moment, there.
