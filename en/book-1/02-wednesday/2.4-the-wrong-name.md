# 2.4 · The Wrong Name

The first time was in the kitchen.

She was washing vegetables and I was standing beside her. Without turning round she said:

*Little sister, pass me that basin.*

I passed her the basin.

And then I stood with my hand still up, not moving.

About three seconds.

---

I want to be exact about those three seconds, because the order in which I thought things was strange, and the order says something.

**The first second**, what I thought was not *she has got it wrong.*

What I thought was: **is there someone else in the room.**

A purely animal reaction. Somebody has said a name, the name is not mine, therefore there must be another person here. My body very nearly turned to look.

**The second second**, I knew there was nobody else.

**The third second**, I knew whose name it was.

---

*Little sister* is not a form of address.

I should establish that, because in a great many families those words are just what you call the youngest daughter, and anybody may use them.

Not in ours.

In ours, *little sister* is a person's name — not the name on the household register; the name actually used. The whole family called her that for twenty-three years, from her birth to her death.

The name on the register was Shen Li.

*Li* as in jasmine.

My mother is called Shen Mo.

I have to stop here, because there is something I only worked out while writing this section.

My grandfather named my mother *Mo*, on the grounds that the jasmine in the courtyard flowered well — and my aunt says there was no jasmine, that he took the character from a book and got one stroke wrong, and that it was originally a different character.

Six years later the third daughter was born.

He named her *Li*.

To go with *Mo*.

Which is to say:

**A character with one stroke copied wrong decided what another person would be called for her whole life.**

And that person later died, and forty years afterwards that name was used by an old woman who no longer remembers much, to call her own daughter.

---

Little sister died in the winter of 1979.

Twenty-three.

The version I know is my aunt's, and my aunt was not there, so it is second-hand. My mother has never told it.

Not once.

I want to stress this. My mother told her own life a hundred times — the university exam, the fall on fieldwork, the posting to that school — rich in detail, stable in repetition.

And she has two blanks.

One is the four years on the farm.
One is little sister.

Of those two she says not one word. Not evasion — evasion leaves traces; evasion changes the subject, says *what is there to tell.* Those two are not evasion. Those two are **absence.** In the version of her life that she tells, 1969 to 1973 has no content, and nothing at all happened in the winter of 1979.

My aunt's version runs like this.

That winter was cold. The old houses they lived in were heated by a coal stove. That night little sister was home alone. The flue was blocked, or the wind was wrong and it drew backwards — my aunt cannot say, having heard it herself.

By the time she was found the next morning it was too late.

Carbon monoxide. It was very common then. My aunt says that in those years there might be two or three on their street in a winter.

---

My aunt told me one more thing, and told it in an ordinary voice, without a pause, without weight. She told it as a detail.

She said: *your mother was supposed to be home that day.*

She said your mother had gone to the school at short notice that afternoon, something about a student needing extra help.

She said your mother had been posted by then, had been teaching six months or so, was terribly conscientious, was often at the school in the evenings.

Then she drank some water and moved on to something else.

I did not ask.

I have still never asked.

I do not ask because I do not know what I would do with the answer.

If I asked *had my mother promised to be home that day*, and then what?

My mother does not remember. My aunt's version is second-hand. And the person who could have settled it was gone in the winter of 1979.

**Nobody alive can verify this.**

What I have is a blank, and a detail my aunt mentioned in passing, and the fact of forty-five years of silence on two subjects.

I can build a story out of those three things. It would be neat, and persuasive, and very like the truth.

**And I would have built it.**

I do not intend to build it.

---

Back to the three seconds.

At the end of the third I did something.

I said: *Mum, I'm He.*

I said it very quietly. I remember saying it quietly, because at that point I did not think I was correcting her. I thought I was reminding her.

She turned round.

She looked at me.

I want to be exact about the look, because it was not the one I expected.

The one I expected was blankness — *she does not know me* — that vacant, unfocused look they do in films.

It was not.

Her eyes were entirely clear, entirely focused, the way my mother has always looked at me. She could see me. She knew me.

And there was something else in her face.

**Puzzlement.**

Not the puzzlement of *who are you.* The puzzlement of *why are you telling me that.*

In her world at that second, she had asked the person standing beside her to hand over a basin, and the person had, and then the person had suddenly announced her own name.

She said: *What?*

I said: *Nothing.*

She said *oh*, and turned back to the vegetables.

---

The second time was three weeks later.

In the front room. She was sitting; I was clearing the low table. She said:

*Little sister, fetch me my glasses.*

I fetched them.

I said nothing that time.

I handed them over, she said *mm*, took them and put them on.

Then I went out to the balcony and stood there for about two minutes.

---

What I thought about on the balcony was something technical, and I know now that I always go to something technical at moments like that. It is my escape route.

What I thought was: **why little sister.**

Forgetting is not random.

I knew that by then — not from Ye Qi; that was later. From my own watching. My mother's forgetting has a pattern.

She forgets the recent and keeps the early. That is the commonest pattern of this disease; everyone knows it.

She forgets proper nouns faster than verbs. She forgot how to use the television remote and has not forgotten how to chop vegetables. She forgets that I came last Wednesday and has not forgotten how to get from this room to the kitchen.

**This is not erasure. This is clearance in an order.**

Layer by layer, outside inward.

And outside is recent, and inside is old.

So:

If she has begun calling me little sister, then in her memory the layer where the name *little sister* sits is **deeper than the layer where the name *Shen He* sits.**

And she still has that layer.

That layer is still there.

---

I need to write this sentence out whole, because the whole of the section is in it:

**My mother did not speak of her sister for a lifetime. And when the outer layers came off, what was underneath was her sister.**

Not me.

Not the twenty-five years of teaching, not that school, not the contour lines, not the university she sat for twice, not my father, not me.

A person who died in the winter of 1979, aged twenty-three, whom the family called little sister.

That person was at the bottom the whole time.

Under forty-five years of silence. Under the university story told two hundred times. Under the third desk from the window. Under nineteen volumes of lesson plans. Under me.

And what is on top is being taken off, layer by layer.

And she is at the bottom.

---

I thought of something else on the balcony, and that one is harder.

When my mother calls me little sister, **she does not feel anything is wrong.**

She is not making a mistake. In her world at that moment, the woman standing beside her — the one who hands over basins and fetches glasses, in her twenties or thirties — is little sister.

That world is self-consistent.

In that world, little sister did not die.

I thought that for the first time in those two minutes, and I did not know what to do with it, and I still do not.

**In her shrinking world, a person has come back.**

And the price is that I am not there.

---

From the second time on, I stopped correcting her.

I should be clear about the reason, because it is not simply *for her sake.*

After the first correction she was puzzled for about two seconds. Two seconds of puzzlement is not much of a price.

I stopped mainly for a more practical reason: **correcting does not work.**

I say *I'm He*, she says *what*, and turns back to the vegetables. She does not thereby remember. The next time she will still say little sister. And every correction is one more puzzlement.

Cumulatively, a great many puzzlements, buying not one remembering.

It is the same piece of accounting as *you've asked already.* I ran those numbers once on a staircase. I do not intend to run them again.

And I have to admit the second reason, which is not flattering.

**Answering to *little sister* is easier than answering to *He*.**

When she calls me little sister, the person being called is not me.

I can hand over the basin, fetch the glasses, say yes, do all of what has to be done — and it is not Shen He doing it. It is somebody.

For a little while, I can be absent.

I tasted that for the first time in those two minutes on the balcony.

And it made me feel worse, for far longer, than being called by the wrong name did.

---

She calls me little sister more often now than she calls me He.

Roughly three to one.

Sometimes she uses both in the same afternoon. Those are the strangest — in the morning's conversation I am He and in the afternoon I am little sister, and she feels nothing whatever; the two do not conflict in her.

I answer to both.

When I answer to *He*, I am myself.

When I answer to *little sister*, I am a twenty-three-year-old who died beside a coal stove on a very cold night in 1979.

I never met her. I have no photograph — my aunt says there is one in a box somewhere at home and it cannot be found. I do not know what she looked like, or her voice, or what she liked.

I know her name was chosen from a miscopied character.

I know she died on a cold night, alone in the house.

I know my mother went to the school that afternoon.

I know my mother did not say one word about her for forty-five years.

And now, every Wednesday afternoon, in that flat, I am her.
