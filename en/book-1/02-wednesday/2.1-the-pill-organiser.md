# 2.1 · The Pill Organiser

The organiser has seven compartments, Monday through Sunday.

I fill it. Every Wednesday afternoon — not because Wednesday is convenient, but because Wednesday is when I go, and once one thing has been tied to another thing it can never be untied again.

To fill it you have to flip all seven lids open at once, like opening a row of very small coffins. It is not a good image. I know it is not a good image. But it is what I think of every time I flip them, and a person has no right of censorship over her own thoughts.

Four medications. Two white tablets, one pale yellow, and a capsule, blue and white, very beautifully made — I have always thought that making the drug for an incurable disease that beautiful is an unexamined ethical problem inside somebody's profession.

Seven compartments times four drugs. Twenty-eight motions. I do it fast. Fast enough that once, afterwards, I could not be certain I had done it, and so I flipped all seven lids open again and counted.

The count was right. I closed the lids. And then I stood at the top of the stairs for a while, because I had noticed that the thing I had just done and the thing my mother now does every day are the same shape.

The only difference is that when I finished counting I was certain, and when she finishes counting she is not.

That is the whole difference. That difference is my entire life.

---

From where I live to where my mother lives is forty minutes by bus, or twenty-six by underground plus an eleven-minute walk. I take the bus.

My reason is not respectable: there is nothing to look at on the underground, and for forty minutes I need one thing to do, and looking out of a window is the only thing that does not require me to have a mind. For three weeks my mind had been something requiring an appointment in advance.

At the seventh stop the bus passes a housing block that is being taken down. Fourteen months now. Three buildings left. Every Wednesday there is a little less of it.

Once I saw a building that had been cut open along its side, all its rooms exposed — compartment beside compartment, like an organiser with the lid off — and the wallpaper in every compartment was a different colour. One was pale green, and there was still a mirror on its wall, facing the empty space, reflecting the sky.

I sat through that entire stop without moving, until somebody pushed past me and knocked my knee.

I know now why I sat still. The mirror was still reflecting, and the room was no longer there. And it was reflecting perfectly. Not the smallest error in it.

I told nobody about it that evening. What I told people was that the traffic had been bad.

---

My mother lives in old university housing. Six storeys, no lift. She is on the fourth.

We have fought about the fourth floor. I want her to move. She will not. Her reason is *too much stuff*, and that reason was already false back when she was entirely lucid — the real reason is that five households in that building are her old colleagues, and in that building she is addressed as *Teacher Shen*, and anywhere else in the world she is just an old woman named Shen.

I always assumed I understood that. I found out later that I did not understand it well enough.

Going up, I count the stairs. Twelve to a flight, eight flights to the fourth floor, ninety-six. I have counted them for six years and I count them every time, and my breath begins to run short on exactly the last step, which means my body and that building are well matched.

The door on the right on the fourth floor is steel, with a lot of the green paint gone off it in irregular shapes. I have a key. I never use it. I ring the bell.

I ring the bell because she likes opening the door.

---

There are two ways she opens it.

The first: the door comes open fast, because she was already standing behind it — which means she remembered that today is Wednesday, which means she began waiting in the morning, which means she may have been waiting four hours, which means that if I were twenty minutes late she would get up and walk to the door and go back and sit down, three times.

The second: the door comes open slowly. Through it, first the sound of slippers dragging, then the chain, then her face in the gap — not knowing me, and then knowing me. Sometimes that *and then* is half a second. Sometimes it is three. I count every one of those three seconds silently.

That day it was the first way.

She said: *You've come.*

She said it in the voice of a person who knew you would. In that second I badly wanted to cry, and did not, because I had not slept in three weeks, and the feelings of a person who has not slept all sit nearer the surface than usual, and I do not trust them.

I said: *I've come.*

And as I said it I lifted the bag a little so she could see what I had bought. This is a procedure of ours: I lift the bag, she looks, she says *why do you buy so much*, I say *it isn't much*. Then she takes it, and then, in the kitchen, without exception, she will unpack everything and put it all back in her own order.

That day she said: *Why do you buy so much.*

I said: *It isn't much.*

And then I stood in the doorway for half a second, because it had just occurred to me that the two sentences we had said we have said perhaps three hundred times, identically, not one word different.

That exchange requires no memory. That exchange lives somewhere else.

At the time I found this reassuring.

---

I do not know how to write the smell of that flat.

It is not unpleasant. It is old. It is a smell made of many things added together, and some of them I can name: the herbal medicine she soaks her feet in, camphor from the wardrobe, oil from the kitchen — the kind that has been accumulating on top of the extractor hood for twenty years and can no longer be removed — and paper, out of the lesson plans she has never thrown away.

And one thing I cannot name, and that one is the main one. I suspect it is the smell of a person: live somewhere thirty-one years and the place acquires your smell, and you can only smell it if you have been away a long time and come back. I left that flat at nineteen. For twenty years since, I have smelled that thing every time I walk in.

My mother cannot smell it. She is inside it.

---

I gave her the bag and she went into the kitchen. I went to look at the organiser.

It sits on the television cabinet, on the right, holding down a sheet of paper. The paper is a calendar she has drawn herself, a grid, with what she has to do that day written in each square, in very small very neat characters — she wrote on blackboards her whole life; the hand is trained.

That day was Wednesday.

The Wednesday square was empty.

Let me be precise: not the Wednesday square on the calendar. The Wednesday compartment of the **organiser**. Four medications, none left. The lid closed.

And I had arrived at half past two in the afternoon.

---

Of the four, two are morning, one is midday, and one is evening.

Which means that at half past two there should still have been one capsule in the Wednesday compartment — the blue-and-white one, the beautifully made one.

It was not there.

I stood in front of the television cabinet for about fifteen seconds. I could hear her in the kitchen moving things, the sound of a plastic bag, and then water.

I had three possibilities.

One. She took the evening dose early. Not good; not dangerous; a few hours early does no harm with that one.

Two. She took it, forgot she had taken it, wanted to take it again, found none left, and did not tell me.

Three. She did not take it. She threw it out, or put it somewhere else, or dropped it on the floor and swept it up herself.

I could not eliminate any of the three.

I have done a great deal of elimination. Three weeks earlier I had completed an exceptionally thorough elimination, using seventy-one genomes, four ancient samples, two machines of different architectures, and two and a half days of pencil. That time I eliminated every possibility but one.

This time — one plastic box with seven compartments, four drugs, one afternoon — I could eliminate nothing.

Because the only record is inside her head, and that record is being cleared: systematically, precisely, and by priority.

---

I went into the kitchen.

She was putting my shopping back in her own order. Two packets of noodles stood side by side in the corner. The eggs came out of their plastic tray and went one at a time into a different bowl — she has done this her whole life, I have said her whole life that it is unnecessary, and she has done it anyway her whole life.

There was a pot on the hob. Soup. Cold. A thin skin on the surface where the fat had set.

I said: *Mum, did you take the midday one?*

She said: *I took it.*

Fast, natural, without a flicker. That is the worst kind of answer, because a person who really took it and a person who really forgot say those three words in exactly the same way.

I said: *And the evening one? There's nothing left in the box.*

Her hands stopped for half a second.

Then she said: *Let me look.*

She went out. I heard her going through things by the television cabinet. I stood in front of the hob and looked at the cold soup.

I touched the rim of the pot with one finger. It was cold. It had been cold a long time.

Which means she had not eaten at midday.

Three of the four medications are to be taken after food.

---

She came back holding the organiser with the lid flipped open.

She said: *There's none left. I took it.*

There was something in her voice as she said it, and I have never found the word. It is not pride, and it is not grievance, and it is not the hardness of somebody caught lying. It is closest to the voice of **handing in homework** — the voice of a person who knows she was supposed to finish, and believes she finished, and is passing you that belief.

I looked at her face.

Seventy-three. A scar above the left eyebrow. As a child I did not know where it came from; later I learned she got it at twenty-six on fieldwork, sliding down a slope and catching a stone. She told me that story with enormous satisfaction, because, she said, the map in her hand did not get wet.

I said: *All right.*

I took the organiser from her.

---

Here is what I did with the rest of that afternoon.

Poured out the cold soup and made another pot. She said not to pour it out, it was still all right. I said it was not. She said *waste*. I said *waste*. Then she stopped.

Wiped the television cabinet and the bookshelf. The third shelf holds her lesson plans, nineteen volumes, 1976 to 2001. I stop at that shelf every time I wipe it. The topmost volume lies open, always at the same page — and on that page is a contour map, drawn by hand, in blue-black ink, of a mountain I do not recognise. The lines run ring inside ring, outer to inner, the spacing as even as print.

She drew the best contour lines in the school. A teacher told me that when I was thirteen, and that teacher still lives in the building.

I closed that volume once. The following Wednesday it was open again, at the same page.

I have never closed it since.

Cut her nails — I will write that later. I cannot write it now.

Watched an hour of television with her. A programme neither of us cared about, concerning fruit from somewhere. Three times in the middle of it she asked me whether I had eaten.

Three times I said I had. I worked hard to make the three tones identical.

---

At twenty past five I had to go.

I put my shoes on at the door and she stood next to me. She said one thing.

She said: *Don't buy so much next Wednesday.*

I said: *All right.*

She said: *You have to eat too.*

I said: *All right.*

I opened the door, stepped out, turned round. She was standing inside it with a hand on the frame. The landing light is on a sensor; it came on as I moved, and it made her face a bad white.

She said: *Go on, then.*

I went down ninety-six stairs.

At the bottom I took the organiser out of my pocket and looked at it — seven compartments, Wednesday's empty, the other six full. I had taken it with me. It needed refilling.

I sat down on the concrete step at the door of the building.

There was sun that day. I sat there about twenty minutes and thought about nothing at all — and I mean that literally; I have tried to reconstruct it, and for those twenty minutes there was nothing in my head but the patch of warmth on my arm.

Then I got up and went to the bus.

On the bus, after the block that is being taken down, I did one thing.

I opened the encrypted note on my phone and looked at the sixteen hexadecimal characters.

They were still there. Not one of them changed.
