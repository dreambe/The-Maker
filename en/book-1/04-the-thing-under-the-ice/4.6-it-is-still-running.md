# 4.6 · It Is Still Running

On the ninth evening I thought of it.

---

We did not work that day. The forecast said there might be a window the next day; we finished checking the equipment and had nothing to do.

There was a communal dinner at the station that evening — the science-station kind: a dozen people, three countries, four languages, one pot.

Afterwards I went back to the small room and sat.

I was thinking about the word *maintain.*

---

Page 58 of Ilan's report says:

> The power of this source shows no detectable variation across the observation period.

I wrote at 4.3 why that mattered: every known natural process has power that varies with time, and this one does not.

**And what I thought of on the ninth evening goes further.**

---

A thing producing heat and a thing **maintaining** a temperature are two things.

**Producing is passive.**

A radioactive mineral produces heat. It does not know it is doing so. Its power is set by its composition and its decay constants; that is physics, and nothing needs to attend to it.

**Maintaining is active.**

To maintain a value you need three things:

**One: measurement.** You must know the present value.
**Two: comparison.** You must have a target, and know the difference between the present value and it.
**Three: adjustment.** You must be able to change your output according to that difference.

**Those three together are a control loop.**

The most elementary object in cybernetics. A thermostat is one. Human thermoregulation is one. An autopilot is one.

**No natural process is a control loop — except life.**

---

I sat up in that room.

Why I sat up is the turn of this whole chapter.

Until then our conclusion was: **that is not natural** (4.3).

That conclusion is already bad enough. It means a thing was made and put there three hundred thousand years ago.

**And *not natural* and *maintaining* are different orders of magnitude.**

A manufactured stone is also not natural. A lump of metal somebody put there three hundred thousand years ago is also not natural.

**And they maintain nothing.**

They merely sit, cooling slowly, compressed slowly, slowly ceasing to be what they were.

**And this thing is maintaining a temperature.**

---

Now the inference in full, step by step, each step having to hold.

**Step one: 0.4 K is a constant value.**

Four measurements, seven years, consistent within error (4.3). And every natural process drifts measurably in seven years.

**Step two: a constant value is not the output of a natural process.**

A natural process outputs a **trajectory** — a curve varying with time. A constant is a special case of such a curve, and that case very nearly never occurs in nature, requiring drive and dissipation to be exactly equal while being fixed by different mechanisms with no reason to be equal.

**Step three: a maintained constant requires a target value.**

This step is the crux.

If that 0.4 K were a coincidental balance — drive happening to equal dissipation — then when external conditions changed, the balance would move.

**And external conditions changed.**

That is what I thought of in that second.

The ice moves.

The Antarctic sheet flows, at a few metres a year in that region. And the ice thickness changes — accumulating above, and (extremely slowly) melting or freezing below.

**Across seven years, the thickness, density, and thermal gradient of the ice above that point all changed.**

Ilan's data has all of it — he measured it, that being a glaciologist's own subject.

**The overlying thickness changed by about eleven metres in seven years.**

Which means the pressure around that point changed, its heat-loss conditions changed, its thermal gradient changed.

**And the 0.4 K did not.**

---

I stood up.

I went and knocked on Ilan's door.

It was a little after eleven, local. He was awake — at that latitude in that season, sleep is something you have to manage deliberately, and nobody sleeps much.

I said: *Dr Vosk, I have a question.*

He said: *Go on.*

I said:

*The overlying ice changed by eleven metres.*

He said: *Yes.*

I said:

*So the heat-loss conditions at that point changed.*

He said: *Yes.*

I said:

*If its power is constant, then after the heat-loss conditions changed, the temperature difference should have changed.*

He stood in the doorway without moving.

I said:

*And the temperature difference did not change.*

I said:

*So its power is not constant.*

I said:

***Its power is varying, and its temperature difference is not.***

---

He stood in the doorway about five seconds.

Then he turned, went in, and came back with his laptop.

He sat down on the floor in the doorway — there is one chair in that room, and neither of us thought of going inside.

He opened the data.

We sat on the floor of that corridor for four hours.

---

What we computed.

We have four measurements of the temperature difference across seven years: 0.4 K, consistent within error.

We have seven years of continuous monitoring of the overlying ice: thickness, density, thermal profile.

**From those two, the source's power at each measurement can be back-computed.**

Because: temperature difference = power × thermal resistance.

And the thermal resistance is fixed by the properties of the overlying ice, which we have.

So: power = temperature difference ÷ thermal resistance.

**We computed four.**

---

The results:

```
measurement 1 (year 0)    power 11.2 W
measurement 2 (year 2)    power 11.6 W
measurement 3 (year 5)    power 10.9 W
measurement 4 (year 7)    power 11.4 W
```

Four numbers.

They differ.

**Range: about six per cent.**

---

And the range of the temperature difference: **under five parts in a thousand.**

---

We sat on the corridor floor looking at those eight numbers.

The power varies, by six per cent.
The temperature difference does not, by under five parts in a thousand.

**It is compensating.**

The ice above thickens, heat loss slows, and it produces a little less.
The ice above thins, heat loss quickens, and it produces a little more.

**It is holding that difference at 0.4 K.**

---

What Ilan said then.

He looked at those eight numbers for a long time.

Then he said:

*I have had this data for seven years.*

He said:

*I computed the power. Page 33.*

He said:

*I computed the **mean** power.*

He said:

*I wanted one number, so I took the average.*

He said:

*I never computed the four separately.*

---

I said: *Why not?*

He said:

*Because my question was how hot it is.*

He said:

*And if I computed the four separately I would have four numbers, and I would not know what to do with four numbers.*

He said:

*Four numbers differing by six per cent, inside my error bars — at the time I would have said that is measurement error.*

---

I said: *It is not measurement error.*

He said: *I know.*

He said:

*Because the error on the temperature difference is five parts in a thousand, and the spread in the power is six per cent.*

He said:

*If it were measurement error the two would match.*

He said:

*Seven years, and I never made that comparison.*

---

He closed the laptop.

He sat on the corridor floor with his back against the wall.

He said:

*Dr Shen.*

I said: *Yes.*

He said:

*We are going down.*

---

I said: *Yes.*

He said:

*I used to think we were going to look at a thing.*

He said:

*A thing under the ice, three hundred thousand years old, of unknown kind.*

He said:

*A stone. A lump of metal. A... something.*

He paused.

He said:

*And a thing that compensates for changing heat-loss conditions is not that.*

---

I said: *No.*

He said:

*A thing that compensates is **doing something**.*

He said:

*It is maintaining a state.*

He said:

*And maintaining a state requires it to know what that state currently is.*

He said:

*Which is to say —*

He stopped for a long time.

The corridor lights are the cold white kind at a slightly unstable frequency. There is a faint flicker in them you do not normally notice, and in a long silence you do.

He said:

*It is measuring.*

---

To end this section, what came after that sentence.

We sat on that corridor floor for about another twenty minutes without either of us speaking.

Then he stood up.

He said: *I'm going to sleep for two hours.*

I said: *All right.*

He took two steps, stopped, and turned round.

He said one thing and then went.

He said:

*It is measuring.*

*And it has been measuring for three hundred thousand years.*

*And the thing it measures — that temperature difference — is the difference between itself and the outside.*

He said:

*It is measuring itself.*
