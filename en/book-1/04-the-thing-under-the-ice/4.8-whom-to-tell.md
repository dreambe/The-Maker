# 4.8 · Whom to Tell

We did not go down.

---

I put that first, because a record read in order arrives here with an expectation: they have the keys, the weather will clear, they will go down.

**That time we did not.**

That drill, those four days, those two people — none of it was used.

---

Here is why.

The day after the chief approved it, the weather cleared.

We prepared to go.

And four hours before departure the station received a notice.

Not addressed to us. A routine circular to all Antarctic stations, an update to an international agreement.

One clause concerned **new approval requirements for subglacial sampling and drilling.**

Its content, simply: any subglacial drilling below one thousand metres requires an environmental impact assessment submitted four months in advance and approved by a three-party committee.

The effective date of the notice was **that day.**

---

The nature of this must be stated, being extremely easy to read as conspiracy.

**It is not.**

That update had been under negotiation for six years. Its origin was a contamination incident in a subglacial lake drilling some years earlier — a team drilled through into a lake sealed for hundreds of thousands of years, and their drilling fluid contaminated it.

That caused considerable controversy at the time. And the outcome of the controversy was a new and far stricter approval process.

**That process is correct.**

What it protects is exactly that kind of thing: an environment sealed for hundreds of thousands of years, uncontaminated, unique.

And what we wanted to drill was precisely such a place.

---

The chief put the notice on the desk.

He looked at it for a long time.

Then he said:

*Four months.*

I said: *Four months from now is winter.*

He said:

*Four months from now is winter. There is no fieldwork in winter.*

He said:

*So it is next summer.*

He said:

*Fourteen months.*

---

Ilan's reaction, the only time since I met him that I have seen him come apart.

He did not shout. He did not throw anything. He said not one word of complaint.

**He sat down.**

He sat down in the chair in the chief's office, and it was not his chair — it was mine; I was standing.

Sitting, he put both hands on his knees and looked at the floor.

About ten seconds.

Then he said:

*All right.*

And stood up.

He said: *I'll put the drill kit back.*

And went out.

---

I have to admit something.

In that moment the first thing I thought of was not Ilan.

It was my mother.

I was doing arithmetic: fourteen months.

I was computing her score.

Three years, twenty-four to twenty-one to eighteen. About three points every eighteen months.

Fourteen months: about two and a half.

**Around sixteen.**

I stood in the chief's office holding a circular about an international agreement, computing whether my mother would still be able to draw that circle this time next year.

---

That evening I made a decision.

I said to Ilan: if we have to wait fourteen months, then we do not wait.

He said: *Meaning?*

I said:

*We spend the fourteen months.*

I said:

*We find somebody who will listen properly.*

---

Now the eleven weeks.

I write this passage because it is the dullest and least dramatic in this record, and the most important to the whole thing.

**What we needed was: a person with power who will listen properly.**

Either condition alone is not hard. Both at once is extremely hard.

**People with power generally have no time to listen properly.**

The higher a person sits, the more crosses their desk, and the less time they allocate to any one thing. And what we had to say needs at least two hours, and after it the listener must go and verify things themselves.

**People who will listen properly generally have no power.**

Someone willing to spend two hours on a strange thing outside their field is generally still doing the work themselves. And people still doing the work cannot approve a transnational drilling project requiring a three-party committee.

---

We made a list.

Thirty-seven names.

Here is how we filtered, because the method is Ilan's and it is excellent.

He said: *Do not sort by rank.*

I said: *By what?*

He said:

*By whether, in the last five years, they have published anything about a thing they could not explain.*

---

The criterion.

If a researcher has, in the last five years, published a paper whose content is *we observed X, we eliminated several explanations, we offer no explanation* —

**that person will listen properly.**

Because they have written one. They know the position. They know what it is like to hold a thing you cannot explain.

And the overwhelming majority of researchers never publish such a thing. Because it is hard to publish (3.2: reviewers want a mechanism), because it does not look good, because it does not help a career.

**A person who has published one is a person who paid a price to report a thing they did not understand.**

---

We filtered thirty-seven by that criterion.

**Four remained.**

Four people who had each, in the last five years, published at least one *we offer no explanation.*

---

We contacted all four.

**The first**: no reply. We wrote again three weeks later; still none. We later learned he was ill, and died that winter. We never spoke to him.

**The second**: replied. Very courteously. He was interested; he was moving institutions next year and could not take on anything new.

That is a real reason. We checked; he did move.

**The third**: replied, and set up a call. Listened for forty minutes.

Then he said something I record verbatim, being the most honest sentence I heard in those eleven weeks:

He said:

*I believe you.*

*And I cannot help you.*

He said:

*The largest thing I can approve from where I sit is the purchase of a mass spectrometer.*

He said:

*What you want needs a signature from someone three levels above me. And I do not know that person, and if I went to him he would ask why an isotope geochemist is bringing him Antarctica.*

He said:

*And I have no good answer.*

Then he said:

*Let me give you a piece of advice.*

*Do not look for a scientist.*

---

***Do not look for a scientist.***

I said: *Then who?*

He said:

*Somebody who controls money.*

He said:

*A person who controls money, if he believes this, can simply give you some, without having to argue its scientific value.*

He said:

*Scientific value requires peer review. And you will not pass peer review — you have no mechanism.*

He said:

*And money does not require peer review. Money requires one person to believe it.*

---

**The fourth** was the last we contacted, and the only one that worked.

And he is not the person on our list.

He is that person's student.

We wrote to the fourth name. The reply came from someone we did not know, whose title read *research assistant.*

The reply began:

> Dr Shen, Dr Vosk,
>
> My supervisor received your letter. He asked me to read it first; he is abroad this month.
>
> I have read it three times.
>
> I have eleven questions.
>
> If you are willing, I would like to ask all eleven before deciding whether to take up my supervisor's time.

---

Eleven questions.

I read that sentence twice.

***Before deciding whether to take up my supervisor's time.***

A research assistant screening his supervisor's post, and the first thing he does is produce eleven questions.

We answered.

Six days to answer eleven questions.

And on the seventh day he sent a twelfth.

And the twelfth is one nobody had asked us before.

---

The question is:

> You say it compensates for changes in heat-loss conditions.
>
> I want to know: **how fast** does it compensate.

---

Why that is the best question of those eleven weeks.

All our analysis had been about *it compensates.*

And *how fast does it compensate* is a question we had never asked.

That quantity is called **response time.**

A control loop's response time is fixed jointly by the speed of its sensing, its computing, and its actuation.

**A thing with a response time of one day and a thing with a response time of a thousand years are entirely different things.**

And Ilan's four measurements are years apart.

**Our data cannot fix a response time.**

We can give only an upper bound: shorter than two years (having completed a compensation within two).

The lower bound we do not know.

---

We wrote back saying so.

He replied. Two sentences.

**The first:**

*Then you need continuous monitoring.*

**The second:**

*My supervisor is back the day after tomorrow. I will raise it with him. I suggest you prepare a three-minute statement, and not one second more.*
