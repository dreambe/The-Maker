# 4.9 · The Point on the Thermal Map

Three minutes.

---

We spent four days on it.

A three-minute statement, four days.

Why, because what we did in those four days is a method I have used ever since.

**The difficulty of a three-minute statement is not its shortness. It is the selection.**

What we had:

The sequence, the checksum, two elimination reports, forty-one ancient and modern samples.
Lu Ting's fifty years, four figures, the spike.
Five regional density curves, the globally synchronous gap.
The joint estimate, seven hundred thousand years, sigma forty thousand.
The empty case in a museum.
Ilan's seven years, four measurements, three methods.
The thermal anomaly: 0.4 K, eleven watts, three hundred thousand years.
The heptagon.
The eight numbers: power varying six per cent while the difference does not.

**Nine things.**

You cannot give nine things in three minutes.

---

Day one, we tried nine. Nineteen minutes.

Day two, cut to six. Eleven minutes.

Day three, cut to three. Five and a half.

That evening Ilan said something.

He said:

*We are cutting in the wrong direction.*

I said: *Meaning?*

He said:

*We keep cutting **evidence**.*

He said:

*And a three-minute statement is not for giving evidence.*

He said:

*Three minutes is not enough for any single piece. Not one.*

I said: *Then what is it for?*

He said:

*For making him want the forty minutes.*

---

On day four we started again.

We threw out all nine.

**We kept one thing.**

---

One figure.

Not Lu Ting's spike. Not the five density curves. Not the heptagon.

**Ilan's thermal map, page 41.**

---

Why that one.

It is the only item in all our material that **requires no background whatever to read.**

A nearly uniform field of colour. Middle-right, at two thousand one hundred metres, a bright point.

**You need not know what a checksum is.**
**You need not know what a molecular clock is.**
**You need not know the crystallographic restriction theorem.**
**You need not know MNI, or OSL, or what a contour line is.**

You need only look at the figure.

And having looked, you will ask a question.

**And that question is what we want.**

---

Here is the three minutes as we set it.

**Second zero to twenty:** say nothing. Put the figure up. Leave it on screen for twenty seconds.

**Twenty to fifty:**

*This is a thermal profile beneath the ice at a location in Antarctica. Four kilometres across, down to two thousand eight hundred metres.*

*This field is ice. Its temperature rises slowly with depth, which is normal, the geothermal flux coming from below.*

*This point is 0.4 kelvin above its surroundings.*

**Fifty to one forty:**

*The point is at two thousand one hundred metres. Ice at that depth is three hundred thousand years old.*

*We have measured it four times by three methods over seven years. The four agree.*

*We have eliminated: instrument failure, geothermal anomaly, radioactive vein, groundwater, subglacial lake, volcanism, measurement error.*

*Seven, all eliminated. Sixty-seven pages of it in the attachment.*

**One forty to two thirty:**

*Its power is eleven watts.*

*Over the past seven years the ice above it has changed thickness by eleven metres.*

*So its heat-loss conditions changed.*

*And its temperature difference did not.*

(Two seconds.)

*Its power varied by six per cent. Its temperature difference by under five parts in a thousand.*

(Two seconds.)

*It is compensating.*

**Two thirty to three:**

*A thing that compensates for changing heat-loss conditions has to measure its own temperature.*

*We do not know what it is.*

*We are asking for continuous monitoring.*

*That is all.*

---

We rehearsed eleven times.

The eleventh: two minutes fifty-seven.

---

That afternoon, the call.

The research assistant was there. His supervisor was there. And a person we did not know, who we later learned was a deputy director of that institution.

I spoke. Ilan sat beside me and said nothing — we had agreed; three minutes has no room for a handover.

I finished.

Two minutes fifty-nine.

---

The other end was quiet for a moment.

The deputy director spoke first.

His first sentence was a question.

**And it is not one of the nineteen we had prepared for.**

He said:

*Is it still producing heat now?*

---

I said: *Yes.*

He said:

*When did you last measure it?*

I said: *Nineteen months ago.*

He said:

*So strictly, you do not know whether it is producing heat now.*

I said: *Strictly, no.*

He said:

*And it has been doing this for three hundred thousand years.*

I said: *Yes.*

He said:

*Then another nineteen months is nothing.*

---

Then he paused.

And then he said the most important sentence of that afternoon.

He said:

*No.*

He said:

*Those nineteen months are exactly the whole of it.*

---

I said: *Meaning?*

He said:

*A thing has produced heat for three hundred thousand years. In three hundred thousand years, nobody saw it.*

He said:

*And in the last seven years, one person saw it.*

He said:

*And in the last nineteen months, nobody has been watching.*

---

He said:

*That thing, across the last nineteen months, has been there, measuring its own temperature, compensating.*

*And nobody was watching.*

He said:

*That is not a data gap.*

He was quiet for a long moment.

Then he said:

*That is a thing doing what it has done for three hundred thousand years, and in those nineteen months it did it about a hundred and sixty million times.*

*And not one of those hundred and sixty million was recorded.*

---

To end this chapter, what happened after that call.

He approved it.

Not the drilling — drilling still needs the four months and the three-party committee.

**He approved continuous monitoring.**

An instrument, on the ice directly above that point, on a satellite link, uploading that point's temperature once an hour.

It went up four months later.

**It has been transmitting ever since.**

By the time I write this section it has transmitted for years.

---

And on the first day it transmitted, I did something.

I printed that thermal map and put it on my office wall.

Beside the other four.

**The fifth sheet.**

And beside it, the patch of bare wall I had left is still bare — what goes on that wall is the mechanism (3.9).

That wall was filled much later.

And what filled it was the point itself.

---

One thing about that point, to end the chapter.

On that figure the point is under two pixels across.

It is the smallest thing on the sheet.

And at the moment that figure was printed and put on my wall — in that moment — at that position, two thousand one hundred metres beneath the ice, there was a thing.

It was measuring the difference between itself and its surroundings.

It measured the difference at nought point four kelvin.

It adjusted its output very slightly, holding the difference at nought point four.

**It finished that one.**

And began the next.

It has done this for three hundred thousand years.

**And it does not know anyone is watching.**

It would be a long while before it knew.

And by then the person watching it would be standing in front of it, and it would do a thing it had not done in three hundred thousand years.

**It would move.**
