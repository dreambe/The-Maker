# 4.3 · The Thermal Anomaly

The figure on page 41.

---

It is a thermal profile.

Horizontal axis: distance, about four kilometres. Vertical axis: depth, from the surface to two thousand eight hundred metres beneath the ice.

The figure is a nearly uniform field of colour — ice temperature rising slowly with depth, which is normal: the geothermal flux comes from below and ice insulates, so deeper is warmer. That gradient is a gentle, predictable, textbook curve.

Middle-right, at about two thousand one hundred metres depth, there is a point.

That point is brighter than what surrounds it.

It is small. At that figure's scale its diameter is under two pixels.

---

My first reaction to that figure, which is not creditable:

**I assumed it was a dead pixel.**

Thermal detectors have dead pixels. A dead pixel reads constantly high or low, independently of what is being measured. Anyone who does imaging thinks of that first.

I looked at the legend.

Its last sentence:

> This point appears in four independent measurements using three different detection systems, and its position is consistent in three dimensions (±11 m).

---

*Three different detection systems.*

A dead pixel on one detector does not appear at the same spatial position on another.

**It is not a dead pixel.**

---

Now the number.

**Nought point four kelvin.**

That point is 0.4 K above the ice around it at the same depth.

The size of that quantity matters, being tiny, and its tininess is exactly the problem.

Nought point four kelvin is a difference you cannot feel with a hand. On everyday scales it is nothing.

And in ice at two thousand one hundred metres it is enormous.

**Because nothing should be there.**

Ice at that depth has its temperature fixed by two things: the weight of the ice above (pressure) and the geothermal flux below. Both are slow, large-scale, smooth.

A point tens of metres across, 0.4 K high, in a smooth field, means **there is a heat source there.**

And the power of that source can be computed.

Ilan computed it. Page 33.

Result: about **eleven watts.**

---

Eleven watts.

A small bulb.

I laughed out loud in the office when I got to that figure.

Not derision. The sound a person makes when a number is so small that it makes the whole thing worse.

**Eleven megawatts would be a power system: something imaginable, grand, science-fictional.**

**Eleven watts is a thing on standby.**

A thing maintaining itself. A thing doing nothing, only declining to go cold.

---

Now the three hundred thousand years.

The point is two thousand one hundred metres down.

The ice at that position has an age from the accumulation rate. Ilan used three methods: layer counting, isotopes, and an anchor on known ash horizons.

Three methods, one result: about three hundred thousand years, error about seven per cent.

Therefore:

**That point has been in that position for three hundred thousand years.**

And it is still producing heat.

---

Now the two possibilities, which Ilan listed in the email, and whose implications are worth opening out.

**One: it began producing heat after the ice formed.**

That requires a sustained energy source, two thousand one hundred metres under ice — where there is no sunlight, no convection, no usable chemical gradient (ice is extremely pure).

It also requires that the thing arrived there after the ice formed, or was generated there.

And two thousand one hundred metres of ice is a complete, continuous, undisturbed depositional sequence — Ilan checked; the layering of that core is intact.

**Nothing drilled down from above.**

**Two: it was there before the ice formed.**

Which means: three hundred thousand years ago that thing was on the surface (or near what was then the surface). And then snow fell on it, year after year, compacted into ice, and buried it two thousand one hundred metres deep.

And across those three hundred thousand years it has been producing heat.

**Eleven watts, three hundred thousand years.**

I worked out the total.

About **1.0 × 10^11 joules.**

Not a large number — roughly twenty-odd tonnes of TNT.

**And it was released across three hundred thousand years.**

A thing releasing the energy of twenty-odd tonnes of explosive, slowly, steadily, without interruption, over three hundred thousand years.

**With no fuel anywhere around it.**

---

Now the thing I found.

Having reached page 41, I read all sixty-seven pages in six hours.

And having finished, I did something Ilan had not mentioned in the email:

**I computed the drift that point ought to have.**

---

The step.

Suppose it is a natural heat source. What kinds are there?

Radioactive decay (a mineral rich in radioactive elements).
Residual heat (something not yet cooled).
Chemical reaction (some slow exothermic process).
Phase change (something slowly crystallising).

**All four share one feature: their power varies with time.**

Radioactive decay falls exponentially.
Residual heat falls by diffusion, faster than exponentially.
Chemical reactions exhaust their reactants.
Phase changes exhaust the material that can change phase.

**No natural process holds constant power.**

So I computed: if it were any of those, how much its power should have fallen across Ilan's seven years of measurement.

The answer depends on which; I did all four.

**The fall ranges from one part in a thousand to three per cent.**

And Ilan's measurement precision, which I found on his page 19: **better than five parts in a thousand.**

Which is to say:

**Of four natural explanations, three predict a drift visible within his precision.**

And he measured four times over seven years, consistent within error.

**No drift.**

---

My reaction on finishing.

I sat looking at those four numbers.

Then I did the calculation again.

Then a third time.

All three the same.

**That source's power shows no measurable change over seven years.**

And every known natural process shows measurable change over seven years.

**It is holding a constant value.**

---

I emailed Ilan a little after one in the morning.

Four hundred-odd words, with all three of my calculations attached.

The last line:

*I think this is stronger than the reasoning you give on page 11. Have you considered it?*

---

He replied quickly — it was daytime there.

His reply:

> I have.
>
> Attachment, page 58.

---

I turned to page 58.

There it is.

Finer than mine. He does seven natural explanations, not four. His error handling is more disciplined. And he adds a test I had not thought of: he compares the point's power against **season** — because if it were thermally coupled to the outside world in any way, its power should show an annual variation.

None.

The last line of that page:

> In sum, the power of this source shows no detectable variation across the observation period. No known natural process has this property.
>
> This report offers no explanation of this.

---

I wrote back:

*Why is it on page 58?*

---

He replied with a passage. I copy it in full, being the real content of this section.

> Because it is the strongest thing I have.
>
> If a report puts the strongest evidence first, the reader immediately understands what you want him to believe.
>
> And once he understands what you want him to believe, every sentence afterwards reads as selling.
>
> When I wrote those sixty-seven pages, the first fifty-seven are all me trying to **refute** it.
>
> Geothermal anomaly, pages 23 to 27.
> Radioactive vein, 28 to 33.
> Groundwater, 34 to 39.
> Subglacial lake, 40 to 44.
> Volcanism, 45 to 51.
> Measurement error, 52 to 57.
>
> All failed.
>
> Page 58 is what was left after I failed.
>
> I put it there because that is where it appeared.
>
> A person reading in order sees me try every explanation first, and only then sees what remains.
>
> By then he will think the sentence himself.
>
> I do not need to say it.

---

I looked at that passage.

Then I did something.

I opened the sixty-seven pages again and read from page one, in order.

**This time it took nine hours.**

The first time I had read the way we read: abstract, figures, conclusion, and a few places I cared about.

**That is the normal way to read a paper. Nobody reads start to finish.**

This time I read start to finish.

Finishing page 57 — the page on which every one of his eliminations has failed — I stopped.

**Before turning to page 58, I thought the sentence myself.**

Exactly as he said.

And the sentence is:

**That is not natural.**
