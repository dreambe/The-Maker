# 4.5 · Geometry

We spent nine days at the station waiting on weather.

In those nine days he showed me something that is not in the sixty-seven pages, and not in the three supplements.

**It is in a notebook.**

---

First, why it is not in the report.

He said: *Because I am not certain.*

I said: *Of which part.*

He said: *The data is certain.*

He said: *What I am not certain of is whether I should write it down.*

---

The thing is this.

That thermal anomaly is a point — under two pixels across on the thermal image (4.3).

Thermal imaging has insufficient resolution. Heat diffuses in ice; a small source at two thousand one hundred metres always looks like a blurred blob whose apparent size is set by diffusion and unrelated to its real size.

**So thermal imaging cannot tell you how large that thing is, or what shape.**

And Ilan did something else.

Two things:

**One: gravimetry.**

A body whose density differs from the surrounding ice produces a very small local gravity anomaly, related to its mass and its shape.

Ice is about nine hundred and twenty kilograms per cubic metre. Any solid substance is much heavier.

So: if that source is a solid, it has a gravity signature.

**Two: seismic reflection.**

Set off a small shock at the ice surface and receive the echoes. Ice is a fairly homogeneous medium and sound travels cleanly through it. Any interface differing in density or elasticity reflects part of it.

That is the standard method in petroleum prospecting and subglacial mapping.

---

He did it.

Across four field seasons he ran two gravity surveys and one seismic reflection profile.

That data is in the report — pages 59 to 63, as *physical constraints on the source.*

And what he gives in the report is only **part** of what it implies: the source is solid; its density exceeds ice; its scale is of order metres.

**He does not give the shape.**

---

His notebook is an ordinary squared-paper notebook, about two thirds used.

He turned to a page in the middle and pushed it across.

On that page is a hand-drawn inversion result.

---

Precisely what I saw.

A plan view — the horizontal outline of the object, seen from above.

The boundary is dashed, inversion being uncertain. Error ranges are marked beside the dashes.

**The outline is not round.**

It has sides.

I counted: **seven.**

Seven sides, equal in length within error. Seven angles, equal within error.

**A heptagon.**

---

I sat looking at it for a long time.

Then I said: *What are the errors.*

He said: *Relative error on side length, eleven per cent. On angle, plus or minus four degrees.*

I said: *Then is the seven reliable?*

He said:

*I tested it.*

He turned the page.

On the next page he had fitted, to the same data, a triangle, a square, a pentagon, a hexagon, a heptagon, an octagon, and a circle.

Each with a goodness of fit.

**The heptagon is best, and by a long way.**

Second best is the hexagon, worse by more than an order of magnitude.

---

I said: *The data supports seven.*

He said: *Yes.*

And then:

*And that is why I did not write it.*

---

I said: *Why not?*

He said:

*Do you know what sevenfold symmetry means in nature?*

I said: *I do.*

---

Here is the thing, being the whole of this section, and a real, deep physical fact proved over a century ago.

**The crystallographic restriction theorem.**

A periodic structure — one built by translating a unit repeatedly through space — can have only five rotational symmetries:

**One-, two-, three-, four-, and sixfold.**

**No fivefold. No sevenfold. Nothing above sixfold.**

This is not an empirical rule. It is a geometric theorem, with an elementary proof: you cannot tile a plane with regular pentagons or regular heptagons; gaps are unavoidable.

**So no crystal in nature is sevenfold.**

Snowflakes are sixfold. Quartz is three- or sixfold. Salt is fourfold. Diamond is threefold.

**Sevenfold, in a periodic structure, is forbidden by geometry.**

---

I said: *Then it is not a crystal.*

He said: *No.*

He said:

*And the problem is: why would a thing that is not a crystal have an exact sevenfold symmetry.*

---

We listed the possibilities. That evening, in a small room at the station, we put six on a sheet of paper.

**One: an inversion artefact.** The symmetry of an inversion can come from the symmetry of the sampling — lay out survey lines in seven directions and you may invert sevenfold symmetry.

We checked his lines. Irregular, eleven of them, oriented by terrain.

**Eliminated.**

**Two: it is a quasicrystal.** Quasicrystals are aperiodic ordered structures and can have five-, eight-, ten-, and twelvefold symmetry. Discovered in 1982; a Nobel Prize.

And the permitted orders for quasicrystals are a finite set, **and seven is not in it.**

Sevenfold quasicrystals can be constructed mathematically, have never been found in nature, and have no known formation pathway.

**Not eliminated, and extremely improbable.**

**Three: several things happened to line up as a heptagon.**

We computed the probability. I will not write the figure.

**Eliminated.**

**Four: a naturally formed, non-crystalline, sevenfold thing.**

We could think of no natural process that makes one.

Fluids do not make polygons (except convection cells, and convection cells are hexagonal).
Crystallisation does not make sevenfold (geometrically forbidden).
Biology makes many symmetries, fivefold among them (starfish), and biology does not grow under two thousand one hundred metres of ice.

**Eliminated.**

**Five: it is manufactured.** That is: some human object placed there three hundred thousand years ago.

There were no human objects three hundred thousand years ago. That is, at that date, human technology was knapping stone.

**Eliminated.**

**Six.**

On that sheet we wrote a *six*, and after it nothing.

Neither of us wrote it.

---

I brought that sheet home.

It is on my office wall now, beside the other three, on the patch of bare wall I had left.

**It is the first sheet to fill that patch.**

And the way it fills it is by having a space of its own.

---

To end this section, what happened later that evening.

Having listed the six, we sat for a while.

The wind outside was strong. Those station huts have thin walls and the wind comes through.

Then I said something.

I said:

*Dr Vosk, why seven?*

He said: *Meaning?*

I said:

*If a thing is manufactured, its maker can choose any symmetry.*

*Fourfold is easiest to make. Sixfold uses the least material. A circle is simplest.*

*Sevenfold is the hardest option there is.*

I said:

*If a thing was made as a heptagon, the seven did not come from nowhere.*

*It came from somewhere else.*

---

He looked at the sheet.

Then he said:

*Seven years I have been asking what that point is.*

He said:

*I never asked why seven.*

He said:

*You have been here half a day, and you asked.*

---

I said: *Because you are inside it.*

He said: *Meaning?*

I said:

*You have looked at it for seven years. You know every parameter. You know how hot, how deep, how large, how heavy, what density.*

I said:

*When you know a thing that well, you stop asking why it is that way.*

I said:

*I saw it for the first time today.*

---

He did not answer.

After a while he turned the notebook back to an earlier page.

On that page is one line, written years ago, the ink a different colour from the rest.

The line reads:

**Why is it still warm.**

He said:

*I wrote that on the first day.*

He said:

*Seven years ago.*

He said:

*I never wrote another *why* after that.*

He said:

*Everything after is *how much*.*
