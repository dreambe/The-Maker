# 4.4 · She Flies Out

We exchanged email for seven weeks, and then I said I wanted to meet.

He said: *Where I am is not easy to get to.*

I said: *Where are you.*

He named a place.

I looked it up. A city in the southern hemisphere I had never heard of, in a country whose name I knew and whose capital I could not have named.

From here: three flights, thirty-one hours in total, two connections, one of them overnight.

---

I went.

I want to write that journey, because it is the first time I left the country for this, and leaving the country changed the nature of the thing.

Until then it was something that happened in an office. It consisted of a computer, a pile of literature, a notebook, and three sheets taped to a wall.

It could be stopped at any time. I could switch the computer off, go home, and not open it the next day.

**And once I had bought a thirty-one-hour ticket, it could not be stopped at any time.**

The same act as pressing confirm in a lift (3.3).

**Everything I do makes stopping more expensive.**

And I knew what I was doing.

---

First leg, eleven hours.

On the plane I read his third set of supplementary material. Across our seven weeks of correspondence he had written three, over a hundred and forty pages in all, entirely answers to my questions.

I had asked forty-one questions.

He answered forty-one.

Of those, six answers were: *I do not know. I have not measured this. If you consider it important, I can measure it next time I am in the field.*

**Six *I do not knows*.**

Their weight must be stated.

In a hundred and forty pages, six explicit *I do not knows*, each identifying exactly where the gap is, each with a proposal for filling it.

Most material I have read gives, when asked about something unmeasured, an inference, or *per the literature*, or a fudge.

**Not from dishonesty. Because in this field, saying *I do not know* has a cost.**

The more *I do not knows* a document contains, the less complete it looks, the less it looks like a result, and the harder it is to get past review.

**And he wrote six.**

On the second leg I copied all six into my notebook.

And wrote beside them:

**One day I want to be able to do this.**

---

On the overnight connection I sat in a departure hall for seven hours.

A small airport. After two in the morning only one convenience store was open, and a row of hard seats.

I telephoned my mother.

It was daytime there. She answered.

She said: *Hello?*

I said: *Mum, it's me.*

She said: *Oh.*

And then: *Have you eaten.*

I said: *I have.*

She said: *Where are you.*

I said: *Away for work.*

She said: *Oh.*

And then: *When are you back.*

I said: *Before next Wednesday.*

Saying it, I glanced at the clock in that hall and ran the arithmetic.

**Before next Wednesday. In time.**

She said: *Oh.*

And then: *Have you eaten.*

I said: *I have.*

The call was three minutes and some seconds.

Afterwards I sat on those hard seats looking at a flight information board opposite.

That board refreshed every few minutes. When it refreshed, all the characters flipped at once with a very small sound.

I sat there a long time watching it flip.

---

Third leg, four hours.

Landing a little after three in the afternoon, local time.

The sky was low. Not overcast — the low of a high latitude, where the sun is not high for most of the year.

Immigration was fast. That airport takes a few international flights a day.

I came out pushing my case.

Arrivals is small. Fewer than twenty people meeting anyone.

I looked through them.

---

Now the moment I saw him.

I knew him because he had sent a photograph — seven weeks earlier, when I asked how I would recognise him.

The man in that photograph is in his early forties, short hair, in a shell jacket, standing in a field of white, wearing sunglasses.

And the man standing there was older.

*Older* must be explained, because it is not about years.

The photograph, he told me later, was taken six years ago. Six years does not change a man of forty much.

**And he had aged by more than six.**

His hair is mostly white. He is thin. His posture — this is the most obvious — when he stands, his shoulders draw inward, the posture of a person who has been alone for a long time, the posture of a person who does not need to occupy space.

He was holding a sheet of paper.

With my name on it.

**Handwritten.**

---

What I thought in that second was very small:

**Nobody needs to hold up a sign to meet a person in an arrivals hall with twenty people in it.**

He could see me at a glance — the only Asian woman in that hall, dragging a large case, looking for somebody.

**And he had made that sign.**

My name on it, in fine marker, the characters upright, centred on a sheet of A4, the margins even.

**He had done a completely unnecessary thing carefully.**

---

I went over.

I said: *Dr Vosk.*

He said: *Dr Shen.*

And then we both stopped.

The pause of two people who have exchanged email for seven weeks, a hundred and eighty-odd messages, perhaps a hundred thousand words, meeting for the first time.

We had discussed the boundary conditions of a heat diffusion equation, the systematic errors of three dating methods, the response curve of a detector at low temperature.

**And we did not know how to greet each other.**

He spoke first.

He said:

*Tiring journey?*

I said: *It was all right.*

He said: *Thirty-one hours.*

I said: *Mm.*

He said:

*I worked it out.*

---

*I worked it out.*

I laughed in that second.

Not politely. The laugh of a person who has just confirmed that she is not alone.

**He had worked out how long my journey was.**

A man waiting for someone coming from thirty-one hours away: he looked up the flights, did the arithmetic, and said the number on meeting.

That is not small talk.

**Small talk is *you must be tired.***

**He said a number.**

---

He took my case.

I said there was no need.

He said: *It's heavy.*

I said: *It isn't.*

He said: *You've brought paper.*

I said: *How do you know?*

He said:

*Rolling resistance.*

**From the way I was pulling it he estimated the mass and inferred paper.**

I had. I had brought a printout of the three-thousand-one-hundred-row table, four hundred-odd pages, and Lu Ting's four figures, in case the files would not open.

I said: *Is that... a professional habit?*

He said: *No.*

He said:

*It's the habit of seven years of not talking to anyone.*

---

We walked out.

The wind outside is strong and cold — a cold entirely unlike the one I know. It is dry, it has a direction, and it comes from that direction continuously, without stopping.

He put my case in the boot of a very old car.

Once inside, he did not start it.

He sat in the driver's seat looking ahead.

Then he said:

*Dr Shen.*

I said: *Yes.*

He said:

*I have to say something first, and then you decide whether to come with me.*

---

I said: *Go on.*

He said:

*Where that point is is not on any national programme's regular routes.*

*The nearest station is a hundred and seventy kilometres away. It is shared by three countries, and I have a position there — two summer months a year, for my own project.*

He said:

*And where I go is not within my project.*

He said:

*Which is to say: every time I have gone to that point in seven years, I **should not have been there**.*

---

He paused.

He said:

*I have falsified no records. My project reports are true; I did the work I was supposed to do.*

*And I used my own time, and some fuel that should have gone elsewhere, to go somewhere not in the plan.*

He said:

*Four times.*

He said:

*If it comes out, I lose the position.*

*And that position is my only route to that point.*

---

I said: *And this time?*

He said:

*This time is different.*

*This time I would be taking an outsider.*

He said:

*Going alone in secret is a violation.*

*Taking an outsider is another thing.*

He said:

*I do not know what it is called. And I know it is not the same.*

---

The car was quiet for a while.

The wind hit the windows in gusts.

Then he said:

*So I have to say it first.*

*You can say no now. You can stay two days, I give you all the raw data, you go home, you publish it yourself.*

He said:

*You do not need me. The data is already yours.*

He said:

*You need not even put my name on it.*

---

To end this section, what I said, because I have gone over it many times since and still hold that it was right.

I said:

*Dr Vosk.*

I said:

*Let's go first.*

*When we come back, the two of us go to that station and tell them.*

He turned to look at me.

I said:

*Not now. Now they would refuse.*

*Afterwards. Afterwards we will have something in our hands.*

I said:

*If they want to discipline someone, they can discipline both of us.*

---

He looked at me for about three seconds.

Then he said one word.

He said:

*All right.*

And started the car.
