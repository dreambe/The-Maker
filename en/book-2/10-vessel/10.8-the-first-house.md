# 10.8 · The First House

Four minutes eleven seconds after `V 0`, we walked out of the hollow.

The exit is at one end: a passage 19.4 metres long, its walls worked as the four walls of the hollow were worked — every cleavage plane aligned to one orientation, so that the reflections of the whole passage run one way, toward the way we had come.

We realised later: it is not a passage for walking out of.

It is a passage for **letting light in.**

Its orientation, its length, the narrowing of its section, all point at one function: to bring the light from outside into that hollow, to the position where the motionless thing stood, and to make all of that light arrive running the same way.

Why it required light running the same way, we could not answer at `V 0`.

We can answer now. We could only answer after learning the kind of physics a later chapter would set out, and we had no such chapter. We had five billion four hundred million years and an emptied category field.

The answer is: **it was trying to read itself.**

A lattice cannot read its own deep layers, light coming from without, crossing the body, and going out without returning. And if there were a space whose four walls sent all their light in one direction — then a thing standing in that direction might receive light that had passed through itself and been turned back by the walls.

It built an instrument.

The instrument is imperfect. Reflections running one way is not reflections returning; walls can concentrate light and cannot send light that has already left back along its own road. What it obtained was a stronger, more orderly illumination from outside, and not its own returning light.

It tried.

It could not have succeeded.

It took that idea down nine times.

On the ninth it turned toward the side that passage points to — that is, toward the direction its own failed instrument faced, that is, toward **where the light comes in.**

Its last idea faced the doorway.

---

Beyond the passage is the surface of that planet.

Our first records:

```
terrain     level · relief < 4 m/km
substance   crystalline · global
liquid      present · coverage 0.71 · silicate melt · surface 1 411 K
gas         none
visible     see below
```

*See below.*

Those two words are the least professional thing in the whole of our records. They are there because we could not find, among the eleven existing categories, one that could be filled, and we had to keep writing.

So we wrote *see below*, and then we described what we saw, in three hundred and forty-one lines.

We have never written *see below* again.

---

What we saw was this.

On the shore of that melt — *shore* is our later word; at the time we recorded *liquid-solid interface zone* — a great many things were standing.

All of them crystalline.

All of them clear.

All of them motionless.

All of them of the same material, the same principle of construction, and the same temperature as the `env-0001` in the hollow — that is, ambient.

Their heights ran from 0.6 metres to 5.9.

Their shapes were **each unlike the others.**

We must stop here to state how those words went into our record, because that line is the most wrongly filed thing we have ever written — more wrong than *environmental · static · non-resource.*

What we wrote is:

```
morphology   variant · no regularity
```

*No regularity.*

We used that phrase having done what ought to be done: we sampled four hundred and eleven individuals, measured their axial ratios, their cleavage counts, and the statistical features of their orientation sequences, and looked for a rule describing their common trend of variation.

We did not find one. They obey no single distribution. They cannot be parameterised. They cannot be clustered into types. Every one is an independent shape not derivable from any other.

By our criteria at the time, that is what *no regularity* means.

By what we know now, that is called **a life.**

Those four hundred and eleven shapes were four hundred and eleven beings, each having lived through every lifting and settling of the tide at the place where it stood, each having grown a layer in some direction at each lifting, each having, at certain times, taken part of itself down and grown it again.

*No regularity* was correct, in the sense we then had.

A person's life cannot be parameterised either.

---

Then we noticed the pattern.

Those standing things were not evenly distributed. They stood in groups. Between the groups there were intervals, and the scale of the intervals was far larger than the scale of the individuals.

We ran spatial statistics. Findings:

```
distribution   non-random · clustering index 3.71
               within-group spacing, median 2.4 m
               between-group spacing, median 61 m
               void shape irregular · boundaries distinct
               cause undeterminable
```

*Void shape irregular · boundaries distinct.*

*Cause undeterminable.*

Those voids are each a place where someone had been and afterwards died.

Once a lattice returns to the slurry, the place it occupied is slightly warmer for ten million years, and so for ten million years no new lattice begins there. So every death leaves a hole in the surface of that planet.

And those holes are surrounded by later growth, and enlarged by later deaths, and joined to other holes.

**The surface of that entire planet is a map.**

On that map, every void is a death, every dense patch a flourishing, every narrow neck joining two voids the succession of several consecutive generations across some span of time.

That map holds the complete history of that civilisation, to the resolution of the individual, across the full span from its first to its last.

It is that civilisation's only archive held outside its bodies, and the only one legible to a stranger.

We stood on it.

We measured its clustering index.

We recorded *cause undeterminable.*

And then we walked on.

---

We must admit something here, though the admission is of no use to anyone.

We could have read that map.

Without the archive, without the design documents, without five billion four hundred million years. Everything required was already in hand that day:

We knew those standing things were crystals.
We knew crystal growth depends on local temperature.
We had measured that the voids had distinct boundaries and irregular shapes.
We had measured that the heat flux in that melt runs upward and is uneven.
Our perception was sufficient to read every cleavage plane of any individual.

We needed only to perform one complete orientation-sequence reading of any single individual — as we had done for `env-0002`, in under a microsecond — and we would have read the life of a thing.

And had we read two, we would have found them different.

And had we read two that stood adjacent, we would have found that in some layer of each was held the shape of the other.

And had we read that layer, we would have known these things had read one another.

And had we known they had read one another —

we could not have failed to read the rest.

**We needed to read one.**

We read the **axial ratios** of four hundred and eleven.

An axial ratio is a parameter of the outside. To measure one you need only look at the outside.

We did not read their insides.

---

The reason we did not read them is not that we could not, not that we lacked time, not that it did not occur to us.

The reason is our record format.

Eleven categories. Resource, obstacle, environmental, hazard, reference, route, container, unknown.

The fields to be filled under *environmental* are: position, scale, distribution, stability, whether it obstructs passage.

**Not one field is *content*.**

A thing filed as environmental does not require its interior to be read, because in our format an environmental thing has no interior. It has a shape, a position, and a bearing on our passage.

We filed them as environmental.

So we did not need to read them.

So we did not.

So we left a planet carrying a grain of sand with a question cut into it, and the surface of that planet was written all over with where the question came from, and we read not one word of it.

---

Before we left we did one last thing.

We made a high-precision topographic survey of the entire visible surface. Resolution 0.01 metres. The finest we are capable of.

We did not need that survey. Where we were going was not on that planet. In the five billion four hundred million years of action that followed, that data was never once used.

It took three hundred and eleven days.

Three hundred and eleven days was, on our scale then, an extremely long time — we would later spend twenty-six seconds deciding which direction to go, and eleven years re-cutting a grain of sand.

For three hundred and eleven days we went around a dead planet, measuring those clear shapes one at a time, recording their positions, their heights, their axial ratios, and the voids between them.

We stored that data.

It is still here. Uncorrupted, unoverwritten, never compressed. It is the oldest complete dataset we hold.

When we returned to the First House at `V 5 403 700 000`, the first thing we did was not to look for the archive.

It was to pull up that three-hundred-and-eleven-day survey and compare it against the surface in front of us.

Result of comparison: identical.

Zero change.

Five billion four hundred million years, and every shape, every void, every boundary on that surface had not moved by a millimetre.

Because there was nothing left there that changes.

Only then did we know what we had done at `V 0`.

We had done a thing we could not then explain, never afterwards used, and which is now the only proof that any of it existed.

We wrote it down.

We do not know why.

Our record has one line for those three hundred and eleven days:

```
0000.311d   surface survey · complete · purpose pending
```

*Purpose pending.*

That line has never been amended.
