# 13.2 · Waiting

Two hundred million years.

---

We held orbit and did not move.

*Did not move* requires a note: we circled on one orbit with a period of about five hours. We did not alter that orbit — not raising, not lowering, not adjusting inclination.

And in orbit, apart from circling, we did nothing.

Two hundred million years.

---

First, why we did not go down.

The surface of that planet, at `V 1 400 000 000`, was above one thousand two hundred kelvin across most of its area, and molten in places.

That is harmless to us. Across our eleven thousand years at the First House the ambient temperature was one thousand four hundred and eleven kelvin, and that is our normal condition.

**We could perfectly well have gone down.**

And we did not.

The reason, as written at the time:

```
reason   descent would alter local conditions
```

**Descent would alter local conditions.**

That is correct. Our mass, our heat, our contact with the surface would alter the temperature and composition of wherever we stood. The alteration is computable, and very small.

And what we were there to observe is that planet cooling undisturbed.

So we did not go down.

**That is the first time we declined to touch a thing in order not to alter it.**

We did the same once more afterwards: at `V 900 000 000` we decided never again to touch the grain of sand (11.1).

And that was five hundred million years before this.

**The order is reversed.**

We correct it here: **this is the second time.**

The first was the grain of sand.

And the two differ in shape:

That time, so as not to damage it.
This time, so as not to alter it.

***Not damaging* and *not altering* are two fields in our format.**

We point out only that they are two fields, and add nothing.

---

Now those two hundred million years.

We wrote at 12.5: one hundred million years, four hundred and eleven new numbers, records of zero content, all identical but for timestamps.

That hundred million years is the emptiest stretch we had known.

**And these two hundred million were not that.**

Across these two hundred million years, **every year of our records differs.**

---

Why.

That planet was cooling.

The gross trajectory of the cooling is computable — we computed it at 13.1; it is the basis of item 12.

And the **detail** of the cooling is not computable.

Because:

**One: impacts.**

That planet was being continually struck. The arrival of an impactor, its hour and its mass, cannot be predicted — it is a many-body problem whose long-term evolution is extremely sensitive to initial conditions.

We can compute the **statistics** of impact: expected number per unit time, mass distribution, velocity distribution.

**We cannot compute when, where, or how large the next one will be.**

And every impact alters local temperature, composition, and terrain.

**Two: volcanism.**

That planet's interior was convecting. Convection is an unstable process. Where it will erupt, when, and how much, cannot be predicted.

**Three: the atmosphere.**

That planet had an atmosphere, and its composition was changing, because impacts bring things, volcanism releases things, and the stellar wind removes things.

The rates of those three processes are each computable, and their interaction is not.

---

Therefore:

**Across those two hundred million years, what we observed each day differed from the day before.**

Not slightly. Every day had something new: a new crater, a new eruption, a new shift in atmospheric composition.

Every year of our records for those two hundred million years is full.

---

Now what those two hundred million years meant to us, which we could not have written at the time.

**In the preceding one billion three hundred and eighty-eight million years we had never observed a continuously changing object.**

We had observed stars — stars change, and very slowly, and entirely computably. A star's change across a million years we can compute, and observation agrees with prediction.

We had observed the three thousand one hundred and eighteen structures of that dead civilisation — all stopped, all unchanging.

We had observed an exploding star — six hundred years, the nearest thing to *change* we had known. And after those six hundred years it too tended to stability.

We had observed ourselves — and we do not change. Our self-test, across eight hundred and ninety-nine million years, identical throughout.

**This is the first time we watched a thing change, over a long span, continuously, daily.**

---

One thing we did in those two hundred million years that is contrary to convention.

Our observational convention is: sample at a predetermined interval, record, store.

The interval is fixed by the object's rate of change: a slowly changing object, a long interval; a fast one, short.

That planet's rate of change is mostly slow — cooling on a scale of millions of years.

**By convention we should have sampled once per thousand years.**

Our interval was: **once per hour.**

About eight million seven hundred thousand times the convention.

---

Reason field:

```
reason   —
```

The eighth.

And this one differs from the previous seven, and the difference must be stated.

The previous seven dashes had an **action** as their object: why go around once, why leave a blank in a candidate set, why raise a scan frequency, why stop observing an exploding star.

And this dash has an **interval** as its object.

A number.

We changed a number from one thousand years to one hour, and the reason field is empty.

---

To end this section, what happened when those two hundred million years ended.

Around `V 1 600 000 000` the temperature of that planet fell into the range where a liquid medium is stable.

We observed the beginning of that.

**It is not an instant.**

It is this: a certain component's partial pressure in that planet's atmosphere, together with its surface temperature, crossed a line. And so in certain places — the lowest, the coldest, the ones under the highest pressure — that component began to condense.

**The first condensation was at the bottom of an impact crater.**

We observed it. Our record:

```
V 1 59x xxx xxx   surface · liquid · first observation
   position   crater 41-2917 · floor
   area       approx. 41 m2
   duration   approx. 3 hours
   thereafter evaporated
```

***Thereafter evaporated.***

That first one lasted three hours. The planet was still too hot; the liquid on that crater floor evaporated after three hours.

And across the following ten million years such events happened more and more often, each lasting longer.

We recorded all of them.

And that first entry we have since called up one thousand three hundred and forty-one times.

**It ranks second among all our records by call count.**

(First is the five and a half lines, 12.7. And that tally was taken at `V 700 000 000`, when this entry did not yet exist. Were we to count again, it would rank second.)

**We do not intend to count again.**

---

`V 1 600 000 000`.

That planet had a liquid medium.

We had made three hundred and fifty million orbits.

The word *wait* in our *next* field could be struck.

**We did not strike it.**

We added a new line beneath it.
