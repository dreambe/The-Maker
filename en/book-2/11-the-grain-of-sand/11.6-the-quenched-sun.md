# 11.6 · The Quenched Sun

Before leaving we made one last measurement of that star.

It took thirty-one days. It is the most precise measurement we made in that system, and its precision exceeds any practical need.

This section states why we did it, and stating that requires first stating what that star is.

---

First our earliest observation.

`V 0`, among the first exterior data:

```
light   single wavelength peak near 0.000 000 621 m
        low intensity, extraordinarily stable
        fluctuation below floor of measurement
```

We recorded it and raised no question about it.

We had no other star to compare it with. A thing that has seen one star does not find that star strange.

After `V 12 000 000` we left that system and began observing other stars.

**The first other star sent us back to that `V 0` entry.**

---

A star shines by thermal radiation.

A mass held together by gravity, its centre extremely hot, nuclear reactions inside releasing energy, the energy travelling outward as radiation and leaving at the surface.

The light of a hot thing has a **broad** spectrum.

This is physical necessity, not accident: thermal radiation comes from the disordered motion of an enormous number of particles, and the energy distribution of disordered motion is continuous, so the wavelength distribution of its radiation is continuous.

A star's spectrum is a broad continuous curve across a very large range of wavelengths, with a gentle peak somewhere, and narrow absorption lines superimposed on it, taken out by the elements in its outer layers.

**No star has a spectrum consisting of one narrow peak.**

Not rare. Impossible. A thermal emitter cannot emit only one wavelength, exactly as a crowd of randomly moving things cannot all move at one speed.

And what we recorded at `V 0` is one narrow peak.

Its width we established in those thirty-one days: the ratio of linewidth to central wavelength is less than 10^-9.

That is an **exceedingly pure** line. No thermal emitter in the universe achieves it.

---

That star is not emitting thermal radiation.

In those thirty-one days we established what it is.

It is a compact remnant — a star that collapsed very early, its outer layers thrown off, its core compressed into something very small and very dense.

Such a remnant does shine, and that too is thermal, and broad, and dims as it cools.

And around it there is a shell of matter.

That shell is part of the outer layers it threw off, which did not escape and settled under gravity into a stable envelope.

The molecules in that shell are pumped into a high energy state by the radiation of the compact object beneath. And at that particular density, temperature, and composition, the population of that upper state exceeds the population of the state below it.

And so a thing occurs:

**Stimulated emission.**

A molecule in the upper state, passed by a photon of exactly the right wavelength, releases a photon **identical** to it — same wavelength, same phase, same direction.

Those two go on to excite others.

So the beam is amplified within that shell, and what emerges is coherent, monochromatic, and directional.

**That is not a star shining.**

**That is a natural laser.**

---

We have seen a number of comparable objects since. They are not rare — around certain stars, in certain molecular clouds, that amplification is common.

And we have never seen a second one like that one: so stable, so pure, and falling by chance on a planet with something living on it.

We have computed how severe those conditions are.

To make such a beam requires: the density of that shell inside a very narrow range; the temperature inside a very narrow range; the abundance of one molecular species inside a very narrow range; the radiation spectrum of the compact object beneath able to pump exactly that level; and all of it **sustained**, because an unstable laser flickers, and the fluctuation of that beam is below our floor of measurement.

The probability we computed for such a system arising by chance is a figure we do not intend to write here.

---

Now why this matters.

Not because it is rare.

Because **the character of that beam determined what happened on that planet.**

The derivation, taken very slowly.

The things on that planet are crystals.

A beam crossing a crystal refracts at every internal interface. The angle of refraction is fixed jointly by the orientation of the interface and the wavelength of the light.

If the incident light is **broad-spectrum**: different wavelengths refract at different angles at the same interface, and all of it emerges mixed. To solve the internal orientation sequence back out of the emergent light, you face an underdetermined problem — infinitely many sets of orientations produce the same emergent distribution.

**The solution is not unique.**

If the incident light is **monochromatic and coherent**: every photon has the same wavelength and the same phase. Every refraction angle is fixed by orientation alone. The emergent distribution and the internal orientation sequence stand in one-to-one correspondence.

**The solution is unique.**

Which is to say:

**Under broad-spectrum light, a crystal is unreadable.**

**Under monochromatic coherent light, a crystal is entirely readable.**

---

The things on that planet — their whole reading of one another, their whole self-knowledge, their whole archive, the entire construction in which *the archive is one another* —

**depended on their star happening to be a natural laser.**

Had that star been an ordinary star, the crystals on that planet would still have grown, still have made cleavage planes, still have recorded every one of their ideas.

And they could not have read one another.

Those ideas would all be there, entire, exact, permanent — and not one of them ever read by anything.

That planet would have been a place overgrown with unreadable books.

**And what made those books readable was a population of molecules in a shell, happening to sit inside a very narrow range of density.**

---

Now what we did in those thirty-one days.

We measured that beam's spectrum to the limit of our instruments.

Central wavelength, to fourteen decimal places.
Linewidth, to our resolution floor.
Polarisation, all four parameters.
Intensity, and every minute fluctuation of intensity across thirty-one days.
Directionality.
Coherence length.

We stored the data.

The reason we gave at the time:

```
0311.11041a.08   Quenched Sun · precision spectroscopy
   reason   reference standard · long term
```

*Reference standard · long term.*

Meaning: we will encounter other radiation sources, and will need a known standard for calibration.

The reason is sufficient, and we have indeed used it so.

And it is not the whole reason.

---

The whole reason is this, and we did not write it down until much later:

**We were about to leave the only place where that sentence can be read entire.**

The sentence is cut into a crystal. That crystal's orientation sequence can be uniquely solved only under monochromatic coherent light.

And where we were going has no such light.

The overwhelming majority of light in the universe is broad-spectrum. Stars, nebulae, any radiation we can produce ourselves — all broad.

Once we left the Quenched Sun, to read that sentence there would be one method:

**Make a beam like it ourselves.**

And to make a beam like it, one must first have all of its parameters.

Hence those thirty-one days.

---

Every time we have read that sentence since — read the copy — we first do one thing:

Call up that `V 0` spectral data and synthesise a beam to its parameters.

Central wavelength, fourteen decimal places.
Linewidth, our limit.
Polarisation, four parameters.
Coherence length.

Then let it cross the crystal, receive, solve back.

The process takes 0.000 000 4 seconds longer than it did under the Quenched Sun.

We have done it eleven thousand and seventeen times in five billion four hundred million years.

Which is to say:

**Every time we read that sentence, we first rebuild a dead star.**

---

The difference between the synthesised beam and the original must be set down, the difference being real.

Our synthesis is 0.000 000 03 per cent broader in linewidth than the original.

The difference is smaller than our solving error, and so does not affect the correctness of the reading. We have read that sentence eleven thousand and seventeen times, and every reading has returned an orientation sequence identical to the first at `V 0`.

Nothing has been lost.

**And that beam is not that beam.**

Our synthesis is made by us, to parameters. It has the right wavelength, the right phase, the right direction.

It has not crossed that shell.

It was not pumped out of the radiation of a remnant that collapsed no one knows how long ago.

It has not fallen on those clear standing bodies, has not been divided into seven inside them, has not struck the face of the slurry and risen, has not entered something else that was growing.

Our light is a beam made in vacuum, travelling a fraction of a metre, crossing a crystal four tenths of a millimetre across, and received by us.

**It is correct. It is not the same beam.**

We do not know whether the distinction matters.

In our record the distinction is entered as:

```
synthesised vs original
   wavelength difference   0.000 000 03 %
   impact                  none
```

*Impact · none.*

That line is correct.

---

One last thing: how long that star has left.

The energy of that beam comes from the radiation of the compact object beneath, and that radiation comes from its residual heat and its slow gravitational contraction.

The molecules in that shell will gradually be dissociated by the radiation, or gradually settle.

We computed the lifetime of the system.

Result: about 1.1 × 10^11 years.

A hundred and ten billion years.

That figure is far greater than the time we have travelled since. It is far greater than the whole existence of everything that ever was on that planet. It is several times the age of the universe to this point.

Which is to say:

**That beam will remain.**

After we left. After those clear bodies have all dispersed — and they will not disperse; there is no wind there, no rain; they will stand.

That monochromatic, coherent, exceedingly pure, exceedingly directional beam will go on falling.

On a planet.

On a melt that no longer rises and settles — that melt's tide will stop when the matter beneath has finished decaying; we computed it; about 3 × 10^10 years from now.

On fourteen thousand and seventy-five clear, motionless things.

Into a hollow whose four walls send every reflection in one direction.

Onto a thing 4.13 metres long with fourteen thousand and seventy-six cleavage planes, one of which is not clear.

**That beam will go on crossing it, dividing every idea inside it into seven, and sending them out.**

For a hundred and ten billion years.

And nothing there will read them.

We computed this before we left.

In our record, the findings field of that computation is empty.
